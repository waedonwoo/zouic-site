An LDO row asks for a load voltage above the input: a linear regulator cannot step up, so that row either moves to another rail or becomes a boost converter with a transformer efficiency. It is shown at 100% here, so the conclusion is not usable.
At least one LDO dissipates more than 0.5 W: junction temperature is already climbing in a SOT-223 / TO-252 at that level. Check it against the package thermal resistance or switch to DC-DC. This is a nudge, not a thermal simulation result.
Battery capacity is 0: treated as mains powered, so energy and runtime stay empty.
Input voltage is 0 or negative: input current cannot be referred and this value is also the LDO efficiency denominator. Put in the real rail voltage (USB = 5 V).

Where the number comes from

P_in = Σ (V_i · I_i / η_i) I_in = P_in / V_in I_design = I_in × (1 + margin) t = (C · V_batt · DoD) / P_in
  • η: direct = 100%; LDO = Vout/Vin (linear regulation, the rest becomes heat); DC-DC = the efficiency you enter
  • V_in: input rail voltage, i.e. the USB or adapter voltage
  • margin: headroom on the supply rating, covering inrush and peaks; it does not take part in the runtime maths
  • C · V_batt · DoD: battery capacity × nominal voltage × usable share, in Wh

The fit reproduces the published IPC-2221 current capacity chart; it is not the standard text. All constants are listed below, please check them.

Constants: k=0.048 (outer layer) / 0.024 (internal layer) · b=0.44 · c=0.725 · 1 oz=1.378 mil=35 µm · 1 mil=0.0254 mm=25.4 µm · rho(Cu, 20 °C)=0.0172 ohm·mm²/m

What this result cannot be used to decide

This is a steady-state average power budget, not a power supply certificate: it excludes startup inrush and capacitor charging shocks, battery internal resistance and cold-temperature derating, DC-DC light-load efficiency curves and switching losses, and sleep current with wake frequency. Battery products must be re-checked against the measured discharge curve at your target temperature.

Frequently asked questions

Why is an LDO counted as an efficiency loss?

An LDO regulates linearly: input power is V_in × I, and the difference (V_in − V_out) × I turns into heat. So 5 V in, 3.3 V out is only 66% efficient. The page writes that as η = Vout/Vin and also reports the dissipation, so you can judge whether that regulator can sit on a small footprint.

Why not divide battery mAh by the load current?

Because battery voltage and system voltage are not the same number. 3.7 V × 2000 mAh = 7.4 Wh is the energy in the cell, and the system consumes energy at the input side, so runtime = usable Wh ÷ P_in. Dividing mAh by a 5 V-side current overstates runtime by roughly V_in/V_batt (about 1.35× here).

Where does the default 25% margin come from?

It is a starting point we chose, not a standard. It covers WiFi transmit peaks, motor and servo startup, capacitor charging inrush, ageing and high-temperature derating. Change it freely; it only moves the recommended supply current, never the runtime — margin buys instantaneous capability, not energy.

What depth of discharge should I enter?

80% is the usual conservative figure for lithium cells, leaving room for the end-of-discharge voltage sag. LiFePO4 and packs with a protection board should use the vendor usable capacity instead. This number scales runtime linearly, so do not treat the nominal capacity as usable.

So which power supply should I buy?

Read the "recommended supply current with margin" row and round up to a common rating (1 A / 1.5 A / 2 A / 3 A). If the source is a USB port, remember USB 2.0 ports are capped at 500 mA — exceeding it means one cable cannot feed the board.

Do my inputs get uploaded?

No. This page calculates in the browser only: no backend call, no database write, no analytics. Everything disappears when you close it. That is also why the constants are printed on the page — so you can check what it actually computes.
