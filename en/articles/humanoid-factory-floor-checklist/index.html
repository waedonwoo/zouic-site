Humanoids Enter the Factory: An Acceptance Checklist

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

Bottom line: shipments and deployment are two different things. The 2026 Embodied Intelligence Industry Innovation Report, compiled by the China Economic Information Service and the China Academy of Information and Communications Technology Shanghai centre, says global humanoid shipments exceeded 22,000 in the first half of 2026, up nearly 300% year on year, while only about 8% entered smart manufacturing and warehouse logistics; another report says over 60% remain in commercial demonstrations, research and teaching. Those figures change the buyer question from how many were released to how many are continuously delivering results. The checklist below follows that framing.

This is deployment acceptance, not prototype acceptance

This checklist applies to buying a robot for a real workstation that must run continuously, not to a demo stage or a research lab. The measure is whether it continuously delivers results. Among the few head cases reported, a Galbot heavy-load robot entered a CATL line for dual-arm tote handling and ran continuously after acceptance; a Kuafu robot from Leju accumulated running hours at Zhaofeng and produced parts; overseas, Agility Digit is reported running at multiple customer sites for tens of thousands of hours. A claim worth rejecting is that high shipments mean the industry has taken off. A tripling of shipments does not equal a working industry; the measuring stick is moving from units off the line to units working on customer lines.

Reliability before capability

The first priority of a production line is always stability. One industry view holds that over 80% of embodied projects remain at verification or demonstration stage; whole-machine reliability follows the weak-link principle, the strongest part does not set the ceiling and any single failure can stop the machine. Dexterous-hand tendon wear and slow sensor drift are invisible in a short demo but become unstable grasps and frequent human intervention on a continuous line. A claim worth rejecting is making the function complete first and stable later. On a line the order reverses: prove you can repeat one action stably first, then extend functions, or more functions simply add more failure points.

Three sign-offs and who is responsible

Acceptance needs three sign-offs. The supplier provides raw records of task success rate, uptime, cycle time and maintenance interval, not a summary brochure; the integrator connects the robot to the existing line and safety fencing and hands over integration records; the buyer retests with real workpieces and cadence and confirms the exception-handling flow. Reported head cases offer comparable metric framings, with a single loading cycle stable at tens of seconds and success around 98%, and another case at about a thousand running hours. A claim worth rejecting is letting the supplier issue a single already-in-production proof. Success on someone else line is not success on yours; three sign-offs with raw records are what allow traceability in a dispute.

The continuous run must be long enough

A demo lasts minutes; acceptance must last long enough. Reported head cases include a heavy-load robot running 7 by 24 continuously after acceptance, a robot accumulating about a thousand hours and producing over 60,000 parts, and Digit over 65,000 hours overseas. These durations are not publicity numbers but a form of reliability evidence, because slow degradation only shows up after enough time. A claim worth rejecting is accepting after a day or two of trial operation. A short trial cannot reveal tendon wear or sensor drift; the acceptance window must be long enough to expose slow faults and the number of interventions in that window must be recorded. The baseline date of 20 November 2026 is our verification date, not any case date.

Where the evidence lives

Four records must be on paper: a success-rate table by task and shift rather than an overall average, an uptime table with every stop and recovery, a manual-intervention ledger with cause and handling, and a cycle/output table of conforming parts per unit time. These correspond to what the report really values, whether results are continuously delivered. A claim worth rejecting is looking only at a vendor summary success rate. An overall average hides shift differences and failure modes; what matters is the success rate and intervention count on the workstation and shift you are deploying, not a pretty mean from another scenario.

Move from demo scenario to narrow scenario

Industry scenario selection is becoming pragmatic, favouring short, low-dexterity processes such as handling, sorting, loading/unloading and inspection. Verification follows that logic: first pick the shortest process chain for a pilot rather than pursuing universality; second run at real cadence with real workpieces for long enough and keep the four records; third do the arithmetic, folding robot cost, maintenance and human intervention into per-part cost against manual work; fourth expand to a second station only if the numbers work. A claim worth rejecting is demanding a universal humanoid replace a whole line at once. Industry experience favours narrow-scene scaling, proving one process before copying it, which is closer to real deployment cadence and exposes problems earlier.

Only worth it if the numbers work

Three boundaries. First, a working scenario is not a working business: the report notes an overseas Digit case with long running hours and high accuracy still carrying a large net loss and negative gross margin, so narrow-scene scaling is not commercial closure. Second, report and case figures come via media and are third-party material not independently verified, used here as framing only. Third, over 80% at verification stage is an industry view, not a statistical verdict. A claim worth rejecting is that if it works continuously, the purchase is justified. Continuous operation is necessary; sustainable deployment also requires per-part cost and maintenance to work out.

Change shipments to on-the-job before deciding

Twenty-two thousand shipped and about 8% in factories, read together, say this industry lacks patience more than attention. What buyers should watch is how that 8% got running: narrow scenarios, long enough validation, four raw records and a per-part cost that works. A claim worth rejecting is that shipments lead industry maturity. A better leading indicator is the on-the-job rate and repeat-order rate, how many units keep working on customer lines and how many customers buy again. It is like hiring: the number of CVs is not the quality of hires; whether someone stays and delivers on the job is the real test.

Frequently asked questions

With 22,000 shipped, why say the industry has not taken off?

Because shipments and deployment are different. Only about 8% entered manufacturing and warehousing in the same period, and over 60% remain in demonstrations, research and teaching. The measuring stick is shifting from units off the line to units continuously delivering results on customer lines.

What records matter most at acceptance?

Four raw records: success-rate tables by task and shift, uptime tables with every stop and recovery, a manual-intervention log with cause and handling, and a cycle/output table of conforming parts per unit time. These reflect continuous delivery better than a single headline success rate.

If it works continuously, is the purchase a success?

Not yet. An overseas case ran long with high accuracy yet still posted a net loss and negative gross margin. Continuous operation is necessary but not sufficient; you must fold in robot cost, maintenance and human intervention and make the per-part cost work.
