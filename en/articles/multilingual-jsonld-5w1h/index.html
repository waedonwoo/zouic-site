Should structured data change currency with the language

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

The thing most easily filed under “configuration difference” on a multilingual site is that the same service uses different currencies on different language pages. As soon as structured data writes a price that follows the page, three questions have to be answered explicitly: who set this number, is it visible on the page, and which currency does it carry. Below, the 5W1H lens takes apart “whether to switch currency with the language”. The conclusion first: currency may change with the pricing convention, but you must write exactly one convention at a time; if the page shows no price, mark up no price; and a list price and the site’s own quote must never be mixed into one Offer, nor folded into a single convenient-looking number.

What: three kinds of page, three kinds of price fact

A service overview page describes the platform’s publicly published list price — a fact of the kind “someone else sets the price, the site provisions it on their behalf”. A quote page describes the pricing convention for projects the site takes on itself — a fact of the kind “the site sets the price”. Project-based products (for example conversation-content archiving or private deployment) have no public price and accept enquiries only — a fact of the kind “there is no price to write”. What schema.org supplies is the vocabulary for describing these facts: Service describes a service, provider points to the organisation offering it, ContactPoint describes a contact channel, hasOfferCatalog and OfferCatalog describe a price list that genuinely exists, and Offer with PriceSpecification and UnitPriceSpecification describe concrete prices. The vocabulary does not judge which number is true; it only requires that what you write can be seen on the page. Collapsing these three kinds of fact into one is the most common source of error in multilingual structured data: the page states only a list price, yet the markup shows a converted local-currency number.

Why: markup that disagrees with visible content is a framing accident, not a syntax accident

The value of structured data is that it turns page facts into extractable fields, and a machine reading price assumes that number also exists on the page. Third-party technical roundups consistently stress: write price or priceSpecification only when the page actually displays a price; quote-based or negotiable services should have no offers, or use a clearly stated starting price taken from the page; inventing a number that is not shown is markup disagreeing with visible content, which is riskier than a missing field. schema.org is equally specific about priceCurrency: use a three-letter ISO 4217 code and do not mix a currency symbol into the value; use a full stop as the decimal separator and do not use a comma as a thousands separator. That makes the boundary of “switching currency” clear: if the Chinese page states a CNY list price and the English and German pages describe overseas project quotes in USD, each side may have its own priceCurrency, but each must agree with the visible number, unit and wording on its own page. Copying a markup block across pages and then changing the currency symbol almost guarantees an inconsistency.

Who: the pricing party, the reselling party and the markup maintainer are not the same people

The official list price is set by the platform; the number on the page, the verification date and the “order page is authoritative” note are a restatement, and the site may neither promise a discount nor add a markup of its own. Project quotes are set by the site, which may choose currency and unit for the target market. The markup is generated by templates or the server and usually maintained by engineering. These three parties have different duties yet share one set of fields, so one rule matters most: whoever sets the price explains it. A list-price page should say plainly that this is the official published list price, give the verification date, state that it is not the site’s own quote and that the order page governs, and the markup should avoid any wording that packages the list price as the site’s selling price. The contact email, available languages and service types listed in ContactPoint are facts the site can supply itself and may be written with confidence. Describing “platform-set price” and “site quote” separately is fair to the reader and also keeps a machine from blending two revenue conventions into one figure.

When: three moments that must be updated together

When the official list price changes, the page number, the verification date and the markup must all change together; when the site’s quote convention changes (for example overseas projects move to another currency), the visible copy of the corresponding language page and its priceCurrency must change together; when the vocabulary or a platform validation rule changes, the field syntax needs review. If the three fall out of step, a hard-to-spot residue remains: the page has switched currency but the markup still carries the old code, or the official list price changed long ago while the page still shows a verification date from months back. Multilingual work adds one more step — updates between language versions are often rolled out in batches, so if the Chinese page changes and the English one does not, the same @id entity ends up carrying different numbers in different language versions. The sound approach is to write price, currency, verification date and language together into one structured manifest, have templates read from it by language, and make “each language page’s markup matches that page’s visible copy” an assertion after export rather than relying on a manual comparison at release time.

Where: what belongs at site level and what at page level

Organisation entities (Organization, @id, contact channels) belong at site level and are usually declared once for the whole site, with every language version pointing to the same @id, so that each language does not invent its own “company”. Services and quotes belong at page level and follow the facts of that language page: service name, description and serviceType use text in that language, priceCurrency uses the ISO code matching that page’s convention, and unit-based quotes use UnitPriceSpecification with unitText stated clearly. Language purity shows up here too — the JSON-LD on English and German pages should contain no leftover Chinese service names or Chinese descriptions, or a machine will still read Chinese fields even after the body text is fully translated. One more boundary is easy to cross: hasOfferCatalog should be used only when the page really lists a price catalogue; wrapping a paragraph of introduction from the service overview into an OfferCatalog fabricates a price list that does not exist.

How: turn trilingual markup into one convention table

Step one, classify by page: the service overview describes only the service and provider, and if it shows the official list price, state the pricing party and verification date in the visible copy; the quote page describes the site’s own OfferCatalog, listing price, currency and unit item by item; project-based products are marked as quote-on-request and carry no price. Step two, for each language version list the five rows “visible number, currency code, unit, verification date, pricing party”; templates read only from this table so page and markup share one source. Step three, run five assertions: every price in the markup can be found in that page’s visible copy; priceCurrency is an ISO 4217 code and the value carries no currency symbol; quote-on-request entries have no price field at all; the English and German markup contains no Chinese characters; and @id and canonical point to the same entity and agree across language versions. Step four, run each of the two validator kinds once — the search-platform side checks eligibility for the display features it supports, a general validator checks vocabulary and syntax; they answer different questions and may return different conclusions, and both runs should be logged.

Boundaries: structured data earns no display and proves no price true

The Service type has no corresponding feature in the search platform’s list of rich-result displays; the markup’s job is to state “who offers what, how to contact them, which quotes exist”, not to buy a special display slot — third-party roundups make this point clearly and it is cited here only as directional context. Nor does markup make a price more credible: whether a number holds depends on the pricing party and the verification date, and a correctly written field with a stale number is still wrong. On “how to mark up quote-based services”, public material disagrees: most advise writing no offers at all, while some suggest a price of zero with an explanatory field as a placeholder; the latter conflicts with the principle “do not invent a number the page does not show”, so this article does not adopt it and does not present either side as a specification requirement. One more framing conflict must be kept side by side: the list-price page retaining the official CNY quote without converting it to USD, and overseas projects quoted in USD, are two parallel conventions — they should not be averaged, not merged by conversion, and not written into the same Offer.

Conclusion: currency may change, conventions must not be mixed

Let each language version describe the price fact that genuinely exists on its own page: a list price states that it is set officially, with its verification date and a note that the order page governs; a site quote states its currency, unit and scope; enquiry-only products simply carry no price. Drive templates from one manifest of “visible number / currency / unit / date / pricing party” so page and markup share a source, and wire the five assertions into the export process. Then even if the search platform never displays these fields, the site preserves something more basic: the price a machine reads and the price a person sees on that page are the same number, in the same currency, from the same source.

Sources and framing (as of 12 November 2026): the purposes of Service, provider, ContactPoint, hasOfferCatalog, OfferCatalog, Offer, PriceSpecification and UnitPriceSpecification, together with priceCurrency using ISO 4217 codes, a full stop as decimal separator and no currency symbol mixed into the value, come from the schema.org vocabulary documentation (official specification). Marking a price only when the page displays one, writing no offers for quote-based services, the Service type having no corresponding rich-result display, and the two validator kinds answering different questions, come from public third-party technical roundups and are tool-vendor and practical framing, not written as platform specification; among them the suggestion that quote-based services may use a zero-price placeholder conflicts with this article’s choice, so it is listed side by side and not adopted. The WeCom official store list price and the service-page note “not the site’s own quote, the order page is authoritative” are a distinction between official platform framing and the site’s restatement framing, with the verification date taken as stated on the page. After this baseline the official list price and platform rules may both change — check the current pages before implementing.

Frequently asked questions

If the English page uses USD and the Chinese page CNY, is the structured data in conflict?

No, provided each side’s priceCurrency matches the visible number on its own page and the two conventions state the pricing party and their scope separately. What conflicts is converting the list price into USD or forcing both conventions into a single Offer; do not average them and do not merge them by conversion.

A project-based product has no public price — what should the price field contain?

Nothing. If the page displays no price, do not mark up price, and do not invent a zero or a placeholder just to make the field complete. Saying in the visible copy that pricing is per project and listing what information is needed to estimate it is more accurate than a field that only looks complete.

The official list-price page shows a verification date — should the markup carry it too?

The verification date on the page is framing for the reader and must stay; the markup should not dress it up as the site’s own quote or as a validity commitment. The list price is set by the platform, the site only restates it, and the order page is authoritative.
