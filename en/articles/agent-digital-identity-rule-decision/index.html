11 Agencies Issue One Badge Format for AI Agents

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

A badge only opens doors whose owner accepts the issuing office's stamp. Ten buildings once meant ten badges. On 27 November, eleven departments including the State Cyberspace Administration published rules on interoperable distributed digital identity, effective 1 March 2027, explicitly encouraging use in AI agent identity marking. The aim is to turn ten different badges into one format.

What four pieces make up a badge

The rules define four basic parts; missing any one leaves a badge nobody can verify. A distributed digital identity identifier is a string following a specific coding rule, unique, permanent and resolvable, like a never-changing number printed on a badge. An identity document is structured data tied to that number, carrying the information needed to verify identity and interact. Keys come in two halves: the public key binds to the identifier so others can verify a signature, while the private key stays with the user to prove the badge is yours. A verifiable credential turns an authoritative proof such as an ID card, diploma or licence into a standardised, programmable digital claim whose authenticity can be checked technically. Together they make a badge that can pass a gate, not just a printed photo.

What do the parties in the rules do?

The identity-chain operator runs the underlying chain, the authoritative identity authority confirms who you are, service providers connect the system to concrete scenarios, credential issuers issue, credential relying parties verify, and users hold their own private keys. Five roles, each with one job; nobody issues and checks at once. That division seems tedious but is the precondition for interoperability: if everyone could stamp their own badge, we would be back to every office telling its own story. The rules also require all parties to observe network security, data security and personal information protection laws. Users are responsible for the truth of the information they provide and must safeguard their keys. Losing a key is not the lock's fault.

Why selective disclosure matters

A easily missed term in the rules is the verifiable claim, which lets the holder disclose part or all of the information. This turns a badge from a fully exposed document into one that can show only the needed field. Entering a campus, you prove you are on the project team without handing over your home address; buying wine, you prove you are over eighteen without revealing your birthday. For agents this is especially important. An agent booking a ticket needs to prove you have the ability and the authorisation to pay, not dump your entire file on the ticket platform. Giving away less is what makes this format safer than emailing a scan of your ID to everyone.

What problem does unified coding solve?

The hard part of interoperability is not having no badge, but one badge reading differently in two systems. The rules set out a unified coding specification and an identifier directory, supporting mutual recognition across identities and platforms and lowering the cost of cross-platform, cross-industry checks. The technical weight lies in the dullest layer: how numbers are formed, where the directory is queried and whom resolution points to. As of 2026-11-28, what can be checked is the principles and obligations the rules establish; detailed coding rules and the directory itself await supporting implementation. Fixing the numbering convention does more than a dozen pages of application vision, because every upper layer must stand on it.

What does listing agent identity marking mean?

The rules encourage distributed digital identity in finance, taxation, transport, customs, public resource tendering and AI agent identity marking. The first five are easy to grasp; agent identity marking entering a national rule this way is new. It acknowledges something already happening: more and more actions are not clicked by a person but by an authorised proxy program. Who sent these programs, how far their authority goes and what they may sign were left to each platform. A common format gives these proxies a badge with a photo, verifiable by the same method. The badge is a format, not a letter of authority; that distinction comes later.

What should agent teams change first?

The first instinct is to look for API docs, but the better move is to draw a table: where your agent appears under what identity, and what each platform recognises — an account, a key or something else. Drawing it surfaces duplicated registration, excess privilege and ex-employees still able to call services. Next, narrow the disclosure scope of verifiable claims to each scenario instead of granting everything by default. Third, assess key custody: who holds the private key, what happens if it is lost, can it be rotated. These are addressed in the rules. None of these three steps need wait for 1 March, because they are your own accounts, not someone else's interface.

A badge is not a letter of authority

The easiest misreading is that carrying the identity format means having permission to act. The rules address whether a counterparty can confirm who you are; what that identity may sign or call is a separate credential. A badge proves the holder is an employee, not that they can sign a purchase order of any amount. Conflating the two is the most common start of agent permission incidents. Once the format is widespread, the real dividing line moves here: whose authorisation can be verified just as clearly, and who still runs on a verbal request.

One common badge format

Read to the end, the rules do something plain: unify the shape, numbering, stamping and checking of a badge so entering another building no longer requires a fresh self-introduction. After 1 March 2027, whether agents carrying their own badge actually pass through fewer steps depends on supporting directories and issuers. The direction is written down: identity moves from each platform recognising its own to everyone recognising one badge. Once the face of the badge is standard, the contest moves to who writes the authority field most clearly.

Frequently asked questions

When do the rules take effect and do they cover AI agents?

The rules take effect on 1 March 2027 and were published jointly by eleven departments including the State Cyberspace Administration. They explicitly encourage use in fields such as AI agent identity marking, providing general rules for identity identifiers, credentials and interoperability; concrete rollout depends on supporting directories and implementation details.

How is a verifiable credential different from a username and password?

A password proves you know a secret; a verifiable credential converts an authoritative proof into a standardised, programmable digital claim that supports selective disclosure and can be verified technically. The former relies on each platform storing secrets, while the latter aims at cross-platform interoperability, with the private key still held by the user.

Does carrying an identity format give an agent permission to act?

No. Identity marking establishes who the holder is; the scope of authority is a separate matter. A badge proves the holder works for a company, but it does not replace a letter of authorisation. Conflating the two is a common source of privilege escalation in agent systems.
