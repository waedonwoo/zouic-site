A list price is not a quote: who sets it, who explains it, who stands behind it

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

Copying the public list price from a platform’s official mall onto your own services page is the easiest step in a resale business and the one most likely to blur responsibility. A reader who sees a number will naturally ask who set it, whether it can actually be bought at that price, and who to turn to when something goes wrong. If the page shows only an amount and not the price setter, all three questions are silently answered as “the site’s quote”. What follows unpacks the boundary between a list price and a quote along the 5W1H axes. Conclusion first: a list price may be cited, but only together with the price setter, the verification date and the note that “the order page governs”; projects the site itself delivers need a separate quoting framing, and the two are not converted, not merged, and neither is used to endorse the other.

What: one number, three different identities

The WeCom official mall lists advanced features item by item. Each product card shows only a few feature highlights and then a “View all N items” link to the complete list; entries such as security, mail, docs, meetings and WeDrive each state how many items can be viewed, while things like external-contact scale are described by capacity tiers. These are the platform’s public product and catalogue information. The WeCom service provider integration guide describes something else entirely: when an order is created, the version, the number of purchasers and the purchase term are fixed, and the pricing strategy the service provider configures on the platform produces a version price; on expansion or a version change, the value of the currently active version is prorated and deducted as a discount amount, yielding the set price; the service provider may waive a further discount amount on that set price and then multiply by a payable coefficient to arrive at the payable price, which the user pays. The payable coefficient depends on who places the order: 1.0 when the enterprise orders itself, the platform revenue-share ratio when an application service provider places it, and 1.0 minus the agreed agent revenue-share ratio when an agent service provider places it. The order price retrieved from the interface is precisely this payable price. The same “price” is catalogue information on the mall page, a computed result in the order chain, and merely a relay on the site’s services page — three identities that cannot be swapped.

Why: the more concrete the number, the more easily it is read as a promise

The value of a list price is that it can be checked; the risk is that it gets read as “the site guarantees a transaction at this price”. Once a reader arrives with that expectation, the site is forced to explain a string of things that should have been written down in advance: is this amount the platform’s public list price or the site’s own quote; does it include discounts and prorations; how many people and how long is it computed over; what changes on expansion or renewal; and does the order page or an emailed confirmation govern. The calculation steps in the official integration guide show exactly the opposite of a single figure: the final payable price sits several layers away from the list price — version, term, proration, discounts and the payable coefficient. Strip out those layers and leave only a number with a currency sign, and you have used the most prominent form to convey the least complete framing. There is a risk in the opposite direction too: third-party channel articles often give a different breakdown and different numbers that do not match how the official mall presents things. Such content is channel self-description or third-party compilation; it cannot be used to fill gaps in the official catalogue and should not go into external commitments. Two framings can coexist side by side, but they must not be averaged, blended, or treated as one source in the same table.

Who: the price setter, the relaying party, the provisioning party and the ordering party

The platform sets the product and the list price and shows the final transaction price on the order page. The service provider configures its own pricing strategy on the platform under the integration guide, may place orders on a customer’s behalf, handles product selection and provisioning, and earns revenue through the share defined by platform rules. The site’s services page is only a relaying party: it organises public information into a readable form and says what services it can provide. The enterprise administrator is the ordering party and pays the payable price. The four responsibilities do not overlap: the relaying party does not stand behind the list price, the provisioning party does not gain its own pricing authority merely by “helping you activate”, and the ordering party is bound by the result on the platform’s order page. Compressing four parties into “seller / buyer” is the most typical distortion in services-page copy. The difference is concrete in the wording: writing “official mall public list price, verified on a stated date, not a quote from this site, the order page governs” and writing “this service, ¥X per person per year” point to entirely different obligations — the first is a citation, the second is a quote.

When: three points in time that must be labelled clearly

The first is the verification date: a list price is copied from an official page at a particular moment, and a page redesign, a promotion or a product change can make it stale, so the amount needs a date beside it rather than the label “latest price”. The second is the order moment: the integration guide says version, seat count and term are fixed only when the order is created, and expansion, renewal and version changes also prorate the value of the already-active version, so the same product at different stages of its lifecycle is not the same calculation. The third is the external communication moment: a cost estimate in an email is an estimate, while the result on the order page is the basis of the transaction; describing both in one sentence turns an estimate into a promise. The three moments also impose a maintenance discipline: when a list price changes, the page number, the verification date and the related notes must all change together. Changing only the number and not the date is more likely to make people assume it is still current than changing nothing at all.

Where: four locations, each with its own framing

The official mall and the order page are where pricing and the transaction live, and where the complete feature list lives too; the few highlights on a product card are not the whole feature set. The service provider integration guide is where order calculation and revenue-share rules live, and it is developer-documentation framing. The site’s services page is where relaying and service description live; it should say which features can be activated, that the site handles selection, provisioning on behalf, deployment and guided rollout, that the price is provided by the official side, that revenue comes from the official share and that the site adds no markup of its own, and it should mark project-based products separately as quote-only. Email and quotations are where the site makes its own quotes, and may state currency (ISO 4217), unit, validity period and calculation basis, but they must be presented in a separate zone from list prices. The classic mistake when the four locations are mixed is writing the final price from the order page into the services page as a list price, writing a reference price from a channel article into a quotation as an official price, or writing a services-page relay into an email as a commitment. Physically separating the presentations is more reliable than a one-line disclaimer.

How: break one price line into six verifiable fields

Field one, the price setter: the official list price is set by the platform, the site’s project quote is set by the site, and each line says which. Field two, amount and unit: per person per year, per account per year, by capacity tier or per project — different units must not sit in the same column for comparison. Field three, the verification date and the source page, noting that this is public information from the official mall. Field four, boundary notes: not a quote from this site, not the final transaction price, the order page governs, no discount promised, no markup added. Field five, feature scope: the product card lists only highlights, the complete list is governed by the official “View all” link, and if a product name is translated literally from the Chinese item it should say the order-page name governs. Field six, a quote entry point: project-based products get no number and instead list the information needed before a quote. With all six, a reader can judge — without even opening the official page — whether the number belongs in their budget, and the site no longer has to re-explain the framing in every email.

Boundaries: what citing a list price cannot prove

A list price does not prove the final transaction price, does not prove the feature list is complete, and does not constitute a site commitment about delivery results or compliance obligations; the order page, the order interface and the contract terms answer those questions respectively. The payable coefficient and the revenue-share rules in the official integration guide are platform-rule framing, with concrete ratios agreed between the platform and the parties; this article makes no numerical inference about them. The price breakdowns and “hidden fee” warnings in third-party channel articles are channel self-description: they can serve as a checklist of questions but must not be cited as official prices. There is also a natural gap: the services page cannot see the prorations, discounts and registration relationships applied at order time, so any claim to “calculate the total for the customer” can only be called an estimate. Writing these into the boundaries is more useful than a footer saying “final interpretation rights reserved” — the former states which layer is visible and which is not, while the latter explains nothing.

Conclusion: let every number carry its source

Cite list prices as they are, with the price setter, unit, verification date and the note that the order page governs; keep a separate framing for the site’s own project quotes, stating currency, scope and calculation basis; and give project-based products no number at all, listing instead the information needed for a quote. Externally keep exactly one account of “who sets the price, who explains it, who stands behind it”, word-for-word identical across the page, emails and structured data. Then even if the official list price changes tomorrow, the site only has to edit those few dated relayed lines, not re-explain its entire business model.

Sources and framing (as of 12 November 2026): the highlight-style product cards, the “View all N items” link and the capacity-tier description of external-contact scale on the official mall come from the WeCom official mall page — an official platform mall page. The order-price calculation chain (version price, proration discount, discount amount, payable coefficient, payable price), the coefficient differences between enterprise self-ordering and the two kinds of service-provider proxy ordering, the fact that the price retrieved from the interface is the payable price, and the revenue share under platform rules after order completion come from the WeCom service provider integration guide — official developer documentation on the platform side. Price breakdowns, certification fees and “hidden fee” warnings in third-party channel articles are channel self-description and third-party compilation: they are cited here only for direction, their concrete numbers are not used, they are not written into the conclusion, and they are not placed in a table alongside the official list price. Where framings conflict, they are set side by side rather than averaged. After the baseline date both the official mall and platform rules may change; the order page and the current documentation govern.

Frequently asked questions

If a services page publishes the official list price, does that make it the site’s own quote?

No. The list price is set by the platform; the site only relays it. The page should state the verification date, make clear that this is not the site’s quote, that it does not represent the final transaction price, and that the order page governs. Without those notes readers readily take the figure as a price the site has committed to.

Why can the price on the order page differ from the list price?

The official integration guide shows that the order price passes through version, seat count, term, proration on expansion or version changes, discretionary discounts and the payable coefficient, and the user finally pays the payable price. The list price is public information, not the output of that calculation chain.

Can third-party channel price sheets fill gaps in the official catalogue?

Not as an official source. Such sheets are channel self-description or third-party compilation and do not match how the official mall presents things; use them as a checklist of questions, but do not put them in a price table on the services page, and do not average or blend them with the official list price.
