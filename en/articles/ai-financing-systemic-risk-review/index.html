USD 4 Trillion in Off-Balance-Sheet Commitments: The Bank of England Opens the AI Ledger

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

The Bank of England turned a full inventory of assets and liabilities to its last page. According to a recent warning, the scale and reach of financing needed for the AI investment boom keep growing and could deepen fragility in the global financial system. The debt and off-balance-sheet commitments of large technology companies are estimated to exceed USD 4 trillion already. A number that large defeats intuition, but put another way it becomes clear: a company buying equipment with its own profits and a company buying equipment with borrowed money while hiding part of the commitment outside the statements are two completely different kinds of risk.

A Few Lines on the Ledger

First separate the numbers by nature. USD 4 trillion is a Bank of England estimate covering the debt and off-balance-sheet commitments of large technology companies, not the liabilities of one company. USD 305 billion is an estimate of bond issuance in 2026, more than double last year, describing financing activity that is happening or about to happen this year. There is a third line with no number but more importance: AI financing is spreading into investment-grade bonds, leveraged loans, private credit and equity markets. The three lines describe three things, how large the stock is, how fast the flow is growing, and where the money comes from. Mix them together and you get an oversimplified conclusion like "tech giants are drowning in debt." Read them apart and you can see that the risk is structural.

Paying from Salary or Paying by Credit Card

A household buys a computer with the salary from this month and waits until next month if it cannot afford it. That is internal cash flow. A household buys a computer on a credit card, then takes out a second card to pay the first card bill. That is leverage. The two look identical at the moment the computer arrives; the difference shows up on the day income expectations are revised down. AI capital expenditure is going through exactly this process. Early investment came from profits; now it comes from issuing bonds, leveraged loans and private credit. According to National Business Daily, the Bank of England points out that complex and opaque financing arrangements may increase interconnectedness within the financial system. Put that into everyday language and it means this: creditors are tied to one another, and when one falls it takes a string down with it.

Why Off-Balance-Sheet Commitments Are Hardest to See

The reason the USD 4 trillion figure deserves to be singled out is that it includes off-balance-sheet commitments. On-balance-sheet liabilities can be found on the balance sheet; off-balance-sheet commitments hide in contracts and notes. When markets are calm, nobody asks; once expectations reverse, those commitments all turn into money that must be paid at once. That is why the central bank keeps mentioning opacity. For companies, off-balance-sheet arrangements have sound business reasons, such as investment commitments in a joint venture or long-term purchase obligations. The problem is not the arrangement itself but that outsiders cannot see it. Risk that cannot be seen gets left out of pricing, and when the price is corrected afterwards the correction is especially violent.

What the USD 305 Billion Says

Issuance more than doubling year on year says financing demand is accelerating, not contracting. There is a cause and effect here that is easy to miss: building computing capacity takes a long cycle and returns arrive slowly, so early investment has to rely on external money; the more urgently a company needs to capture market share, the more it leans toward borrowing first and delivering later. The USD 305 billion buys not revenue already earned but expectations of future revenue. When the expectation holds, the financing chain runs smoothly; once it is revised down, the Bank of England warning becomes concrete and the related financing arrangements may come under pressure. Every link in that chain is rational, yet together they may form a fragile structure. This is the hardest part of systemic risk to guard against.

Why Raise This Now

For the past two years the story of AI financing has been "not enough money." Chips must be grabbed, data centres built, power queued up, and whoever gets money first takes the position. That story still holds today, but its other side is starting to show: the cost and source of money have become more important. Once investment-grade bonds and private credit are both in play, AI is no longer only a technology industry matter; it is tied to the health of the credit market. That boundary is exactly where the central bank is looking. As of 27 November 2026, what can be checked is the estimates and projections from the central bank and the directional judgment about where financing is spreading; there is no public company-by-company breakdown. The direction is clear, the granularity is not, and that is the frame of mind to bring to a warning like this.

What This Means for Buyers of Computing Capacity

If you are signing a long-term contract to buy computing capacity, this news carries a concrete reminder: look at where the money of the counterparty comes from. A supplier expanding on its own cash flow and a supplier growing on debt may deliver equally well in the short term, but their stability five years out is completely different. The question to ask in the contract is not who offered the lowest price, but whether that capital structure can last through the final delivery. Advance payments, performance bonds and staged delivery milestones are not red tape in this environment; they are tools for keeping the counterparty financing risk outside the contract. Opening the ledger for one look is more useful than attending ten product launches.

Is This a Bearish Call on AI?

The Bank of England warning is easy to read as "the AI bubble is about to burst." It did not say that. It said that expanding financing, lengthening chains and more complex arrangements may deepen fragility. This is a risk notice, not a timing prediction. The need to build AI infrastructure is real, and the shortages of computing capacity, power and chips are real too. The problem is the payback cycle. Returns are measured in years, the patience of financing is measured in quarters, and that mismatch will not disappear on its own just because the technology is advanced. For the industry, the steadier path is to bring capital expenditure closer to real revenue, not to fill the gap between them with ever more complex financing structures.

What the Next Page of the Ledger Says

What is worth watching is not how much more debt was issued, but three more concrete things: whether off-balance-sheet commitments are being disclosed more, whether private credit is taking a larger share of AI financing, and how big the gap is between the growth of bond issuance and the growth of revenue. All three can be checked in public data, and all three can give an early signal. USD 4 trillion is a frightening headline, but the value after the headline is that it puts a question on the table: the AI race is no longer only about whose model is stronger or whose data centre is bigger, but also about who is willing to lay the ledger open for others to see. Opening the ledger is not a bad thing; the fear is that it stays shut.

Frequently asked questions

Which companies does the USD 4 trillion refer to?

According to Bank of England estimates, this is the total debt and off-balance-sheet commitments of large technology companies, not the liabilities of any single company, and not the same as debt already due. Off-balance-sheet commitments usually sit in contracts and notes rather than the main balance sheet, which makes them harder for outsiders to see.

Has the USD 305 billion in bonds already been issued?

No. This is a Bank of England estimate of bond issuance by large technology companies in 2026, more than double last year. An estimate reflects the pace and direction of financing demand, not an amount already issued.

Is this warning saying the AI bubble is about to burst?

No. The warning says that expanding financing, a wider range of coverage, and complex and opaque arrangements may increase interconnectedness within the financial system. This is a structural risk notice, not a prediction of when anything bursts. The demand for AI infrastructure is still real.
