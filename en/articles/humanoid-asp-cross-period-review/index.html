Humanoid Robot ASP: How Far Did It Fall in a Year?

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

When humanoid robot prices come up, one set of numbers keeps being cited: an average selling price of about USD 29,600 in the first half of 2026, against about USD 37,000 in the same period a year earlier. Both figures come from an IDC estimate relayed by technology media, a third-party estimate framing, and are not written up as official statistics. Average selling price, the median product and the cost of a robot actually used in a factory are three different quantities. Keeping them separate is what makes this declining curve readable.

The average fell from about 37,000 to about 29,600 US dollars

According to IDC figures relayed by media, humanoid robot revenue in the first half of 2026 exceeded USD 740 million and shipments approached 25,000 units. Dividing one by the other gives an average of about USD 29,600, which matches the average IDC itself reported. In the same period a year earlier, IDC put the average at about USD 37,000. Under the same framing, full-year 2025 revenue was about USD 440 million and shipments about 18,000 units, giving an average of about USD 24,400, a division performed by the media. Each pair of figures belongs to a different reporting period and should be cited together with that period. This is a third-party estimate framing and is not written up as official statistics.

The average decline is read as a product price cut

An average is revenue divided by units and is heavily shaped by the shipment mix. That mix is tilting toward low-priced small machines from Chinese vendors: small robots for research and education carry lower unit prices and ship in larger numbers, and counting them in the denominator pulls the average down. Full-size humanoids accounted for 41.6% of revenue in IDC framing for 2025, so a single full-size unit moves the average far more than a small one, while the full-size machines of Western vendors are mostly in pilot and shipments are just starting. The falling average reflects a change in mix, which is a different matter from whether full-size robots used in factories have become cheaper. Reading the former as the latter conflates two different shipment structures.

Who estimates, who relays, who sets prices

There are three kinds of actors in this chain of numbers. IDC is a research institute that provides estimates of revenue, shipments and the average; after being relayed by technology media these enter public reporting, and IDC itself does not publish official per-unit transaction statistics. The media performed the division for the full-year 2025 average and noted that the division was done by the media. On the vendor side, Unitree, 1X and Tesla publish or partly publish prices, while Figure, Agility Digit and the Atlas of Boston Dynamics do not publish prices and go through pilots and enterprise contracts. Vendors such as AgiBot ship mostly research and education models. When reading an average, first establish whether it comes from an estimate, a relayed report or a vendor page, since the three sources carry different levels of certainty.

Periods cannot simply be subtracted from one another

Time points need checking one by one. The first half of 2026 and the same period a year earlier are two reporting periods, and full-year 2025 is a third; the three have different shipment mixes, so the averages obtained by dividing each period cannot be treated directly as a single product price curve. The USD 5,900 price for the Unitree R1 was the launch price reported by Bloomberg in July 2025, and the current price has not been rechecked. The pricing of the 1X NEO and the long-term target of Tesla Optimus are statements of different kinds, the former being pre-order terms and the latter a target range. The as-of date of 21 November 2026 is the checking point, not the publication date of these figures.

Which layer of material the numbers sit in

The average and shipment figures sit in the market estimate system of IDC and reach the public through technology media, covering a whole-market framing. Public prices sit on vendor pages and in media reports: the Unitree G1 starts at USD 13,500 excluding tax and shipping, the Unitree R1 launched at USD 5,900, the 1X NEO is listed at USD 20,000 or USD 499 per month with a refundable USD 200 deposit, and the Tesla Optimus has a long-term target of USD 20,000 to 30,000. Figure 03, Agility Digit and the Atlas of Boston Dynamics have no public price, and their price information sits in the non-public layer of pilots and enterprise contracts. The public prices that can be found cover only the smallest, most consumer-facing models; industrial machines are outside the public price range.

Three steps to read an average

Step one, establish its nature: USD 29,600 is an average of revenue divided by units, a third-party estimate pulled down by small research machines, and not the transaction price of any particular full-size model. Step two, look at the mix: separate the average into full-size and small machines, then split by vendor and use, and check whether the two periods used different shipment structures. Step three, set the purpose: decide whether the question is about the market average, the median transaction price of a model, or the total cost of actually deploying one full-size robot in a factory; the three cannot substitute for one another. Public prices serve only as a reference range and cover only consumer-oriented small machines; industrial machines have no public price and should be checked separately through pilots or contracts. Only after these three steps does the number enter a judgment.

Neither estimate framing nor deployment status enters definite conclusions

Several boundaries need holding. The IDC data is an estimate from a research institute relayed by technology media, a tier 3 estimate framing, and cannot be written up as official statistics, nor can the division of two periods be treated as a price cut for the same product. Deployments in the factory sense mostly still require human supervision and are limited to repetitive tasks, and vendors such as Unitree and AgiBot ship mostly research and education models; these are tier 3 and tier 4 items from media and industry compilations and do not enter definite conclusions. Public prices have their own limits: the starting price excludes tax and shipping, the R1 launch price has not been rechecked against the current price, and Optimus is a long-term target rather than a current price. Writing conditional estimates as established facts is the most common overreach in this kind of review.

Keep the average, the median product and factory cost separate

What the data confirms is this: under IDC third-party estimate, humanoid robot revenue in the first half of 2026 exceeded USD 740 million and shipments approached 25,000 units, giving an average of about USD 29,600 that matches the framing given by IDC itself; the same period a year earlier was about USD 37,000; full-size machines accounted for 41.6% of 2025 revenue. USD 29,600 is an average pulled down by small research machines, not the price of any full-size model. The falling average shows the shipment mix tilting toward low-priced small machines and is insufficient to prove that humanoid robots have entered a factory price range. Public prices cover only consumer-oriented small machines, while industrial machines go through pilots and contracts with no public price. Keeping the three quantities in separate accounts is what gives this curve meaning.
