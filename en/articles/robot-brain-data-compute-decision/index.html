Robots Start Building a Brain. Three Bills Come First

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

Unitree founder Wang Xingxing said on 26 November that a combined brain-and-cerebellum system may be ready by year end or next year. The next day, Noetix Robotics product vice president Tang Ziyang put it more bluntly: the next journey for our company and for humanoid robot makers is brain training. Two statements a day apart, one point: the centre of gravity is moving from teaching the body to stand steady to preparing a meal for the brain.

Why build a brain now

A reporter from First Financial heard the same thing at several industry forums: OpenAI's GPT-6 series Astra model had become a driver of embodied progress. Zhang Yiming of Guosheng Securities said physical AI may have formally begun in September. Huang Zhonghuang of SWS Research described the capital logic: in the first half, AI coding exceeded expectations and pushed up big-tech capital spending, which flowed down the compute chain; in the second half, coding iteration slowed and the market looked for the next driver, finding physical AI. Zhang Chengyu of Analysys judged that after two years of capital enthusiasm, players would move from cerebellum to brain. Robots that fight, dance and run still act on commands, cannot sense the real world and cannot answer an improvised human request. All those gaps belong to the brain.

The recipe's first bill: where do ingredients come from?

The raw material for a brain model is data, and the most expensive kind this round is real data. Zhang Yiming relayed a founder back from Silicon Valley: companies like OpenAI and Anthropic need about one million hours of physical AI data, expected to exceed ten million next year, a tenfold jump. Real data includes data from real machines and egocentric data captured by wearable devices. Zhao Dongbin of the Chinese Academy of Sciences said at the REAL Physical AI Open Day that world models trained on real data are closer to reality; pre-training can mix simulation and real data, with high-quality real-machine data reserved for post-training. Xie Chen of Lightwheel Intelligence saw post-training demand rise after Astra, with accurate, high-grade teleoperation data becoming core. The demand on ingredients shifted from quantity to precision.

Why post-training needs different ingredients

Zhang Yiming gave a ratio: the share of real-machine post-training data will rise from 5 to 10 percent this year to 20 percent or higher next year, with real-machine embodiment and tactile data the largest increment, pushing VLA toward VLTA. The added T is touch. Tactile and force sensors on dexterous hands and electronic skin, along with radar and spatial cameras, are new collection hardware. Zhang expects demand to rise for grippers and electronic gloves with vision-touch or electronic skin. That is already showing in financials: RoboSense reported on 25 November that third-quarter lidar shipments for robotics and other uses rose 309 percent year on year to 145,200 units, outgrowing ADAS lidar, with robotics becoming the main source of gross profit. The recipe changed; the purchase order followed.

The second bill: is the stove big enough?

More data means more compute. Zhang Chengyu estimated China's physical AI development infrastructure market will grow from RMB 33.5 billion in 2026 to over RMB 80 billion in 2030; current compute supporting physical AI is about 178 EFLOPS, 5 to 10 percent of national intelligent compute, with smart cars taking over 80 percent of that. Huang Zhonghuang said overseas makers building robot brains already use clusters of about 100,000 cards. Tang Ziyang sees cloud compute becoming essential: multimodal models need multi-card parallel training and several experiments at once, and the cloud scales on demand, with far lower short-term validation cost than owning a cluster and a machine room. The stove has a size; the heat may not follow.

Why the stove does not cook through

Shang Mingdong, co-founder and COO of Jiuzhang Cloud, gave a contrast: compute used for large-model training last year and before averaged above 90 percent utilisation, but physical AI uses compute quite differently. There are many stages: simulation, then data replay, then training and validation, looping repeatedly, each consuming compute in its own way. Poor optimisation and handoffs create data waits and fragment GPU compute, raising cost. He said the physical world cannot be replayed and physical AI is highly latency-sensitive; the contradiction has moved from simply stacking compute to efficiently linking stages into a tight hot loop. As of 2026-11-28, what can be checked are these industry judgements and RoboSense's financials; there is no unified measure of whether physical AI infrastructure is mature. Turn the flame higher and the meal still fails if the pots disconnect.

The third bill: who pays for the meal?

Tang Ziyang listed the pain points: domestic Nvidia cards for world-model training are few and expensive, cluster scheduling efficiency depends on infrastructure quality, and all-flash storage for data is costly. Together these mean brain training is not something a robot maker can carry alone. Zhang Chengyu expects capital spending to flow into physical AI infrastructure, continuing until a steady state around 2033, with physical AI remaining in development for a long time. Cloud suppliers have no clear leader yet, and Zhang expects another build-out of physical AI compute and data infrastructure in the second half of next year. The people paying are still looking; the bills for stove and ingredients arrived first.

What should buyers put in the checklist

The easiest mistake when preparing a brain is treating data volume as data quality. Three things belong in the checklist: first, the source and collection conditions of real-machine data, and whether it can be traced; second, the share of new modalities such as touch and force in post-training data, and whether those sensors can actually enter the supply chain; third, records of handoffs between compute stages, including whether data replay and validation waited and how long. The third is least visible yet most decisive for cost. Filling in these three tables tells you more about whether a company really builds brains than any demonstration.

The menu is written, the food is not served

The route from motion control to brain is clear: the ingredients are real data, the stove is compute infrastructure, and the cook is a toolchain linking simulation, replay, training and validation. The current state is that ingredients are rising in price, the stove is not cooking through and the cook is still being hired. Zhang Chengyu's remark that physical AI will stay in development for a long time sounds conservative but is the most honest judgement available. Whether a robot can answer an improvised human request will not come from one polished demo, but from whether this meal can be cooked on time, every day. The menu is ready; check the kitchen first.

Frequently asked questions

Why did robot makers turn to brain training?

Unitree's Wang Xingxing said a combined brain-and-cerebellum model may be ready by year end or next year, and Noetix Robotics' Tang Ziyang said the next stage for robot makers is brain training. The industry sees GPT-6 Astra improving physical AI simulation completeness; capital markets, meanwhile, are looking for a driver after AI coding slowed.

How much did real-machine data demand grow?

Zhang Yiming relayed a data-collection founder saying leading customers need about one million hours of physical AI data, rising to ten million next year, a tenfold increase; the share of real-machine post-training data will rise from 5 to 10 percent this year to 20 percent or higher next year. These are industry judgements, not completed purchases.

How is physical AI compute different from large-model training?

Shang Mingdong noted that large-model training compute averages over 90 percent utilisation, while physical AI has many stages needing repeated iteration; poor handoffs create data waits and fragment GPU compute. The contradiction has shifted from piling on compute to efficiently linking simulation, replay, training and validation.
