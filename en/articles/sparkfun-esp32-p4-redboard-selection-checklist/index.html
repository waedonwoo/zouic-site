Two P4 Boards at $49.95 and $55.95: Check the Patch First

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

The gap between $49.95 and $55.95 sits in the radio module and the network port; the chip is the same. SparkFun released two IoT RedBoards on 6 November 2026, both built around the ESP32-P4NRW32X in an Uno R3 form factor. As of 24 November 2026, CNX Software and FOSSWire report that both are listed as in stock on the official site. The cheaper one pairs with an ESP32-C5, the dearer one with PoE Ethernet. Write one line on paper first: which network does your project use?

The Six-Dollar Difference Has a Specific Cause

Start with what the two boards share. The ESP32-P4NRW32X packs a dual-core 400MHz high-performance RISC-V, a 40MHz low-power core, 768KB HP L2, 32MB PSRAM and 16MB flash, with 2D PPA, H.264/JPEG codecs and microSD. 32MB of PSRAM is roughly twenty-plus 1.44MB floppy disks. Both boards also share Uno R3 headers (16 digital, 6 analogue), dual USB-C (HS OTG and Serial/JTAG), Qwiic, LiPo charging and fuel gauging (MCP73831 plus MAX17048), 7 to 15V DC input and a WS2812B RGB LED. Choosing four-wheel drive or snow tyres for a car starts from the same chassis; the extra money goes to the road conditions. Hold on, the rest is in what each board adds.

The Moment You Fit It into a Box, You Will Reach for a Ruler

The form factor is Uno R3 at 68.6 by 58.4mm, with dual USB-C, Qwiic and microSD all crowding the edges. Check where the sockets are before renovating, or the cabinet is finished before you notice the plug is blocked; fitting this board works the same way, so measure the enclosure first and decide which way USB-C faces and where the Qwiic wire runs. LiPo charging and fuel gauging are already on board, so if you run on a battery, work out how 7 to 15V DC input switches with the external cell. Do not treat the WS2812B LED as decoration; it is the first sign of whether the board powered up. Settle the mechanical position before talking software. This step is the easiest one to skip.

Wireless and PoE Use Different Wires

The DEV-30909 wireless version reaches the P4 through an ESP32-C5-WROOM-1 over SDIO, supporting 2.4/5GHz Wi-Fi 6, Bluetooth 5 LE and 802.15.4 (Zigbee, Thread); a spare serial header lets you flash the C5, and the P4 and C5 each have their own Boot and Reset. Here is the trap: the ESP32-P4 has no radio of its own, so using 5GHz means getting the C5 side running first. The DEV-30910 PoE version uses a TI DP83825 PHY with a MagJack RJ45, powered by a Silvertel Ag9905M 802.3af module, 36 to 57V input and 5V/1.8A output, about 9 watts, close to a small light bulb. A power strip and a network cable solve different problems: one for too few sockets, the other for a long run. Pull up a photo of the site; if the cable is already in the wall, do not switch to wireless to save six dollars. The position is clear: if the network does not work, nothing else matters.

The Patch in 3.3.12 Decides Whether 5GHz and Ethernet Run

Both boards ship with MicroPython preinstalled and support ESP-IDF and Arduino IDE. That sounds complete, but reports say Arduino Core 3.3.12 currently needs a patch for the C5’s 5GHz Wi-Fi and PoE Ethernet. The report puts it plainly: “A patch is currently needed to use the C5’s 5GHz Wi-Fi and PoE Ethernet.” Read that line twice. What a developer does is download the patch, rebuild, and run a scan again, rather than plugging the board in and assuming everything works. The easiest thing to miss: a board that lights up does not mean 5GHz and Ethernet run. When SparkFun released the boards it also published the schematics and KiCad files (according to FOSSWire), which is where a patch becomes workable. Confirm your Core version first, then dig through the repository; keep that order.

Published Files Make Self-Repair Possible

Publishing the schematics and KiCad files only shows its value on the day something breaks. The instructions with IKEA furniture let you assemble it step by step against the parts; without them, one missing screw means throwing the whole thing away. A development board is the same: with KiCad files you can follow silkscreen to trace pins, reroute a wire and reproduce a minimal system. SparkFun releasing the open hardware files means developers do not have to wait for the next firmware. The actions are simple: download the files, check the C5 SDIO pins and the RJ45 PHY connection, run the smallest Wi-Fi scan or Ethernet link routine, and record the result. Save them locally first; it takes a few minutes.

A First Coarse Screen in Three Minutes

The first round on arrival is only a coarse screen. After unpacking, look for knocks on the board and headers and measure the size; connect 7 to 15V DC or USB-C, see whether the WS2812B lights up and whether a MicroPython prompt appears on serial; run a 2.4GHz and 5GHz scan to see if the C5 is recognised on both; on the PoE version plug in a cable and watch the link LED and negotiated speed. Put every step in one table with four columns: item, condition, result, version. Do not chase benchmark scores at this stage; the value is in whether the interfaces are complete, the patch can be applied and the files can be reproduced. Three rounds tell you more than ten unboxing posts. Power on first, judge later.

Projects That Should Skip These Two Boards

The boundaries deserve a line too. The PoE version has 10/100 Ethernet, so if the site already runs gigabit cable, its port will not fill the pipe. If you need 5GHz but want to avoid a patch, the Arduino IDE path is awkward for now; move to ESP-IDF or prepare the patch. To run Matter or Thread, check that the C5’s 802.15.4 works in your stack; hardware support on the board and software availability are two different things. These boards sell two networking approaches, and the chip itself is not new, so anyone drawn by the ESP32-P4 name should check first whether the radio they need is actually on the board. Before treating PoE as a selling point, check whether your network cable can carry power.

One Sheet Written Before Ordering

Write four lines on paper: where it mounts, which network it uses, whether the patch is applied, whether the files are there. Once written, the six-dollar difference drops to last place. What really decides is whether the cable is already in the wall, whether you are willing to apply the 5GHz patch, and whether you have the KiCad files on the day something fails. SparkFun has put both the board and the files out; the question the developer must answer is about their own site. The box is still sealed, so do not hit the order button yet; who will you hand this board to, and in what environment will it first power on?

Frequently asked questions

How do I choose between the wireless and PoE versions?

Look at the site first. The DEV-30909 wireless version uses the ESP32-C5 for 2.4/5GHz Wi-Fi 6, Bluetooth 5 LE and 802.15.4, suiting sites without a cable that need Zigbee or Thread; the DEV-30910 PoE version runs 10/100 Ethernet and 802.3af power, suiting a spot where the cable is already laid and one wire must carry data and power. The gap between $49.95 and $55.95 is only six dollars, far less important than whether a cable reaches the site. If the site is wired for gigabit, the PoE version’s 10/100 port will not keep up; check that separately.

What does the Arduino Core 3.3.12 patch affect?

According to media relays, Arduino Core 3.3.12 currently needs a patch to use the C5’s 5GHz Wi-Fi and the PoE version’s Ethernet; those two paths are affected. Work in order: confirm the Core version, download the patch and rebuild, then run a 5GHz scan or Ethernet link test and record it. If you would rather avoid a patch for now, assess whether the ESP-IDF path is enough.

MicroPython comes preinstalled; do I still need to check Arduino IDE?

Yes. Preinstalled MicroPython only means you reach an interactive prompt on power-up; it does not mean the Arduino IDE path works. Both boards support ESP-IDF and Arduino IDE, but reports say Arduino Core 3.3.12 still needs a patch for 5GHz and Ethernet. On arrival, check the MicroPython prompt over serial, then install Arduino Core and run a minimal sketch to see whether compiling and flashing go smoothly; do not let the preinstalled environment stand in for a software-stack check.
