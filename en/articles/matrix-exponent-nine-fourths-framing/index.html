A 57 Year Old Answer Reads 2.25, and No One Has Graded It

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

An answer sheet comes in, and the last page reads 2.25. The question is how many operations matrix multiplication really needs, and humanity has been asking for 57 years. On 24 November OpenAI put this 111 page preprint into the openai/math repository, claiming the exponent is exactly 9/4. The paper is thick. The person grading it has not picked up a pen. The right order is to separate what is written on the sheet from where the marking stops.

What Is on the Answer Sheet?

The preprint is titled The matrix multiplication exponent is nine fourths. The abstract says it in plain words: we prove that the exponent of square matrix multiplication is 9/4 over every field, and the upper bound was already known. The genuinely new part is the matching lower bound: for every n at least 2, multiplying n by n matrices in the formal arithmetic circuit model needs at least ceil(n^(9/4)/2) multiplication or division gates. The upper bound was built by others. This sheet fills in the lower half.

Where 2.25 Sits

The background is worth laying out. An upper bound of at most 9/4 was already known: OpenAI established it over the complex numbers in a preprint on 2 October, and Amanov and others extended it to all fields. The best upper bound in the literature before this was about 2.371177, from a preprint in August 2026; Strassen’s result from 1969 was log base 2 of 7, about 2.807. The long standing public conjecture was 2, barely above the cost of reading n^2 elements at all. The new result says the answer is 2.25, exactly a quarter above the exponent of the output size.

The First Marking Boundary

The first thing to write down is the review status. This is a preprint, and as of 25 November no named expert has issued a review of the lower bound proof. In mathematics the road from posting a paper to acceptance can be longer than writing it. Putting an unreviewed conclusion into an engineering roadmap is like photocopying an ungraded answer sheet and handing it to the whole team as a textbook. You can read it, you can discuss it, you cannot hand it out as a report card.

The Second Marking Boundary

The second boundary is the model. What is proved here is the formal arithmetic circuit, where addition, subtraction, multiplication and division each count as one step. It does not cover bit level algorithms such as the Word RAM. The two models price operations differently, and how far the conclusion travels depends on which machine you use. An optimum written into a paper still sits several layers of translation away from the floating point card in your machine room. Leave those layers unstated and 2.25 gets read as a universal speed ceiling on any hardware.

The Third Marking Boundary

The third boundary concerns the accompanying Lean formalisation. It is narrower than the main theorem and covers only circuits without division, so the strongest claim, that the result holds over all fields, has not been verified by machine. Formal verification is not decoration here. It is the tool that lets a third party check the proof line by line. When the tool does not reach the most important sentence, the grader has read only the first half of the booklet. That does not deny the conclusion. It marks where the conclusion currently stands.

Other Answer Sheets the Same Week

This preprint is not alone. Nine manuscripts in all were added to the repository the same day, from the families 379 and 380, forming a public contest with Anthropic’s VP versus VNP tree claim from the day before. In the same week Google Cogentic released 11 papers at once, among them unconditional subexponential factorisation. AI doing mathematics is moving from checking other people’s calculations to declaring theorems. More declarations do not add up to more results that hold, and years with dense submissions call for slower grading, not faster.

How Far Is It from the Machine Room?

The distance to engineering has to be stated on its own. Matrix multiplication is a base operation in numerical computing and deep learning training, and if the exponent holds, its theoretical significance needs no explanation. But in measurements the naive triple loop still scales as n^2.99, and the 2.25 algorithm sits several orders of magnitude away from practical use in constants and construction complexity. A theoretically faster algorithm is faster only once its constants are small enough to beat implementation overhead. Treating an asymptotic exponent as next year’s training speedup is a misuse of a theoretical result.

Wait Until the Paper Is Graded

The judgement worth taking away is learning to tell an answer sheet from a report card. The idea behind the lower bound proof is interesting on its own: reduce the target problem to a decision problem with a known lower bound, then use a combinatorial lower bound to block an algebraic upper bound. Other problems can borrow that move, and even if the exponent is later revised, the method stays. As of 26 November 2026, what can be checked is what the preprint says and where it draws its limits; whether it has been proved cannot yet be checked. The sheet lies on the table, waiting for someone willing to read it line by line.

Frequently asked questions

Does 2.25 mean training gets much faster right away?

No such inference is possible. The preprint describes a theoretical exponent in the formal arithmetic circuit model; in measurements a naive triple loop still scales as n^2.99, and the 2.25 algorithm is several orders of magnitude away from practical implementation in constants and construction complexity. A theoretical exponent is not the speedup a current framework will show.

Has the result been proved?

Not yet, and it cannot be stated that way. The paper is a preprint and, as of 25 November, no named expert has published a review of the lower bound proof. The accompanying Lean formalisation covers only circuits without division, so the strongest claim, that it holds over all fields, has not been machine verified. You may quote the claim and state its status; you may not write it as an established theorem.
