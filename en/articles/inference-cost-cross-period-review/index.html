Inference cost across quarters: how TCO line items move under one framing

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

First, be clear about what this review does not do: this website holds no verifiable cross-period price series, so it draws no curve claiming a quarter-on-quarter fall in cost per million tokens, and it reports no speedup ratios. What follows unpacks inference cost as a line-item review along the 5W1H axes, with the six dimensions standing for data, framing, sources, time span, counterexamples and boundaries. The focus is not how total cost moves, but how the line items move under one framing and which numbers must not be compared side by side. Conclusion first: denominator, cost boundary, power boundary, quality gate and baseline date — if any one of the five changes, the two periods are no longer comparable. An everyday analogy: comparing vegetable prices between two supermarkets, where one sells washed produce and the other sells it with soil and roots, one charges member prices and the other runs a same-day promotion — four numbers in a row look comparable, yet the units, the time points and the framing are all different.

What: the review covers line-item framing, not values

On the data dimension, start by admitting what is in hand and what is not. Verifiable are the line-item framings and the checking method; not verifiable is a price or unit-price series spanning several quarters — this website does not have the latter and does not construct one. The official MLCommons description of MLPerf Inference provides the reference: the benchmark suite defines the problem, the models and the quality targets through reference implementations and specifies scenarios, latency constraints and submission rules, for architecture-neutral, reproducible comparison; officially, a benchmark result corresponds to a specific workload, scenario and submission configuration and is not a universal throughput constant. A contestable claim: inference cost was never a single number — it is a ratio of a set of line items divided by a denominator, and once the denominator changes, the whole ratio measures something else; staring only at the total-cost aggregate while ignoring the line items amounts to hiding framing drift behind the decimal point. The first step of the review is to build a line-item table: the numerator is split into items by cost boundary, and the denominator is stated clearly as per token, per million tokens or per request; how the items are split is defined by the reviewing party and written into the template — it is not an official framing. If the table cannot be built, stop here and do not go on calculating.

Why: if any of the five variables changes, the two periods are not comparable

The framing dimension explains why line items matter more than the aggregate. Break incomparability into five switches: the denominator (per token, per million tokens or per request, and whether all outputs were compliant); the cost boundary (cloud rental or owned hardware, whether network, storage and labour are included, whether idle capacity is included); the power boundary (the chip’s nominal thermal design power, TDP, or measured whole-system power); the quality gate (whether model, precision, latency constraint and compliant outputs are the same); and the baseline date (the checking date 2026-11-14, not the run date). A contestable claim: if any one of these switches is flipped between the two periods, the change obtained by subtracting or dividing the two periods’ numbers mostly reflects the altered framing, not a change in cost itself. So the conclusion should not be that cost fell or rose, but whether line item X under framing A may stand beside the same class of item from the previous period; how items move under one framing is worth examining, while numbers across framings should only be marked not comparable.

Who: responsibility for framing drift sits with the reviewing party, not the benchmark body

The sources dimension separates who defines, who submits and who labels. MLCommons and the reference implementations define the problem, the models, the quality targets, the scenarios, the latency constraints and the submission rules, keeping the comparison architecture-neutral and reproducible; submitters decide their configurations and produce a benchmark result; the reviewing party is responsible for labelling clearly which workload, scenario and submission configuration a result corresponds to, and for judging whether the two periods share a framing; third-party compilations serve only as hints for checking items. A contestable claim: responsibility for framing drift sits with the reviewing party, not the benchmark body — the benchmark’s job is to define one measurement clearly, and it owes no guarantee that numbers from two differently configured runs remain comparable when someone compares them across periods.

When: cross-quarter comparison presupposes one shared ruler for both periods

On the time-span dimension, the word “quarter” is what most easily leads people astray. The baseline date is the checking date 2026-11-14, explicitly not the date on which any run or event occurred; for a cross-quarter claim to hold, three things must first be pinned down: each period’s start and end dates, the length of the sampling or measurement window, and the stretch of time the measurement covers (peak or steady state, with or without warm-up). A contestable claim: if the two periods used different sampling windows, or one period took peak throughput while the other took steady-state power, “across quarters” merely lines up two sets of incomparable numbers in two columns, and the time label manufactures a comparability that never existed. The reminder in third-party compilations — that the highest throughput of one run must not be spliced with the lowest power of another — is exactly this class of checking item; but it is compiled by third parties, the originals were not directly verified, it only indicates a direction of checking, and no specific price or speedup ratio enters the conclusion.

Where: which framing table the four boundaries land in

This dimension places the five switches in their concrete record positions. The denominator boundary lands on ratios such as cost per token: per the checking items compiled by third parties, the denominator must use compliant outputs from the same measurement window, and peak throughput must not be divided by an on-demand price; the cost boundary lands on a line-item table, in which cloud-rental and owned-hardware items must not be mixed — the former is an operating-expense framing, the latter a capital-and-depreciation framing; the power boundary lands on measured whole-system power rather than chip TDP; the quality gate lands on model, precision and latency constraints. A contestable claim: unless these four land in one shared framing table, the review merely piles up numbers of different provenance and different units — rich in appearance, yet no item can be checked against the other period. The two named counterexamples also take their places here: dividing peak throughput by the on-demand price to get a cost per million tokens splices quantities from different measurement windows into one ratio; mixing cloud-rental and owned-hardware items into one table compresses two boundaries into one.

How: how a line-item review is organised and how numbers get marked incomparable

The method dimension folds the six dimensions into a repeatable set of moves. One, state the data boundary: this website holds no verifiable cross-period price series, and the object of the review is line-item framing, not values. Two, build the framing table: fill in values one by one for denominator, cost boundary, power boundary, quality gate and baseline date, with the two periods side by side. Three, check item by item: under one framing, see which class each line item falls into and whether the framing matches the previous period, rather than watching whether the total-cost aggregate rose or fell. Four, mark incomparability: if any of the five differs, or a counterexample appears such as peak throughput paired with an on-demand price or cloud rental mixed with owned hardware, mark it immediately as not directly comparable and keep it out of subtraction or division. Five, write down counterexamples and boundaries: keep the excluded numbers and the reasons for exclusion in the record instead of quietly deleting them. A contestable claim: the quality of a review is measured not by how many percentages it computed but by how many numbers it honestly marked incomparable; a review that computes with every number usually just dresses framing drift up as a trend.

Boundaries: no fabricated series, no hint promoted into a conclusion

The boundaries section spells out the restraint. First, this website holds no verifiable cross-period price series; the whole piece gives no specific price, unit price, percentage or speedup ratio, and forecasts no future cost trend — wherever numbers appear, they are the conditional statements inside official anchors or the names of counterexamples, not a series measured by this website. Second, the third-party compilations on “TDP cannot replace measured whole-system power”, “the highest throughput must not be spliced with the lowest power” and “the denominator must use compliant outputs within the same measurement window” serve only as hints for checking items; they are compiled by third parties, the originals were not directly verified, no specific price or speedup ratio enters the conclusion, and they are not promoted into the conclusion itself. Third, this article confines itself to analysis at the engineering and framing level: no valuation, no accounting or legal characterisation; consult separately on commercial terms and prices. A contestable claim: writing a review despite having no data is not a shortage of information — it is the deliberate removal of the most deceptive, seemingly precise trend numbers.

Conclusion: the conclusion is framing analysis only, handing over a comparability checklist

Bringing it down to the deliverable: this review hands over no cost curve; what it hands over is a two-period comparability checklist — whether the denominator is defined the same way, whether the cost boundary is the same scope, whether the power boundary is measured in both cases, whether the quality gate is the same configuration, whether the baseline date is explicitly the checking date, and whether each line item falls into the same class in both periods. A contestable claim: when the conclusion is only that these five must be locked before anything is comparable, it is not the absence of a conclusion — it swaps “how much the cost changed” for “which numbers are entitled to be compared”, and the latter is the useful one when data is insufficient. Inference cost is the same: first write the denominator, the boundaries, the gates and the baseline date into a template, then talk about how the line items move; movements under one framing deserve item-by-item attention, while numbers across framings are marked incomparable across the board. For the reader, this review provides a ruler for judging whether somebody else’s declining curve deserves trust.

Sources and framing (as of 14 November 2026): the baseline date is the date of this check, not the date of any benchmark run or procurement; this article holds no verifiable cross-period price series, so it neither has nor constructs price or speedup-ratio values. Level 1 (normative text): this article cites no publicly verifiable normative text; its judgements rest on the benchmark body’s official descriptions. Level 2 (platform or consortium official documentation): the official MLCommons description of MLPerf Inference states that the benchmark suite defines the problem, the models and the quality targets through reference implementations and specifies scenarios, latency constraints and submission rules, for architecture-neutral, reproducible comparison; it also makes clear that a benchmark result corresponds to a specific workload, scenario and submission configuration and is not a universal throughput constant — the benchmark body’s official framing. Level 3 (this website’s own framing): the five-item framing framework (denominator, cost boundary, power boundary, quality gate, baseline date), the five review steps, the two-period comparability checklist and the decision line that a different framing means marking numbers incomparable are all checking items this article designed for the review, not official conclusions. Level 4 (third-party compilations): the compilations on “TDP cannot replace measured whole-system power”, “the highest throughput of one run must not be spliced with the lowest power of another” and “the denominator of cost per token must use compliant outputs within the same measurement window”, plus the two counterexamples of dividing peak throughput by the on-demand price to obtain a cost per million tokens and mixing cloud rental with owned hardware in one table; these are compiled by third parties, the originals were not directly verified, they serve only as directional checking items, and no specific price or speedup ratio enters the conclusion. Level 5 (framings set side by side): when the two periods differ on any of the five framing items, the numbers are written side by side and marked not directly comparable, explicitly without averaging or mutual conversion; this article’s conclusion does framing analysis only. Wherever prices and compliance appear, the description is engineering-level only and carries no legal characterisation.

Frequently asked questions

Why does this review not give a declining cost curve?

Because this website holds no verifiable cross-period price series, and forcing a curve into shape would amount to fabricating numbers. The object of this review is line-item framing, not values: only after the five items — denominator, cost boundary, power boundary, quality gate and baseline date — are locked down do the two periods acquire the precondition of comparability. The conclusion confines itself to framing analysis.

What is wrong with dividing peak throughput by the on-demand price to get a cost per million tokens?

It splices quantities from different measurement windows into one ratio: peak throughput comes from the highest point of some run, the on-demand price is a different framing, and the denominator is not necessarily the compliant output within the same window. This is one of the named counterexamples; it should be marked not directly comparable and kept out of subtraction or division.

Can cloud-rental and owned-hardware costs be put into one table for comparison?

No. The former is an operating-expense framing, the latter a capital-and-depreciation framing; the cost boundaries differ, and mixing them into one table compresses the two boundaries into one without anyone noticing. The correct move is to state each scope in the framing table first and, whenever any of the five framing items differs, mark the numbers “not directly comparable”; such checking items, compiled by third parties, serve only as directional hints, and no specific numbers enter the conclusion.
