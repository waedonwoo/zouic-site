After the High-NA EUV tool arrives: delivery and facility acceptance hold points

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

When a High-NA EUV system (a high-numerical-aperture extreme-ultraviolet lithography tool) arrives on site, what gets signed is not a single “tool passes” sheet but three separate records: tool parameters, exposure conditions and facility provisions. What follows breaks the matter down along 5W1H into a checklist that can be worked through step by step, covering applicability, preparation, steps, records, review and exception handling. Conclusion first: wafers per hour (WpH) moves with the exposure dose, so copying a throughput figure into a delivery acceptance criterion without its dose condition means signing off a variable as if it were a constant. It is like accepting a central air-conditioning system: the nameplate cooling capacity is bound to a specific outdoor temperature, and acceptance has to record the operating condition along with the number — copy only the number, and at the height of summer there is nothing to check it against.

What: three records, not one throughput number

Applicability comes first, that is, what exactly the checklist accepts. Per ASML’s official TWINSCAN EXE:5000 product page, the tool offers 0.55 NA (numerical aperture) and 8 nm resolution, prints features 1.7 times smaller and roughly 2.9 times the transistor density in a single exposure, delivers imaging contrast about 40% higher than NXE, uses anamorphic optics, and carries an exposure field half the size of NXE — all of this goes into the tool-parameters record. The other two records are the exposure-conditions record (dose conditions and the throughput framing tied to the dose) and the facility-provisions record (vibration, temperature control, power, vacuum and mask handling, plus tool-to-tool overlay accuracy). One contestable claim: squeezing the three into a single “tool passes” sheet is a category error — their measurement points, responsible parties and failure criteria all differ. First step to follow: set up three separate sheets, each carrying a verification date, a measurement point and a signatory, and do not move on while any sheet remains incompletely signed.

Why: a throughput number means nothing without its dose condition

Preparation first nails down one principle: throughput is a conditioned quantity, not a fixed attribute. ASML’s product page itself explains the coupling — the exposure field is only half that of NXE, which calls for faster wafer and mask stages, together with a lower exposure dose per wafer to shorten the cycle time; throughput, exposure field and dose are already interlocked in the official framing. The ≥185 WpH@20 mJ/cm² figure from ASML’s 2023 financial-reporting materials is written that way precisely because WpH only holds when bound to a dose condition; the @20 mJ/cm² is not a footnote, it is half the number. One contestable claim: a delivery acceptance criterion that states a WpH figure stripped of its dose cannot be reproduced at review and should not be accepted by signature. Action to follow: give every throughput figure a mandatory “dose condition” field in the record sheet; if it is empty, the whole row is invalid and goes back for completion. Better to leave a record blank than to keep a number nobody can reproduce; the checklist governs reviewability, it does not crown the best vendor.

Who: four parties, four signing boundaries — the party under acceptance must not vouch for itself

Preparation also fixes who signs which sheet. The tool vendor answers for parameters and conditioned figures; the facility team answers for the compliance records on vibration, temperature control, power, vacuum and mask handling; the process and tool-matching parties supply the measured overlay; the buyer or accepting party only signs the summary and never fills in numbers on anyone’s behalf. One contestable claim: letting the tool vendor casually sign off the facility conditions as well makes the accepted party vouch for itself — however complete the records, their independence is gone. Rule to follow: each sheet is signed only by its responsible party, plus one countersigning reviewer who enters no numbers; if a cross-party field is left empty by either side, the whole item goes back. Writing responsibility into the sheet header works better than into meeting minutes — headers get validated at export. Everyday life is the same: when handover-checking a flat, plumbing, structure and fire safety each come from a different inspection body, and the renovation contractor cannot sign for all of them.

When: one signature at each of four hold points — no merging, no backdating

At the step level, acceptance is spread across four points in time, each with its own signature: the arrival and unpacking check (transit and as-delivered condition, and whether the nameplate matches the official product page), the standalone parameter re-measurement of the tool after installation, the exposure-condition lock-in (dose, throughput framing and the figures are fixed together — the most critical step, because until the conditions are locked every later number hangs in the air), and the pre-volume-production review of tool-to-tool overlay together with the facility interactions. One contestable claim: merging the four into one “final acceptance” discards the intermediate-state evidence, and afterwards nobody can tell whether a deviation came from transport, installation or condition drift. Requirement to follow: each hold point carries a timestamp, an original record number and a named owner; no signatures added after the fact, no backdating; a skipped point is marked pending review, not passed by default.

Where: the measurement point goes into the record, not into a note saying “next to the tool”

Where records and steps cross is exactly where the “where” gets written concretely. The record items are fixed at eight categories: resolution, NA, exposure field, dose condition, throughput framing, tool-to-tool overlay, facility provisions (vibration, temperature control, power, vacuum, mask handling), and the signing responsible party; each item gets three entries: the measurement point, the instrument or method, and where the raw data is stored. One contestable claim: writing down a measured value without its measurement point hands over a number that cannot be redone — the reviewer would not know at which tool, at which port, to re-measure. Action to follow: attach a measurement-point number, an instrument number and a raw-data file name to every item; vague locations such as “next to the tool” are judged non-conforming and sent back. The hold point answers when something was measured, the measurement point answers where; only with both can a record be traced and reproduced like following a map.

How: six steps to follow, plus a pre-export check that stops empty fields

Applicability, preparation, steps, records, review and exception handling are chained into one workflow that can be followed as written. One, build a three-column record: official statements, conditioned figures, this site’s own judgement — the columns never bleed into each other. Two, copy every throughput figure together with its dose condition; if it is missing, mark the row invalid. Three, sign off in sequence at the four hold points: arrival, installation, condition lock-in, pre-volume-production. Four, before export, review whether the three columns have stayed separate and whether any dose condition is empty. Five, exception handling: a missing dose, or a clash between two dose framings placed side by side, goes into the pending-review queue — no silent pass, no automatic averaging. Six, archive the original records and the review signatures. One contestable claim: the value of a checklist lies not in how many items it lists but in the assertion before export that automatically stops an “empty dose condition”; without it, even the longest checklist is only an editorial convention resting on people’s diligence.

Boundaries: when dose framings sit side by side, take no average and infer no adoption schedule

This block holds the places where the checklist stops carrying weight and restraint is required; it belongs to exception handling as well. The most typical exception is framings placed side by side: the ≥185 WpH from ASML’s 2023 financial-reporting materials corresponds to 20 mJ/cm², while a third-party compilation of a 2026 roadmap gives a different set of figures at a 50 mJ/cm² dose — the two dose conditions are not the same. One contestable claim: averaging two figures taken under different dose conditions, or stitching them into a line such as “throughput improves”, is the second error this checklist exists to stop; the correct move is to write the difference out with the framings side by side, state explicitly that no average is taken, and keep the third-party figures out of the delivery conclusion. The third-party compilation of the 2026 roadmap serves only as a directional cue; it is as compiled by third parties, the original was not directly verified, and its concrete figures do not enter the conclusion. Three further boundaries: no assessment of the vendor’s commercial progress, no inference about when a specific wafer fab will adopt the tool, and no filling in of unpublicised parameters or yields on the vendor’s behalf.

Conclusion: write “accept together with its conditions” into a field, not into a reminder

The closing sequence: first fix the three separate records, then bind a dose-condition field to every throughput figure, then lock in the signatures at the four hold points and the responsibilities of the four parties, and finally turn “empty dose condition means invalid” into an assertion that runs before export. One contestable claim: if “remember to check the dose” lives only in training material instead of a field validation, that reminder is the first casualty when a new crew takes over or a deadline presses; only as a mandatory field and an export gate will the conditions be signed off together with the numbers. The checklist does not tell you whether the tool is good; it only guarantees that when you declare acceptance passed, there is a stack of evidence that someone else could recompute end to end — not a single throughput number of unknown provenance, divorced from its dose.

Frequently asked questions

Why must a throughput figure always be signed off together with its dose condition?

Because wafers per hour (WpH) is a conditioned quantity: the ≥185 WpH in ASML’s 2023 financial-reporting materials corresponds to a dose condition of 20 mJ/cm². A number noted without its dose cannot be reproduced at review — it signs off a variable as a constant. The checklist rule that an empty dose-condition field invalidates the whole row exists precisely to stop this.

Can the three records be merged into a single signing sheet?

Not advisable. Tool parameters, exposure conditions and facility provisions differ in measurement points, responsible parties and failure criteria; pressing them into one “tool passes” sheet is a category error and also discards the intermediate-state evidence. Set up three separate sheets instead, each with a verification date, a measurement point and a signatory, and do not move on while any sheet remains incompletely signed.

What should be done when figures under two different dose framings clash?

Write the difference out with the framings side by side, state explicitly that no average is taken, and keep the third-party figures out of the delivery conclusion; the item goes into the pending-review queue and is marked pending review rather than passed by default. The ASML 2023 financial-reporting materials and the third-party compilation of a 2026 roadmap rest on different dose conditions — that is exactly such a case.
