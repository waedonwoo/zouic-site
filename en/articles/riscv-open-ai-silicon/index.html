Open silicon does not sell price-performance, it sells the right to take the chip apart

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

Measured in tokens per dollar, Tenstorrent cannot beat NVIDIA today, and probably does not intend to compete on that axis. What an open ISA genuinely makes scarce in AI accelerators is this: customers can read it, change it, license it, and put the whole chip into their own supply chain — four things the CUDA system grants none of.

A chip deliberately built against the trend

Blackhole is built on TSMC 6 nm with 120 Tensix cores (140 at launch, revised to 120 by firmware in January 2026; the vendor says typical workloads lose about 1-2%), 16 SiFive X280 big cores plus 752 small RISC-V control cores, 180 MB on-chip SRAM, 28-32 GB GDDR6 (448-512 GB/s), ten 400GbE links, PCIe Gen5, 300 W and 664 TFLOPS Block FP8.

The most interesting decision is that it does not use HBM. While everyone else fights for HBM4 capacity, Blackhole picks far cheaper GDDR6 and moves data through on-chip SRAM and a 2D NoC mesh; scale-out also avoids NVLink in favour of ten standard 400GbE direct links. This is the contrarian bet Jim Keller stated publicly at TT-Deploy 2026: he does not believe in the disaggregated-plus-specialised-hardware-plus-all-SRAM route, and argues for one chip running both prefill and decode so a single model shape cannot land a knockout blow.

Products and prices (vendor website): p100a USD 999, p150a/b USD 1,399, four-chip liquid-cooled TT-QuietBox workstation around USD 9,999-11,999; the Galaxy 6U system packs 32 Blackhole chips, 23 PFLOPS dense FP8, 1 TB GDDR6 and 32 TB/s interconnect for about USD 110,000, with GA in April 2026. The point of that price level is not cheapness — it is that for the first time individuals and small institutions can buy a fully auditable AI accelerator.

How far does the open actually go

The licensing route is already producing real products: RISC-V International reports that Korea's BOS Semiconductors used Tenstorrent's Tensix NPU IP to build the Eagle-N, an automotive-grade RISC-V AI accelerator chiplet for ADAS and cockpit compute. A division of labour where one company makes IP, another a chiplet and a third the vehicle is almost impossible inside a closed ISA system.

Stating the limits honestly

The disadvantages are equally clear: no MLPerf submissions, so every performance figure is a vendor or community self-test; the claim of running about 90% of Hugging Face models is a vendor statement from TT-Deploy (2.5 million out of 2.8 million models passing), not an independent reproduction; the software ecosystem trails CUDA by years, and national labs, sovereign compute projects and research institutions willing to write their own kernels are the main customers today. A number like roughly 308 t/s on DeepSeek is also a server-side result quoted by Keller, not a comparable public benchmark.

So do not write this up as open silicon about to overturn NVIDIA. The more accurate reading is: it is proving that a different business model works — making money from IP licensing, auditability and a seat at the toolchain table, rather than renting out a locked ecosystem. For domestic vendors, this path is also more actionable than building another CUDA: secure licensable cores and interconnect first, then talk about ecosystem.

Bottom line

An open ISA will not change market share in the short term; it changes who is entitled to help define the next generation of interconnect and toolchains. With GDDR6, Ethernet and an Apache 2.0 software stack, Blackhole turns an AI accelerator from a black box back into an engineering design you can take apart. What is worth tracking is not benchmarks but three things: whether MLPerf submissions appear, whether Tensix IP licensing produces a second volume customer, and whether tt-metal attracts sustained contributors from outside Tenstorrent. If those three land, openness starts to compound.

Frequently asked questions

Why does Blackhole avoid HBM?

It uses GDDR6 (28-32 GB, 448-512 GB/s) with 180 MB of on-chip SRAM and a 2D NoC, designing around data movement rather than stacking memory bandwidth. It is a deliberate route around HBM capacity and cost, and still needs independent benchmark validation.

What does open source actually cover for these chips?

The open RISC-V ISA, the Apache 2.0 tt-metal/tt-forge stack, published board schematics, kernel-level access, and IP licensing to third parties. It does not mean the hardware design can be copied freely; each project's licence governs that.

Is now the time to move production inference to Tenstorrent?

Mainstream production still runs predominantly on NVIDIA. Tenstorrent has no MLPerf submissions and published performance is mostly vendor-measured, so it fits research institutions, national labs and sovereign compute projects willing to write their own kernels.
