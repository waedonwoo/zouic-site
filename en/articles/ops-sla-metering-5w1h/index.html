Operational SLA metering: who records what, and which record prevails in a dispute

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

This is a decision dossier: it records the decisions that have to be made in the metering of a service level agreement during operations, not a lesson in how to write down a 99.9%. Two things have to be settled first: the framing under which that percentage is computed, and which record prevails once the two sides’ numbers disagree. The cloud SLA measurement model in ISO/IEC 19086-2:2018 gives an example that makes the point sharply: two providers both state 99.9% availability, one defining unavailability by time and the other by transactions, and the customer holding those two commitments cannot compare them at all. From this comes a falsifiable claim: until the measurement definition is written down clearly, any argument about “was the target met” is spinning its wheels. This note uses 5W1H to break the six decisions apart, with the emphasis on multi-party responsibility and framing boundaries; beyond that boundary it discusses only measurement definitions and evidence-retention methods that are reviewable in engineering terms. Conclusion first: fix the definition and the record priority, then talk about meeting the target — reverse the order and every later step has to be redone.

What: the decisions are the measurement definition and the record priority, not the percentage

The first decision is to recognise what is actually being decided. What is genuinely at issue in operations is not a number such as “should availability be 99.9% or higher”, but the measurement model that defines that number and the record that counts in a dispute. ISO/IEC 19086-2:2018 establishes common terminology and a cloud SLA measurement model, defining the cloud service measurement CSM (one reproducible measurement of the object being measured), SLO and SQO (the former sets the service level objective to be reached, the latter describes service quality characteristics), as well as measurement conditions and interpretation rules; yet it explicitly does not mandate any one specific set of metrics, leaving the choice of indicators to the user. This is exactly where a falsifiable judgement lands: the standard gives the specification for the graduations on the ruler, but does not decide for you which length to measure or which ruler to use. Treating “set the metrics, set the framing, set the priority” as something that must be decided before signing, rather than as an understanding that will simply grow out of operations, is the central position of this dossier; anyone who thinks writing down a percentage completes the SLA has in fact left the most important decision for later.

Why: the definition has to come before any talk of meeting the target

Why can the order not be reversed? Because the same percentage measures entirely different things under different algorithms. A time-based algorithm asks “what proportion of a period was unavailable”; a transaction-based algorithm asks “what proportion of requests failed”. A brief outage that hits every request yields widely different degrees of unavailability under the two framings, and both reports can state 99.9% with a straight face. An everyday analogy: two shops both claim an “out-of-stock rate of one in a thousand”, one counting the hours out of stock, the other counting orders that could not be filled, and the customer has no way to judge which is more reliable. The availability example with two providers in the standard text is precisely about this. From this comes a sharp, falsifiable claim: whether a target was met is not a question of fact but a question of definition; with definitions unaligned, however complete the monitoring and however dense the sampling, the parties are only talking past each other in a more refined way. Fixing the definition first is, in essence, agreeing the scoring rules before the match starts — otherwise after the game anyone can produce a score that favours them.

Who: who records, who reviews, who adjudicates — three sets of records kept apart

The second decision concerns the division of responsibility, and this is where multi-party responsibility most easily becomes vague. At least three kinds of record are being produced at the same time: the provider’s own monitoring system, independent probing on the customer side, and third-party re-checking brought in when necessary. ISO/IEC 19086-3:2017 gives core conformity requirements covering service monitoring, roles and responsibilities, SLO, measurement, remedies and exceptions, and explicitly does not prescribe a uniform SLA structure — which is to say the standard only asks that roles and responsibilities be stated clearly and does not rank priorities for you. The three records each have their own time source, sampling window, exclusion rules and retention period; if the annex does not state whose priority is highest, a dispute turns into “whose monitoring is more complete” rather than “did a failure occur”. Here is a falsifiable judgement: more records do not make the picture more trustworthy; without a pre-agreed priority, multiple records instead drag a dispute from fact-finding into an argument over data legitimacy. So the decision to be made is quite concrete: who owns each record, how the fields are aligned, which record prevails on conflict, and what conditions trigger third-party involvement.

When: definitions and priority have to be fixed before a dispute arises

The third decision is about timing. The measurement definition, sampling window, exclusion rules and record priority have to be written down once, at the contracting stage, as a contract annex — not patched together after some outage when each side digs through its own logs. The reason is direct: definitions added after the fact inevitably tilt toward whoever benefits, the provider leaning toward broader exclusion rules, the customer side toward stricter sampling, and by then there is no neutral baseline to fall back on. The standard lists “measurement conditions and interpretation rules” separately precisely because once conditions can vary, results cannot be compared. One falsifiable claim: leaving the definition to “be refined later” is not pragmatic — it postpones the decision most in need of a calm moment to the least calm, least trusting moment. The workable engineering approach is to freeze the definition, the record fields and the priority into a versioned annex, route every adjustment through a change with an audit trail and a noted effective time, so that each compliance judgement can be traced back to the version of the definition in force at that moment, rather than to whoever argued loudest at the time.

Where: which document and which measurement point hold the definition and the records

The fourth decision is about placement. The measurement definition belongs in the contract annex and in both parties’ monitoring-system configurations, not scattered across operations chat threads or a single email; concretely, it must state which layer the measurement point sits at — whether data is taken at the provider edge, a customer-side probe, or the end-to-end transaction entry; it must state the threshold for declaring unavailability, whether planned maintenance is excluded, and who approves an exclusion; and it must state where each of the three records is stored, how long it is kept, and whose time source it is synchronised to. ISO/IEC 19086-2:2018 lists “measurement conditions” as part of the measurement model, which is exactly why the same indicator yields different numbers at different measurement points. Here is a falsifiable judgement: an SLA that states only a percentage, with no measurement point and no exclusion rules, is for engineering purposes almost no SLA at all, because it cannot be independently reproduced — a different person following it would get a different result. Only by pinning the framing to a specific document and a specific measurement point can one speak of “reviewing it as written”; otherwise every compliance conclusion holds only for that one run, that one person, that one configuration.

How: turn the definition into four reviewable items

The fifth decision is how to put it into practice. The recommendation is to turn the measurement definition into four reviewable items, each entered in the annex. First, fix the indicator and the algorithm: whether availability is time-based or transaction-based, what the numerator and denominator are, how long the statistical window is, spell out the CSM collection framing and the SLO target value, and describe the quality characteristics that SQO captures. Second, unify the fields across the three records: align time source, sampling window, exclusion rules, retention period and responsible owner item by item, so that the same name does not carry different meanings. Third, pre-set the conflict rule: when provider monitoring, customer-side probing and third-party re-checking disagree, which prevails, in what order they are reviewed, and what triggers third-party involvement must all be written down in advance rather than negotiated once a divergence appears. Fourth, agree remedies and exceptions: ISO/IEC 19086-3:2017 lists remedies and exceptions among its core conformity requirements, so which situations count as exceptions, how remedy works and how evidence is kept must be defined together. The falsifiable claim: reviewability does not come from more expensive monitoring but from “a different person following the annex reaches the same conclusion” — that is the floor engineering acceptance should hold, and the only ground that stands up in a dispute.

Boundaries: this note does not interpret clauses and makes no legal characterisation

The boundary has to be stated clearly, otherwise a dossier written in engineering terms gets misread as a contract template or a legal opinion. This note discusses only measurement definitions and evidence-retention methods: how to break 99.9% down into a reproducible algorithm, how to make the three records comparable, and how to pre-set which record prevails in a dispute; it does not interpret the effect of any specific contract clause, makes no legal characterisation, and does not draft liability caps or compensation formulas for either side. ISO/IEC 19086-2:2018 explicitly does not mandate any one specific set of metrics, and ISO/IEC 19086-3:2017 explicitly does not prescribe a uniform SLA structure, which means the standards give a framework rather than a ready-made answer — forcing that framework into a single “standard wording” is a misuse. One falsifiable claim: all this note can settle is “whether measurement and evidence are reviewable”; whether a breach occurred and how liability is assigned belong to the contractual and legal layer and should not be answered by monitoring data on its own; treating engineering records directly as a legal conclusion crosses exactly the line this note deliberately holds.

Conclusion: fix the measurement definition and the record priority first, then talk about meeting the target

Pulling the six decisions together, the conclusion can be put as one executable order: fix the measurement definition, then fix the division of records and their priority, and only then talk about meeting the target. The reason the same 99.9% can lead to completely different conclusions is that underneath it sit two algorithms, time-based and transaction-based, three records each with its own framing, and an adjudication rule that was never agreed in advance; until those three things are settled, “met” is a word without a unit. The recommendation is therefore to do it in this order: at contracting time, write the indicator algorithm, measurement point, exclusion rules and the three records’ fields and priority into the annex, and freeze them into a version with an effective date; during operations, collect and judge only against that one version, route every change through an audit trail, so that each judgement traces back to the definition in force at the time. For all parties this is far less trouble than comparing whose monitoring is more complete after the fact: before any dispute arises the scoring rules are already written, and all that remains is to read the numbers as the rules prescribe.

Frequently asked questions

If two providers both state 99.9%, can their figures be compared directly?

No. The example in the body of ISO/IEC 19086-2:2018 is exactly this: one defines unavailability by time, the other by transactions, and the customer cannot compare them. First ask which algorithm availability uses, what the numerator and denominator are and how long the statistical window is; only after the definitions are aligned do the two percentages become comparable.

Provider monitoring, customer-side probing and third-party re-checking — which prevails in a dispute?

This note does not assign it for you, but argues that it must be written into an annex at contracting time. The three records each have their own time source, sampling window, exclusion rules and retention period; without a pre-set priority, a dispute drags from fact-finding into an argument over “whose data counts”. The standard only asks that roles and responsibilities be stated clearly and does not rank that order for you.

Is it not more flexible to leave the definition to be refined once a problem occurs?

Quite the opposite. A definition added after the fact inevitably tilts toward whoever benefits, and by then there is no neutral baseline. The measurement definition, sampling window, exclusion rules and record priority should be written down once in a calm period and frozen into a version with an effective date, with later adjustments routed through a change with an audit trail, so that each judgement traces back to the version in force at the time.
