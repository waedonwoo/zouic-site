5,000 Cards, RMB 430 Million: The Logic of Buying Changed

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

A utility bill states how many units you used this month and the price per unit; nobody prepays ten years of electricity. Compute procurement used to work the other way round: hoard a batch of cards, then think about how to use them. Weimob announced on 26 November plans to buy 5,000 compute cards in batches for about RMB 430.5 million, mainly for image and video generation. The most telling part of the announcement is not the total but the purpose: it says generation business, not a general training pool.

What does this bill actually buy?

RMB 430.5 million divided by 5,000 cards gives an average of about RMB 86,000 per card. That conversion only turns a large number into an imaginable scale; it cannot identify the model, which the announcement does not state. Buying in batches is also a signal: not filling up at once, but taking delivery at a pace. Put these two with the purpose and the picture is clear: the company is preparing inference capacity for image and video generation, a workload that follows customer demand and, unlike pre-training, cannot eat a whole pool in one run. How many cards to buy depends on how many you can keep busy, not how many you can obtain.

Why inference came first

Securities Daily counted more than ten listed companies since November disclosing compute procurement, leasing or service contracts, with inference-side demand the main driver. IDC and Inspur expect China's intelligent compute scale to grow 87.9 percent in 2026. These numbers say two sides of one thing: total demand is rising, and the rising part falls more on use than on training. Image and video generation turns a model into a service running every day, with a card behind each call. Unlike training, which can pause for tuning, a service stopping for a minute means a minute of lost use. For a board, such spending is easier to account for because it links directly to business volume.

Hoarding and pay-as-you-go have different billing cycles

Training cards follow invest-first, produce-later, with the risk that the model may never work; inference cards follow add-cards-when-demand-exists, with the bill following call volume. The former is buying a machine outright, the latter paying a monthly utility bill. Weimob buying rather than renting everything suggests it judges a stable base load in its generation business, worth carrying as a fixed cost. But the word batches leaves room: if volume falls short, later batches can slow down. Between ownership and on-demand, it found a middle position.

Who keeps an eye on this bill?

Capital markets are not uniformly enthusiastic. Chad Morganlander, senior portfolio manager at Washington Crossing Advisors, said on Yahoo Finance that investors should avoid new cloud service providers with poor balance sheets and confusing credit financing, and focus on high-quality stocks and the real economy; he said Nvidia, though a good company, contains a great deal of financial engineering, and related firms could come under pressure once the capital-spending wave slows. This is not saying AI is over; it puts the supplier's balance sheet into the review as well. As of 2026-11-28, what can be checked is the announced procurement intent, institutional forecasts and interview views; whether contracts land and cards arrive needs later disclosure. In a 2027 compute budget, a supplier's solvency and business capability belong in the same review.

Upstream has already cashed in

In contrast to downstream accounting by throughput stands upstream delivery data: Hesai's robot powertrain module deliveries nearly doubled quarter on quarter, and Leader Harmonious Drive logged about 400,000 sets of humanoid orders in the first three quarters. Both are company disclosures, arriving ahead of complete machines. That reminds buyers that industry prosperity does not transmit evenly: some links get paid first, others still wait for orders. Judging whether a company's compute purchase is real means looking beyond the announced amount to whether its business generates calls, and whether its suppliers have started delivering.

What is this bill most afraid of?

Most afraid of cards arriving while business volume does not. Depreciation on 5,000 cards does not stop because generation volume shrinks; idle cards burn money like an idle machine room. Buying in batches reduces that risk but cannot remove it. The real guardrail is the call-volume curve: how many minutes a day each card runs, how much content it generates, how much revenue that brings — three numbers that must line up. Record them, and the next batch needs no guesswork. Conversely, watching only the procurement amount makes it easy to read one expansion move as a long-term meal ticket.

Three accounts buyers should keep now

First, split by purpose: how much is for training, inference and backup, not one blended total. Second, pace: timings, delivery confirmations and acceptance records for each batch, all with timestamps. Third, unit economics: business volume per card per day and the revenue it corresponds to, calculated as finely as possible. None of these need wait for another announcement. They are your own accounts and come closer to the answer than any institutional forecast. Keep them well and you are qualified to decide whether the next batch is worth ordering.

The bill changed from stock to flow

From hoarding for training to paying by use for inference, what changed is not the card but the way it is paid for. You used to buy a possibility; now you buy a service that happens every day. Weimob spending RMB 430.5 million in batches tests whether it can build up image and video generation call volume; the contracts of more than ten listed companies test the same thing. The good thing about a utility bill is that it reconciles monthly; the bad thing is there is no credit. Compute procurement has finally learned to breathe with the business.
