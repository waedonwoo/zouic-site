A 3000 Dollar Robot Brain, and the OEM That Decided to Build Its Own

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

A domestic humanoid robot sells for 50,000 to 300,000 yuan, while a single NVIDIA Jetson Thor module costs about 3,000 dollars at volume and draws 40 to 130 watts. That 3,000 dollar figure is becoming a dividing line for robot makers. According to TMTPost, UBTech chairman Zhou Jian said publicly: “Many foreign chips are too expensive, taking up one third of hardware material costs.” When the whole machine sells for 300,000 yuan and one brain inside it eats a third of the bill, the arithmetic is hard to ignore. As of 24 November 2026, more makers are moving in house silicon from the options list to the must do list. If the chip can be bought, why build it yourself?

You Can Buy the Chip, but Not the Right to Define It

Start with what the chip does. The central control SoC in a humanoid robot is what engineers call the main brain; some designs put the brain and the cerebellum on one die, handling both decisions and real time joint motion. Buying a general module is like buying a ready made engine: it runs once installed, but the drawing for when to shift and how much throttle to give sits in the hands of the supplier. Building the chip yourself is about holding the pen that writes working conditions into silicon. This is not only about saving money. A module taking one third of hardware material costs means every machine sold pays a toll to the chip supplier. The difference between buying an engine and building one is not whether it runs, but who rewrites the drawing when the car breaks. Whoever holds the definition right decides whether the next generation of robots is fast in compute or steady in the joints.

The Delay Sits in Action Generation, Not in Compute

A study characterising VLA models across two generations of Jetson Orin and Thor platforms found a striking result: up to 75 percent of end to end latency is spent in the action generation stage, which is limited by memory bandwidth rather than by compute. Thor pushes compute far higher, yet the improvement in end to end latency is far smaller than the ratio of compute gain. A sports car with 300 on the speedometer stuck on a ring road at rush hour manages twenty: the peak on paper and the speed on the road are never the same thing. Robots need millisecond level real time response, while a general GPU architecture serves throughput first, not deterministic latency. Piling on compute does not make the hand of the robot move faster. That judgment sits directly beside the study data.

Thirty Joints Cannot Be Run on One Core

A humanoid robot carries twenty to thirty joints, and multi axis coordination needs millisecond precision. The MCU at each joint does two jobs at once: running motor control algorithms with control cycles measured in microseconds, and handling communication protocols that report state back to the main controller. Squeeze both onto one core and it is like a chef in a divided kitchen stir frying while taking orders; either the heat or the ticket slips. A single core design cannot solve that contention at the architecture level, and raising clock speed only buys a bigger pan on the same stove. A closer description is bespoke tailoring with synchronised definition: when the fabric lies at hand the stitches come tight; when it must be fetched from the storehouse even the best tailor misses the deadline. At the joint layer, the contest is never about peak parameters; it is about who builds real time behaviour into the architecture.

Four Routes Point to the Same Thing

According to TMTPost, the ways robot makers enter chips fall into four routes. Tesla goes furthest with full in house development: AI5 delivers 2,000 to 2,500 TOPS at about 250 watts, and Musk called it the only way to make enough Optimus units, with a target of one million a year. Dreame develops within its own ecosystem; the Sky series from Xinji Chuanyue was released and scaled into mass production in March 2026, using a heterogeneous mix of multi core CPU, dedicated NPU and a separate MCU. UBTech and Muxi set up a joint venture in May 2026, each holding 35.01 percent, with Muxi founder Chen Weiliang as chairman and UBTech founder Zhou Jian as vice chairman. AgiBot chose strategic investment, backing the Tsinghua linked RISC-V firm Xinji Wuxian as well as Huixi Intelligent. The cards in each hand differ, but the thing each must prove is the same: can the chip hold up in real working conditions?

Every Gigabyte Times One Million Machines Changes the Bill

One set of figures in the Tesla plan deserves a slow read. Musk cut the Optimus memory plan from 144GB to 72GB, then changed it back to 96GB. At one million units a year, every gigabyte added or removed, multiplied by a million, is a number large enough to break the cost structure. From 144GB to 72GB is a gap of 72GB, and going back to 96GB adds 24GB again. This is not engineers jumping back and forth in a lab; it is the volume ledger forcing the design to move. The memory money saved on one robot becomes the budget of a whole production line when multiplied by a million. Put the other way, whoever settles memory and real time behaviour inside the architecture once saves the whole round of revisions. The bill for the whole machine and the part only becomes legible at the scale of a million units.

Joint Ventures and Investments Are the Lighter Bets

Not every maker wants to fund a chip team of its own. Xi Xuan Chuang Zhi, the joint venture of UBTech and Muxi, closed a 500 million yuan angel round in mid November at a post money valuation of 4 billion yuan; its first chip is positioned as a brain and cerebellum on one SoC, benchmarked against NVIDIA Thor, with tape out planned for the second half of 2027 and volume production in 2028. On the AgiBot side, the first cerebellum chip from Xinji Wuxian has completed tape out and returned silicon, with a second generation taped out in 2027 and volume ramp also pointing to 2027 and 2028. AgiBot also invested strategically in Huixi Intelligent, which shipped about one fifth of the global high compute robot chip market in the first half of 2026, ranking second; AgiBot is both a strategic shareholder and its first customer at scale. Korean players are working together too: in September 2026 Mobilint teamed with LG Electronics, Doosan Robotics and Daewoo on an on device AI semiconductor project. The financials sit beside these plans: Muxi booked 2.824 billion yuan in revenue in the first three quarters of 2026, up 128.45 percent year on year, with research spending near 30 percent of revenue; UBTech reported 1.269 billion yuan in the first half, up 104.2 percent, sold 921 full size embodied robots and lost 339 million yuan in the period, while raising its Walker S shipment guidance from 2,000 to 3,000 units to 5,000. Chip investment is real money, and the profitability of robot makers is not yet thick enough to fund chip teams indefinitely.

Volume Production in 2028, but Rivals Will Not Wait

The schedule reaches far ahead, while rival moves are close at hand. NVIDIA launched the Jetson T3000 in July this year with 32GB of memory, 70 watts and 865 FP4 teraflops, alongside the T2000 with 16GB, 40 watts and 400 FP4 teraflops, both planned for availability in the first quarter of 2027. Domestic commercial options have not stood still either: D-Robotics has adapted its Xuri S600 to more than twenty robot makers, with the Xuri series shipping over eight million units in total, and Huixi R1 PRO completed six consecutive days and 17,625 production line operations on the AgiBot G2 with a 99.99 percent task success rate. On one side is tape out in 2027 and volume in 2028; on the other is eight million units already shipped and more than seventeen thousand operations already finished. Writing an in house schedule into a purchase decision made today is like inspecting a house against the drawing. Rivals will not wait for the chip to be finished.

Shipments Are the Final Judge

Bring the whole matter back to one judgment: robot makers cannot wait for NVIDIA because the cost and architecture mismatch of general chips becomes harder to bear at mass production. A brain and cerebellum on one die, heterogeneous MCU work at the joints, and a million unit ledger after memory amplification all make sense on paper. Yet the chip will not arrive until 2028, and in those two years neither NVIDIA nor domestic commercial chips will stand still. The right to define is worth fighting for, but it is ultimately redeemed by scale: tape out, returned silicon, validation and shipment, unit by unit handed over, is how the pen actually ends up in your hand.
