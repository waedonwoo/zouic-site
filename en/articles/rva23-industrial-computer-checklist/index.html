From a RVA23 Board to an Industrial Computer: What Is Still Missing

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

Bottom line: RVA23 chips are here, industrial computers are not. ERICI material makes this concrete: the first RVA23 machines are already eight-core 64-bit development and edge platforms with virtualisation extensions and an IOMMU, but a computer that survives a control cabinet, talks to the field and stays available for a decade or more must satisfy eight further conditions. The value of the checklist is separating chip compliance from machine usability so buyers can ask and sign row by row. A board that boots and a machine that lives ten years in the field are two very different purchases.

What this checklist covers and what it does not

It applies when you need to judge whether an RVA23 board can be delivered as an industrial computer, producing a nine-row table marked yes, no or not stated. It does not evaluate chip performance, predict when a product will close a gap, or replace EMC and safety certification. The ERICI reference conditions are: an RVA23 SoC with virtualisation extension and IOMMU, 24V DC wide-range input, minus 40 to 85 degrees Celsius extended temperature, EtherCAT/CAN/TSN fieldbus and real-time I/O, DIN-rail or panel mounting, hardware watchdog and isolated digital I/O, industrial EMC and safety certification, 10 to 15 year availability, and a partitioned stack running real-time alongside Linux. A claim worth rejecting is equating RVA23 compatible with industrial grade. The first is an ISA and profile baseline; the second is a set of machine-level engineering requirements.

Why a gap sits between a board and a machine

ERICI describes a standoff: board makers wait for software, software waits for boards, and customers wait for both. RVA23 was ratified in October 2024, making virtualisation and vector extensions mandatory so software can rely on them on every compliant chip; the first RVA23 SoCs and boards arrived in 2026. What is missing is not cores but the industrial hardware around them and the open partitioned stack above them. A claim worth rejecting is that software problems always solve themselves with time. Wide-range power, temperature, fieldbus, mounting, certification and long-term availability will not appear just because software matures; they must be written into requirements at selection time.

Who fills the table and who reviews it

Hardware rows belong to structural or electrical engineering, software rows to system and driver teams, certification and availability to procurement and compliance, and one reviewer checks that not stated has not been quietly turned into yes. ERICI lists two examples, Firefly AIBOX-K3 and Milk-V Jupiter2, both on SpacemiT K3 with eight X100 cores up to 2.4GHz and RVA23. Per manufacturer data cited by ERICI from October 2026, the first has 9 to 20V DC input and minus 20 to 60 degrees Celsius; the second has 12V or USB-PD, extended temperature not stated, and EtherCAT and CAN-FD via modules. Both show not stated for the partitioned stack. A claim worth rejecting is using example parameters to represent the whole RVA23 camp. Examples are starting points, not conclusions.

Profile dates and availability dates are different clocks

ERICI timeline: RISC-V began at UC Berkeley in 2010, the foundation formed in 2015, RISC-V International was based in Switzerland in 2020, virtualisation and vector extensions were ratified in 2021, RVA23 was ratified in 2024, and the first RVA23 boards appeared in 2026 when ERICI started. Specification citations use manufacturer data from October 2026; our baseline date is 19 November 2026. A claim worth rejecting is treating the 2024 profile ratification as a product maturity date. A profile solves the software baseline; machine usability adds hardware and certification time. The two timelines must be recorded separately.

Which document carries each piece of evidence

Wide voltage and temperature come from the datasheet, fieldbus from interface descriptions and module lists, mounting from mechanical drawings, watchdog and isolated I/O from schematics and board documentation, EMC and safety from certificate identifiers and scope, long-term availability from written commitments, and the partitioned stack from demos and release versions. ERICI states its stack was demonstrated on a SpacemiT K3 PicoITX board and is not yet on the two example products; it also states there is no affiliation or endorsement. A claim worth rejecting is treating a demo video as delivery evidence for the target product. A demo proves the stack can boot; it does not prove a specific enclosure has passed the item.

Six steps, each with evidence

First, list the nine conditions and allow only yes, no or not stated plus an evidence link, never basically supported. Second, check the RVA23 SoC, virtualisation extension and IOMMU first; if they fail, stop the table. Third, check power and temperature against actual site voltage and cabinet temperature, not just the nominal range. Fourth, check fieldbus and real-time I/O, whether onboard or via modules, and whether the module is included in the quote. Fifth, check mounting, watchdog, isolated I/O, EMC and safety certificates and 10 to 15 year availability commitments in writing. Sixth, check the partitioned stack: whether real-time and Linux run side by side on one SoC, on which board it was demonstrated, and how versions are maintained. A claim worth rejecting is skipping the fifth step because a board says RVA23. That step is what decides whether it survives in the field.

Exception handling and what the checklist does not do

Items not stated are recorded as not stated, with no inference or analogy from similar products. A vendor saying supported via a module must be recorded separately with whether that module is in the quote. Example parameters are not extended to the whole camp, and certificates must be checked for scope rather than applied across models. A claim worth rejecting is writing expected support into an empty cell to make the table look better. A blank can still be questioned; a guess can never be traced to a source. The checklist does not rank chips, predict when gaps close, or make compliance commitments for vendors. It only answers whether an item has checkable evidence.

What is missing is not cores but the ring around them

ERICI material offers a reusable judgement table: an open ISA addresses long-term dependency, RVA23 addresses the software baseline, and the remaining nine items address whether the machine can live in a cabinet for ten years. A claim worth rejecting is that open chips will automatically produce complete machines. In reality board makers, software and customers wait on each other, and this checklist is a tool to break that wait by making requirements explicit. For buyers the conclusion is plain: fill the table before looking at the logo.

Frequently asked questions

Is RVA23 compatibility the same as industrial grade?

No. RVA23 is an instruction-set profile baseline that guarantees virtualisation and vector extensions. Industrial grade additionally requires wide-range DC, extended temperature, fieldbus, mounting, watchdog, EMC certification and long-term availability. The two are not on the same layer.

Can example product specifications represent all RVA23 boards?

No. ERICI explicitly states that the Firefly AIBOX-K3 and Milk-V Jupiter2 are examples of manufacturer data published in October 2026, with no affiliation or endorsement. Several key items are marked not stated and cannot be generalised to the whole camp.

How should a not stated item be handled?

Record it as not stated and ask the supplier for written evidence. Do not infer or extrapolate from similar products. Especially for certification and long-term availability, only certificate identifiers and written commitments count; turning not stated into expected support destroys the traceability of the whole table.
