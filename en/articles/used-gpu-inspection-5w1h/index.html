Inspecting a used GPU: who signs, what gets tested, which evidence stays

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

Buyers and sellers in the used AI hardware market often discuss two different things: the seller quotes what fraction of the original price can be recovered, while the buyer calculates how much compute revenue the machine can still generate today. Those numbers can differ by multiples, and an inspection report actually answers a third question — whether the condition of this machine at this moment can be reviewed, and whether responsibility can be attributed when something fails. Conclusion first: fix the residual-value definition, then the inspection template, then write warranty transferability and leak liability into the contract. In the other order, running more benchmarks only tests an object nobody has defined.

What: inspection delivers a reviewable record of condition

Channel due-diligence lists are quite plain: verify provenance by asking for original purchase documents, especially for high-value H100 units, where a legitimate seller can show invoices naming authorised distribution; check warranty status, since some manufacturer warranties transfer with ownership and some do not, so verify before assuming; stress-test under a representative workload, because memory errors, thermal throttling and performance degradation do not show up in basic enumeration; confirm firmware and driver compatibility, since grey-market units may be blocked from firmware updates; and inspect physical condition — thermal paste ageing, fan wear, physical damage. Signing the results together with serial numbers, timestamps and the operator name produces the actual deliverable. A single nvidia-smi screenshot only proves the machine was recognised at that moment.

Why: inconsistent residual-value definitions, and liability costs that will not compress

Public numbers clash sharply. One channel guide says enterprise GPU resale typically recovers 60 to 80 percent of original purchase price depending on age, generation, supply and whether a whole server is sold; other market-tracking and accounting views show book residual value far below that after about three years; media analysis explains a generation whose used price exceeded launch price as a premium for delivery time and cites enterprise lead times of 36 to 52 weeks. These do not have to contradict each other — they measure resale price, accounting book value and availability premium respectively. Blending them into GPU holds value well or only twenty percent after three years misleads either way. Inspection matters precisely here: it moves the argument about what something is worth onto checkable facts about what this unit can actually do now.

Who: the person signing must be able to carry the responsibility

The chain includes the seller, a broker or IT asset disposition provider (channel guides put ITAD commissions at 15 to 25 percent for logistics, certified data destruction and buyer sourcing), certified refurbishers (a guide says reputable brokers run about 20 to 30 percent below certified refurbished units), grey-market sellers who may offer no warranty and may carry firmware or regional locks, the buyer operations team, and the manufacturer deciding whether warranty transfers. Each offers a different strength of promise: brokers usually give a limited warranty, the grey market essentially none. The signature on the inspection report should belong to the buyer operations team or its appointed third party, not the seller — otherwise the only record capable of proving a pre-existing memory error was produced by the interested party.

When: check at four moments

Before ordering, check provenance and warranty documents. On arrival, run the full inspection inside the agreed test window; trading advice for used liquid-cooled systems often mentions a 72-hour acceptance period. Before production, stress-test with a representative workload rather than merely powering on. After deployment, keep continuous health records so the delivery condition becomes a comparable baseline. Vendor-stated scale explains the density: one repair provider claims over 100,000 GPU servers under service, an annual failure rate generally between 3 and 5 percent and card values of 30,000 to 50,000 US dollars, arguing that repair speed directly affects what the asset is worth on the balance sheet (company statement, not independently verified). Even a modest failure rate means a meaningful absolute number across a batch, which is why evidence from the delivery moment matters so much.

Where: four layers of checks, do not stop at powering on

At the out-of-band layer, read FRU data with ipmitool fru, temperatures, fans and voltages with ipmitool sensor, and temperature alarms in the SEL log. At the system layer, verify configuration with dmidecode, lscpu, free and lsblk, and GPUs with nvidia-smi. At the stress layer, run a representative workload plus stress-ng and memtester while watching temperatures and error counters. At the liquid-loop layer, inspect coolant colour, hardened tubing, dripping quick disconnects, pump noise and O-ring condition, perform a pressure test, and confirm that leak detection ropes and sensors reach IPMI for remote alarms (channel advice for used liquid-cooled servers). Also check the actual hosting conditions: rack load limits, power and PDU specifications may not accommodate a high-density liquid-cooled GPU server. Those are site problems, but they decide whether the machine can be used at all.

How: turn inspection into a document you can present

Step one, require original purchase evidence and an authorised-distribution invoice, and record serial numbers and factory configuration. Step two, stress-test with a representative workload and keep the output, not just a screenshot showing successful enumeration. Step three, confirm that firmware and drivers can be updated — the restrictions common on the grey market show up here. Step four, sign all results together with timestamps, operator, firmware versions and serials into one inspection report, and put the acceptance window and leak liability into the contract. Liquid-cooled units additionally need coolant replacement records and pressure-test results. After four steps, a dispute can be answered with this is how the machine was tested on delivery and these were the results, rather than it looked fine at the time. That record is also the basis for warranty claims and for later resale pricing.

Boundaries: numbers you cannot use directly

The 60 to 80 percent recovery figure is a channel estimate, not transaction statistics; 20 to 30 percent and 15 to 25 percent are experiential ranges for channel price gaps and commissions; the 3 to 5 percent annual failure rate, units under service and spare-parts scale are repair-provider self-descriptions; 36 to 52 week lead times and used prices above launch price come from media analysis of a specific generation at a specific moment. They use different definitions and samples — do not average them, and do not put them into a valuation formula for one machine. Another common overreach is assuming liquid cooling always saves power: once pumps, fans and radiator efficiency degrade, actual draw need not be lower, and PUE still depends on where the meters sit. An inspection report can prove condition; it cannot prove market price.

Three rules even a small team can set today

One, write down which residual-value definition you use — resale transaction, book depreciation or immediate availability — and never mix them in one report. Two, fix the inspection template with provenance documents, out-of-band logs, configuration checks, stress-test output, firmware version, operator and time. Three, put the acceptance window, warranty transferability and leak liability in the contract. None of these requires a platform or extra tooling. In the compute aftermarket you can compress coordination cost but not liability cost; the inspection report is the piece of paper that fixes the liability cost up front.

Sources and qualifiers (baseline 11 November 2026): provenance checks, warranty transfer, representative-workload stress testing, firmware compatibility and physical inspection, together with 60–80 percent recovery, 20–30 percent price gaps and 15–25 percent ITAD commissions, come from a used AI hardware channel guide (channel estimates, not transaction statistics). The ipmitool, dmidecode, stress-ng and memtester steps, the 72-hour window and leak-liability clauses come from channel articles on used liquid-cooled servers (practical trade advice). The 3–5 percent annual failure rate, units in service and spare-parts inventory are a repair provider own statements (corporate claims, not independently verified). The 36–52 week lead times and the delivery-time premium come from media analysis tied to a specific model and moment. These figures use incompatible definitions and are listed side by side here, never averaged and never used for valuation.

Frequently asked questions

Why do published GPU residual values differ so widely?

They measure different things: resale price relative to original cost, accounting book value, or the premium for immediate availability. Name the measure first and the numbers become comparable.

Does a clean nvidia-smi output count as a completed inspection?

No. Basic enumeration will not reveal memory errors, thermal throttling or performance degradation. You need a representative workload, out-of-band log review and confirmation that firmware can be updated, all captured as a record.

What is most often missed on used liquid-cooled systems?

The leak action chain. Beyond coolant, tubing, quick disconnects and pressure testing, confirm that detection ropes and sensors reach IPMI for remote alarms and put leak liability into the contract.
