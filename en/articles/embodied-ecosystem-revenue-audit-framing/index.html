Every Robot Company Is Building an Ecosystem, but the Ledger Is Full of Data Acquisition Centers

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

Second Hand Rose have a famous line: "Bro, you play rock and roll, so what's the use of playing it?" Change one word and it lands right at the front of the 2026 embodied AI industry: "Bro, you build an ecosystem, so what's the use of building it?" In January Fourier held an ecosystem summit, in March Zibianliang held a developer conference, on the same day in April AgiBot and Zhongke Huiling kicked things off, in June DeepRobotics landed in Hangzhou, in October Unitree held a physical AI conference in Hangzhou and AgiBot launched Lingqu OS in Shanghai. Into November, the list keeps growing. Building an ecosystem has been welded from an option into the industry standard. And that is exactly the problem: when everyone builds an ecosystem, the word itself becomes something that has to be taken apart.

Why Build an Ecosystem at All

Embodied AI is stuck in an awkward place: technical demos run fast, commercialisation is strapped for cash. Look at a set of contrasting figures. In the first five months of 2026, China recorded 103 embodied AI contract awards, with research and education taking 70 percent; by order value and unit count, the biggest customer is the data acquisition center. By the end of the first half, more than 116 data acquisition centers were in operation, under construction or planned nationwide, using over 2,500 robots. Translate that: most robots are still learning in a driving school and have not hit the road. Under this structure, selling robots alone cannot carry the valuation. Machine makers are squeezed by three pressures at once: capital wants an order curve, customers want scenario solutions, developers want toolchains. The three pressures together force one shared move: use an ecosystem to organise demand that has not yet been proven.

An Ecosystem Is Not One Playbook but Four Businesses

Take the leaders apart and their ecosystem logics are completely different. Unitree turns the ecosystem into control over hardware interfaces: it gives away the robot platform for free, then sets the exam questions, handing logistics sorting, loading and unloading, and spray painting tasks to partners for development, with purchase discounts once they work. AgiBot turns it into an operating system tax: Lingqu OS claims end to end latency jitter under 4 microseconds at 1000Hz load, full open sourcing by year end, and the Yuansheng plan commits 2 billion yuan over five years to bind 80,000 developers and 511 companies. UBTECH goes for full stack supply chain coordination, building a ten thousand unit smart factory in Liuzhou, with customers such as BYD, Zeekr, Audi and Airbus, industrial users who demand acceptance standards. Dobot turns a decade of existing customers into an embodied testing ground, calling the production line data accumulated from 100,000 collaborative arms its data flywheel. Put the four side by side and the cards are on the table: after technology, grab the standard; after the standard, grab the ledger.

What Are the Ones Outside the Ecosystem Doing

There is another group of companies that hardly hold ecosystem conferences at all. The first are hard currency players who do not need an ecosystem: Leaderdrive, Moons Electric, Inovance and Orbbec, whose products are necessities in themselves. We do not define the standard; we supply the things the standard cannot bypass. The second are the integrated: Galaxea plugs into the Baige computing power of Baidu plus a data factory, and Youlu embeds an embodied large model into JD JoyAI, selling shovels to gold miners, not grabbing a position but eating certainty. The third are the lone wolves who choose not to build: Figure founder Brett Adcock puts it bluntly, "We do not need an ecosystem to sell robots; we need robots good enough that customers come to us." The fourth are the leftovers, who can afford neither a conference nor integration; their demos run but repeat purchases are hard to come by. The leaders use ecosystems to raise the barrier, and this tail end cannot even reach the ticket door.

What Is on the Back of the Ledger

The front is the ideal state; on the back there is a less presentable mechanism. According to Phoenix Weekly Finance, about 59 percent of the 1.4 billion yuan of large orders placed with UBTECH in 2025 came from data acquisition centers funded by local governments; data collection revenue at Leju climbed to 45 percent, with its largest customer being Shijingshan Chanfa, in which it holds a stake; several of the winning bids of AgiBot had purchasers that were its own equity participants, Hubei Optics Valley Dongzhi and Zhuhai Zhihui Yuanqi, with the data flowing back the other way. The pattern usually runs in four steps: local state capital funds a joint operating company, the data acquisition center buys tens to hundreds of robots from the joint venture, and the company then buys back the training data in the name of data services. Two real transactions on the books, and the money has made a circuit within one system. The industry calls this a closed loop with different objects: A sells robots, B buys data, the objects differ and the entities are separated, making it harder to audit than a traditional related-party transaction.

Two Ledgers Must Not Be Mixed

Buying equipment is beyond reproach in itself; a data acquisition center has to buy robots to be built. The equipment is a real asset, the joints really turn, and a related-party transaction is a valid transaction. What truly calls for caution is the two ledgers being mixed. One is the equipment ledger, calculated by depreciation, utilisation and operating costs; the other is the data ledger, calculated by useful data hours, cross device reuse value and the price outside buyers are willing to pay. Run only the equipment ledger and not the data ledger and a loop appears that looks beautiful but hangs in the air: equipment sales confirm revenue, data purchases confirm turnover, local projects confirm investment, yet the outside demand that would ultimately pay for the data never shows up. Conflating supply security with cost reduction is a sleight of hand; conflating the equipment ledger with the data ledger is the upgraded version of the same trick.

Regulators Have Caught Wind of It

In August the NDRC named the risk of blind imitation and a rush to pile in; in September reports said the CSRC issued window guidance to investment banks, requiring companies planning to list to prove recurring revenue or genuine technological innovation; and Mech-Mind founder Shao Tianlan directly blasted revenue assembled for the occasion. The broader order structure confirms it: in that first half of 2026 round of awards, research and education took 70 percent, data acquisition centers were the bulk, and genuine routine industrial use was rare. The prospectus of Unitree itself states that it does not fully know the specific use scenarios of its customers and that its advantage rests on price performance rather than irreplaceability. This is not the problem of one company but an industry wide one. Capital wants orders, local governments want an investment calling card, and companies want pre listing revenue quality; all three take what they need and the books look clean, but not one has answered a single question: take away the cameras and the demonstration scenarios, can this robot run continuously in a factory for three months without stopping?

Four Variables to Test What Is Real

To judge whether the ecosystem of a company is real, do not listen to the launch event; look at four variables only. First, the share of independent third party revenue, what remains after excluding shareholders, state capital and data acquisition joint ventures. Second, the non-related repeat purchase rate, whether outside customers buy again in the second year after the first order. Third, cash collection six months after acceptance, not revenue recognised on shipment but money actually arriving. Fourth, external pricing of data, whether any unfamiliar customer is willing to pay real money for your data. Once these four numbers are run, the tide goes out and it is obvious who was swimming naked. As of 27 November 2026, what can be checked is the structure of contract awards, the scale of data acquisition centers and the order shares tallied by the media; the four variables above have not been publicly disclosed. Investors have already moved first: they no longer look at demos when reviewing a project, they look at warehouses and at customer sites, and companies that pass both can be counted on fewer than ten fingers in this sector.

Waiting for the Day the Cameras Go Off

Back to that opening line. "Bro, you build an ecosystem, so what's the use of building it?" Before product market fit is proven, it is a ventilator that lets the industry last a little longer. After product market fit is proven, it is the watershed that decides who holds the operating system, who holds the materials and actuators, and who holds the real production line. 2026 is not the first year of commercialisation; it is the first year of elimination. Only on the day robots can truly switch off the cameras and reliably replace people on a night shift line does the ecosystem become useful. Until then, the livelier the conferences, the closer you must watch the ledger. Four variables laid out on the table shine brighter than the lights at any ecosystem launch.

Frequently asked questions

Are orders from data acquisition centers fake revenue?

It cannot be judged across the board. A data acquisition center has to buy robots to be built, the equipment is a real asset, and a related-party transaction is a valid transaction. What calls for caution is mixing the equipment ledger with the data ledger: equipment confirms revenue, data confirms turnover, but outside demand willing to pay for the data never appears. Judge by the four variables rather than by labelling the orders.

What are the four acceptance variables?

The share of independent third party revenue, what remains after excluding shareholders, state capital and data acquisition joint ventures; the non-related repeat purchase rate, whether outside customers buy again in the second year after the first order; cash collection six months after acceptance, money actually arriving rather than revenue recognised on shipment; and external pricing of data, whether any unfamiliar customer pays real money for the data.

Is building an ecosystem a problem in itself?

No. At a stage when technical demos run fast and commercialisation lags, an ecosystem can organise the supply chain, developers, data and scenario partners first; it is a normal mechanism in the early phase of an industry. The problem is treating the ecosystem as the destination. When end demand fails to arrive over a long period, the buffer slowly turns sour.
