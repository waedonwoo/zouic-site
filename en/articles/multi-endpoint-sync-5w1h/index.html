The main site changed its framing: when the other ends follow and how lag is declared

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

Changing one line of framing on a site — say, writing “list price is not a quote from this site” into the services page — looks like a template edit. But more than that page speaks outward: the trilingual translations have to change with it, the scope and baseline date in the machine-readable list have to be refreshed, sitemap and structured data are derived from the export, the mini program is another codebase with another release process, and machine readers already walked off with old copies of the pages. These ends do not take effect at the same time, and are not even triggered by the same operation. The 5W1H breakdown follows. Conclusion first: write an impact list for a framing change, state for each end how it is triggered, who owns it and how lag is declared; not yet followed must be marked explicitly, not left silently on the old framing.

What: one change touches at least six places

Take the real chain inside the site: the source templates and multilingual dictionaries are the origin; a full export freezes the trilingual pages, section pages, sitemap shards, robots and machine-readable text into static artefacts; a commit pushed to the build repository triggers a Pages build; the incremental article export covers only article pages, so the machine-readable text needs a separate refresh, and that refresh must carry the monitoring address from the runtime config or a whole block of observation notes is dropped; the mini program lives in its own directory with its own version and platform release process; and finally external caches and stale copies held by machine readers. The six take effect in different ways: some are derived automatically by the export, some must be triggered by hand, some wait for platform distribution, and some are not under site control at all. Calling all of them publishing causes the parts that do not follow automatically to be missed, and the missed parts are often precisely the texts that make the clearest outward promises.

Why: silent lag is far more dangerous than declared lag

Users understand that ends differ in time: mini program review takes time, the CDN has a cache, third parties still hold old snapshots. What users cannot accept is the site itself not having said clearly which end is the old one. One concrete risk is the machine-readable text: the page has moved to the new framing while the list still carries the old figures and baseline date, so a machine reader gets a self-contradictory description. Another risk is translation: Chinese changed, the EN/DE dictionaries were not filled in, so the page either exposes Chinese or falls back to an old translation, and the reader sees a third framing. The corresponding practice in the project is to refresh the trilingual machine-readable text separately and confirm the diff leaves only the count and baseline date lines, and to scan all templates for zero missing keys — the point of these checks is not to make all ends consistent instantly but to make inconsistency immediately visible. Declaring lag explicitly is, in essence, turning temporarily different into a recorded state rather than an unclaimed blank.

Who: the trigger, the followers and the endorser are not the same person

The person changing the framing owns a clear impact statement: whether this change is fact, price, licensing or visual, and which ends reference it. Export and build scripts are automatic followers and handle only what is wired into the flow; any manually generated separately step needs someone to trigger it explicitly. The platform is the distributor: Pages builds on push, and GitHub Docs also notes that DNS propagation for a custom domain can take a long time; the mini program follows the current platform release process, and the site cannot assume push means live. The endorser is the release record: it states who confirmed this change, which ends have followed and which are marked as lagging. The most easily overlooked parties are machine readers and search platforms, which read neither in-site release notes nor come back to check on their own; all the site can do is write the baseline date, version number and scope into outward-facing text so old copies carry “which day version this is” by themselves.

When: three sync rhythms, written into one table

The first is same-batch sync: templates, trilingual dictionaries, structured data and sitemap are produced by one and the same export and must align in this release, never left for the next one. The second is declarable lag: ends with an independent release process, such as the mini program, may follow in the next version, but the change record must state pending follow-up, the target version and the owner, and the site side must use wording that does not mislead users. The third is uncontrollable lag: CDN caches, external crawls and stale copies held by machine readers, where the site can only wait and mark with a baseline date and version number, and cannot promise a purge. The three rhythms have to be distinguished, or uncontrollable lag gets investigated again and again as an incident while a translation that belonged to the same batch slips to the next release. Real practice in the project follows this layering too: after the source is final, one full re-export, confirm the snapshot matches the source before pushing; after the push leave a deployment window, then re-verify; and refresh the machine-readable text separately and check the diff.

Where: the impact list lives in one place, the status lives at each end

Keep a framing-change impact list in the project conventions with six columns: end, content carried, trigger, whether same-batch, lag-declaration template, owner. Trilingual pages and structured data are marked same-batch, machine-readable text separate trigger and needs config, sitemap derived by export, mini program independent release process, and external caches and machine readers mark baseline date only, no purge promised. Status lives at each end itself: pages carry a check date and baseline date, the machine-readable text states scope and baseline date in its file header, the mini program notes the main site version it followed in its version record, and the release record aggregates the status of all ends. Keeping list and status apart stops which ends should follow drifting with human memory while how far it has got can be read from each end itself. That also means no centralised sync system is needed; one table plus a few checks is enough.

How: turn a framing change into a six-step closed loop

Step one, classify the change: fact, price, licensing, visual or machine-readable scope, each with a different impact, and price and licensing ones must cover every end. Step two, list the ends to follow from the impact list, marking which are same-batch and which may lag. Step three, change the source side in one pass: templates, trilingual dictionaries, structured data, the framing note and the baseline date, and run a missing-key scan over all templates. Step four, check the artefacts after export: trilingual pages exist, the machine-readable text was refreshed separately with the expected diff, the sitemap has no duplicates or dead links, and no paragraph of config injection was dropped. Step five, after pushing wait for the deployment window, run the three-source self-check and do a cross-source word-for-word comparison; write the followed and pending ends into the release record. Step six, re-check and close the lagging ends at the next release, keeping the baseline date and version note from that moment. The key to the six steps is the words “pending follow-up” in step five: better to write them out than let an old framing keep speaking outward with nobody owning it.

Boundaries: three kinds of lag this list cannot govern

First, platform-side time: build queues, review and DNS propagation are not under site control, and documentation gives only possible time ranges, not a promise; the concrete process and duration follow the current platform documentation. Second, external copies: search engines, AI clients and third-party indexes hold the versions they fetched themselves, and the site can neither force a refresh nor claim on that basis that the whole web is synced; it can only let old copies prove their own date via baseline date and version number. Third, the accuracy of the content itself: framing being consistent across ends does not mean the framing is correct, since fact-checking, licensing basis and the nature of third-party sources are a separate line. In addition, the content of artefacts in a public repository is itself public and deleted files remain in version history, so a framing change should also take care not to carry internal paths, keys or debug information into the artefacts. This note is about whether the same sentence is consistent across ends, not whether the two ends align on feature scope.

Conclusion: better to write the lag down than differ silently

The real goal of multi-endpoint sync is not every end saying the same sentence in the same second, but being able to answer at any moment which end speaks which version, who is responsible for catching up and when it is expected to. Fixing the impact list, the three sync rhythms, the baseline date and version note, and the pending follow-up status in the release record covers most of the risk with a very light mechanism. The seemingly fussy checks in the project — zero missing keys in the trilingual dictionaries, a machine-readable diff reduced to counts and baseline date, pushing only once source and snapshot agree — are all doing the same thing: turning inconsistency into a visible item. Cross-end consistency is not a one-off victory of complete alignment but the habit of being willing to write the lag down with every change.

Sources and framing (as of 13 November 2026): that Flask source generates build artefacts through a full export, that a commit pushed to the build repository triggers Pages, that the incremental article export covers only article pages, that the machine-readable text needs a separate refresh which must carry the monitoring address from the runtime config or a paragraph is dropped, that the post-refresh diff leaves only counts and baseline date, the all-template missing-key scan, pushing only once source and snapshot agree, the three-source self-check and cross-source word-for-word comparison after the deployment window, and the mini program being a separate code directory all come from ZouIC site-internal engineering records and script implementations and count as site-internal framing. That DNS propagation for a custom domain may take a long time and that Pages repository content is public with deleted files remaining in version history come from the official GitHub Docs and count as official platform framing. Third-party experience compilations on static-hosting distribution delay and multi-endpoint publishing are cited only directionally and are not written into the conclusion; platform process durations follow the current official documentation and the site makes no promise.

Frequently asked questions

I changed one line of Chinese copy — why does the machine-readable text also need a separate refresh?

Because the incremental export only covers article pages, so the counts, scope note and baseline date in the machine-readable list are not recomputed along with it. The project refreshes the trilingual versions separately and checks the diff; the refresh also has to carry the monitoring address from the runtime config, or a whole block of observation notes is left out.

The mini program has not caught up yet — should the new framing be hidden on the main site?

No, but it cannot stay silent either. Mark it as pending follow-up in the impact list with a target version and an owner, and let each end carry its own baseline date and version note. Users accept a time gap between ends; what they do not accept is the site itself being unable to say which end is the old one.

Can search engines and AI clients be required to update to the new framing immediately?

No. External copies are governed by the other side crawling and caching; the site can neither force a refresh nor claim the whole web is synced. What is controllable is stating the baseline date, version number and scope in outward-facing text so old copies carry their own timestamp, and treating this kind of lag as a separate, uncontrollable category.
