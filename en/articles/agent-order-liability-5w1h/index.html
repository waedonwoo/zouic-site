When an agent buys the wrong thing, who pays: the gap in authorisation and chargeback rules

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

The hardest question in agentic payments is not whether the money was debited but whether that debit counts as user authorisation. When a user delegates the decision, one semantic error can buy the wrong product: the cardholder really did complete biometric authentication, the merchant really did ship the order, and the agent developer can say it only executed an instruction. Existing card-network dispute, chargeback and retrieval rules assume the transaction was initiated by a human and have no slot for this error. Conclusion first: until rules catch up, what you can do is not decide who pays but preserve the authorisation scope, the original intent and the confirmation action as a record you can produce.

What: an error where nobody broke a rule and nobody absorbs the loss

A law firm published a hypothetical case: a user tells an agent to buy a CNY 1,999 skincare set as soon as it appears in a livestream; the agent buys a CNY 1,999 custom blind box marked non-returnable once opened and completes the debit. The cardholder completed biometric authorisation, so it cannot be treated as impersonation fraud; the merchant shipped the order with the rules stated in advance and is not at fault, so it cannot be charged back as a merchant dispute; the error came from semantic interpretation by an AI platform that is not part of the clearing rules, so the issuer cannot demand compensation from it. Three compliant parties, loss with nowhere to land. This is not wordplay: interviews with e-commerce companies running AI payment agents report exactly the worries about buying the wrong item, buying too much and duplicate orders, and about enterprises, developers and users each telling a different story once a customer complains.

Why: legacy risk controls model people, while the new error comes from semantics

Traditional risk control profiles human behaviour: IP address, country and spending habits form the basis of detection, while transactions between agents do not follow those patterns, leaving existing models poorly fitted (industry views reported by Economic Information Daily in September 2026). The nature of the error changed too. Fraud is identity misuse; a wrong order has a genuine identity and a completed authorisation form, with the deviation occurring between the original intent and the final business action. In the same reporting, an Ant Group risk-control lead described two capabilities that are needed: more native identity verification on ambient devices such as glasses, earphones and watches, and continuous validation of user intent and authorisation before and after the transaction, reconstructing multi-level delegation chains and extending protection to pre-constraints, in-event decisions and post-event tracing. A single one-time authorisation cannot cover a multi-step delegation.

Who: six parties, and the responsibility documents have only just started

The chain includes the user, agent developer, merchant, issuer, clearing network and payment institution. In August 2026, under the guidance of the People’s Bank of China, the Payment and Clearing Association of China issued a self-regulatory convention on agentic payment applications. It asks members to explore a know-your-agent mechanism on top of know-your-customer, to define authorisation boundaries clearly and to sign authorisation agreements with users that allocate responsibility clearly; core functions such as account management, transaction processing and clearing must be performed by licensed institutions, under the principle that whoever provides the payment service bears primary responsibility (the convention is relayed through media and legal-firm summaries). Two group standards on agent identity authentication and authorisation frameworks, and on runtime security requirements, were launched by a Chinese cybersecurity association in the same period. Note the status: these are self-regulatory documents and standards under development, not statutes or case law, and cannot by themselves decide an individual dispute.

When: during the rule gap, merchants are already using a clumsy workaround

The timeline: the convention was published in August 2026; in September 2026 Economic Information Daily reported that unclear liability is one of the core pain points of agentic payment and that such disputes have already occurred in practice; the two group standards are at launch stage with no citable technical specification yet. The practical workaround is unglamorous: one e-commerce company, on learning that an order came from an agent, has customer service call the user to confirm before shipping, which users dislike. The same interviews describe measures being added — budget controls and second confirmation at authorisation, a user-confirm-order reminder at order intake, and transaction logs plus risk signals for tracing. The executable stop-loss measures today are therefore process and evidence, not waiting for a unified liability standard.

Where: one gate at each of three stages

At authorisation, record quota ceilings, allowed categories, validity window, whether confirmation-free payment is permitted, and the original instruction text. At order intake, recognise that the order came from an agent and trigger user confirmation for high-dispute categories. At tracing, reconstruct the chain from original instruction to parsed result to selected SKU to amount to confirmation action, instead of keeping only a payment-success record. Legal analysis adds another gap: the red line against outsourcing core risk control has no clear answer when an issuer delegates transaction authorisation decisions to an external large model. So the boundary of your own system also needs writing down: which judgements an external model makes and which must stay in your own rules engine determines whether the decision path can be explained afterwards.

How: make explainability a system requirement

Step one, write authorisation as a structured object rather than one sentence saying automatic ordering is allowed: quota, category, time window, per-transaction ceiling and whether splitting is permitted, each individually presentable. Step two, force second confirmation for high-dispute categories such as custom goods, non-returnable items, pre-orders and virtual goods; sacrificing some conversion is cheaper than post-hoc argument. Step three, define a log format retained long term: original instruction, model parsing output, candidate SKU list, final selection, confirmation action, timestamp and version — a missing element becomes another each-tells-a-different-story dispute. Step four, run automatic payment as a small category whitelist and let the real error rate decide whether to expand. None of these steps makes you the rule maker; they make you the one party in the chain holding a complete record.

Boundaries: what these materials cannot establish

A domestic self-regulatory convention and international card-network dispute rules are two different systems and cannot be applied to each other. The convention is an industry document, not law, and corporate practices in press interviews are not regulatory requirements. The CNY 1,999 blind-box case is a hypothetical used by a law firm to illustrate a institutional gap, not an effective judgment, and must not be reported as a court finding nobody is liable. Reluctance to authorise and merchants calling to confirm come from individual company interviews, not an industry baseline. One more confusion to avoid: completed biometric authentication only means the authentication step passed; it does not place the agent interpretation inside the authorised scope, and it does not follow that the merchant must compensate either. Leave who pays to rule makers and make your own evidence chain complete.

Three things even a one-person company can put in place

First, write the authorisation boundary into a presentable structured record that users can review to see what they agreed to. Second, add a confirmation gate on the merchant side and never allow confirmation-free payment for high-dispute categories, spending the cost up front rather than in after-sales. Third, define the log format before connecting payment, keeping the original input and parsed output for every decision a model touches. None of these waits for a standard or requires platform cooperation. The real threshold for agentic payment is not giving agents more autonomy but drawing a provable boundary between efficiency and control — during a rule gap, the party that can prove its own actions is the least likely to be pulled into a dispute.

Sources and qualifiers (baseline 11 November 2026): the self-regulatory convention on agentic payment applications was issued by the Payment and Clearing Association of China in August 2026; its contents are relayed through reporting by Economic Information Daily on 19–20 September 2026 and a law-firm study. The statements about unclear liability, disputes already occurring, the poor fit of traditional risk controls and the two capabilities described by the Ant Group risk-control lead come from that reporting and represent industry and corporate positions. User reluctance to authorise, merchants calling to confirm, and budget controls plus second confirmation come from interviews published by Nanfang Plus and JW View (individual media cases). The CNY 1,999 blind-box example and the chargeback-rule gap come from a published study by a Guangdong law firm (third-party legal analysis using a hypothetical, not a judgment). The group standards are at launch stage with no citable formal text seen.

Frequently asked questions

Does completed biometric authentication mean the user approved this specific transaction?

Authentication passing does not mean the agent semantic interpretation stayed inside the authorised scope. The difficulty is precisely that the case can neither be handled as impersonation fraud nor easily charged back as a merchant dispute; existing rules have no slot for this kind of error.

Is a merchant calling the customer to confirm a step backwards?

It is a transitional practice while liability rules remain unclear. It does cost experience, but for non-returnable, high-dispute categories it is cheaper than arguing after the fact. The better direction is a structured confirmation gate rather than a fully manual one.

What should a small team add first when accepting agentic payments?

Limit categories and quotas first, then define the log format. You need to be able to reconstruct the original instruction, the parsed result, the selected SKU and the confirmation action; without that chain, any later claim is only an oral account.
