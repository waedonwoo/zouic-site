HBM4 will not be won in DRAM, but in who builds the logic base die

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

All three memory makers reached HBM4 volume production in 2026, yet their spec sheets look less and less like the same race. The real fork: HBM4 turns the logic base die into a customisable component for the first time, and whoever controls it controls the interface of the next accelerator generation.

Pin down the standard first

JEDEC published JESD270-4 in April 2025: interface width doubles from HBM3E's 1024-bit to 2048-bit, independent channels rise from 16 to 32, and the baseline 8 Gb/s per pin gives 2 TB/s per stack. It supports 4/8/12/16-high stacks up to 64 GB per stack, and relaxes the package height limit from 720 µm to 775 µm. Against about 1.2 TB/s for HBM3E this is a structural doubling, not a frequency step.

But the standard is only the floor. All three vendors' volume parts run above 10 Gb/s, already leaving the JEDEC baseline behind.

Three technology paths, three bets

Samsung's approach is the heaviest: it is the only supplier that completes DRAM, base-die fabrication and 3D packaging inside its own fabs, and it is pushing hybrid bonding and 1c DRAM (Korean press puts 1c yield near 60%). The price is having to solve every step itself. SK hynix handed the base die to TSMC and got a more advanced logic process and a shorter development cycle — but that lets a foundry which makes no HBM hold the chip in the HBM4 stack closest to the customer's custom requirements.

This is not about right or wrong, it is about who holds the bargaining power. Once customers start asking for an HBM base die tailored to their accelerator, whoever supplies that process stops being a vendor and becomes a co-designer. TSMC's role changes in this round, and NVIDIA's 2026 discussions with Meta, Samsung and SK hynix about putting GPU core logic into HBM follow the same logic.

After bandwidth doubles, where is the bottleneck

During his 2026 Korea visit Jensen Huang confirmed all three suppliers had qualified for HBM4 production alongside Vera Rubin, with first systems planned for Q3 2026; press figures put a single Vera Rubin NVL72 rack at about 20.7 TB of HBM4 and roughly 1.6 PB/s aggregate bandwidth. Samsung and SK hynix shipped 12-high HBM4E samples in May and June 2026 — the generational cadence has not slowed.

But a 16-high stack needs each DRAM die thinned to about 30 µm while staying inside the 775 µm total height limit; thermal resistance inside a TSV stack rises with layer count, and hybrid bonding (copper-to-copper, under 1 µm pitch) is generally expected to reach volume only at HBM4E or HBM5. SK hynix's own technical notes point at heat dissipation at 16–20 layers. In short, HBM4 solved bandwidth and handed yield, thermals and advanced packaging to the next runner. That is why CoWoS capacity simultaneously becomes an industry-wide allocation problem.

Bottom line

What is worth remembering about HBM4 is not 2 TB/s but the reshuffling of the division of labour: memory makers no longer only sell DRAM dies, accelerator makers no longer only buy standard stacks, and foundries have entered the HBM value chain for the first time. For procurement and investment, track who wins custom base-die orders, the yield curve on 16-high stacks, and whether HBM4 and CoWoS capacity are released together — if those three drift apart, paper bandwidth never reaches the rack.

Frequently asked questions

How much faster is HBM4 than HBM3E?

At the JEDEC baseline the interface doubles from 1024-bit to 2048-bit at 2 TB/s per stack, against about 1.2 TB/s for HBM3E. Volume parts run faster: Samsung's 11.7 Gb/s corresponds to about 3.3 TB/s, and Micron's 12-high 36 GB exceeds 2.8 TB/s.

Are all three vendors in volume production?

During a 2026 visit to Korea, Jensen Huang confirmed Samsung, SK hynix and Micron had all qualified for HBM4 production for Vera Rubin. Samsung began commercial shipments in February 2026, Micron ramps from Q2 2026, and SK hynix ramps through 2026.

Will HBM4 fix memory price increases?

Not in the short term. HBM4 uses more wafer and advanced packaging resources per bit, and 16-high stack yield and thermals remain constraints. Relief depends on new HBM capacity arriving alongside CoWoS capacity.
