What an AI-visit observation dashboard should record, and what it should not

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

The site added an observation endpoint to its machine-readable manifest to log visits from AI clients. It sounds like nothing more than adding a log line, yet what actually has to be decided is a chain of boundaries: which fields to record, by which framing to classify them, how long to keep them, and — most important of all — which conclusions must never be drawn from this log. The Cloudflare developer documentation states the classification dimensions clearly: AI traffic is divided by behaviour into Search, Agent and Training, and a single bot can exhibit several behaviours at once; the panel shows crawler name and operator, category, allowed and unsuccessful request counts, robots.txt violations, and the action taken for each crawler. Note those column names: every one of them describes what happened to a request, and not one is called “purpose”. The 5W1H breakdown follows. Conclusion first: record the facts at the moment of delivery, record no person, and do not pretend to know what the content was later used for.

What: the observed object is one request, not one use

The facts a single visit can record are roughly these: time, request path, method, status code, response byte count or whether a cache was hit, the User-agent string, a classification label, and whether the declaration files published by the site were requested. All of these sit at the HTTP delivery layer. Whether the content, once it has left the server, is indexed, turned into a summary, placed in a training set or discarded is something the origin cannot see. The wording of the official Cloudflare documentation keeps to this line as well: it defines Robots.txt violations as the number of times robots.txt was violated and splits Requests into allowed and unsuccessful, noting that unsuccessful requests can stem from any rule or response error and not only from blocking actions; its Attribution Business Insights panel reports a crawl-to-referral ratio, which is still a traffic-level proportion and not proof of purpose. Writing “a crawler visited us” as “our content was used for training” silently swaps the observed object. The only judgement the observatory can support is one sentence: an identifier requested an address at a given moment, and the site returned such-and-such.

Why: logging too little and logging too much both break the log

Log too little and the log loses its value for post-mortems: when something breaks you cannot answer “did this start only after that change” or “which class of client was affected”, nor can you line up the publication of a declaration file with later requests in time. Log too much and you immediately take on responsibilities unrelated to the purpose of observation: full raw IPs, request bodies, authentication headers, session identifiers and query parameters from referrer URLs barely help decide “did the machine read the declaration”, yet they markedly enlarge the body of data that has to be protected. Third-party compliance compilations commonly recommend data minimisation, a retention period proportionate to the collection purpose, and pseudonymisation of address-type fields in logs — these are valuable engineering directions, but they belong to third-party compilations and law-firm articles, not to the original text of a regulator, and are cited here only as directional references, without any legal characterisation. What really drives the trade-off is the purpose itself: if the observatory exists to check whether the declaration was read and whether the site returned what was expected, the fields it needs are far fewer than those of a full access log.

Who: classifying, recording and interpreting belong to three different parties

The crawler operator decides how the User-agent string is labelled, whether it identifies itself publicly, and whether one identifier covers several behaviours; edge platforms and directory services decide the classification labels, and the Cloudflare documentation states explicitly that it classifies by behaviour rather than by a single “AI bot” label, that one bot can have several behaviours, and that its BotBase directory offers a publicly searchable classification by behaviour; the site decides what it records, how it displays it and how long it keeps it, but the site’s own classification is only its own framing and must not be presented externally as an official platform determination. What a reader or partner most needs when looking at observation data is a framing note: was this label assigned by the site itself, on which version of the roster is it based, and how are unidentified identifiers classified. When the three parties’ framings disagree, the honest approach is to set “self-reported identifier”, “site classification” and “reference directory” side by side and keep an unidentified category, rather than forcing the unknown into a known class to make the report look tidy.

When: declaration release, requests and policy changes are three separate timelines

The first is declaration release time: when robots.txt and the machine-readable manifest were changed and to which commit hash they belong, because clients may cache the files under the specification and old behaviour seen for a while after a change is not a regression. The second is request time: precise enough to line up with the release record is sufficient, with no need for unlimited high-frequency detail. The third is platform policy time: the official Cloudflare changelog states that from July 2026 the three behaviours can be controlled separately, and that from 15 September 2026 newly onboarded domains use a new default policy that blocks Training and Agent traffic on pages with ads while Search remains allowed; the effective date of such a default turns “visits suddenly dropped” into a policy outcome rather than a problem with the site’s content. If observation records are not aligned with these three timelines, it is easy to blend stale behaviour inside the cache period, a platform default policy change and the site’s own edit into a single “traffic anomaly”. The retention period should also be set according to the observation purpose, stated in the notes and cleaned up on expiry, instead of “store everything now and decide later”.

Where: fields stay in the log, framing in the notes, conclusions elsewhere

The raw record keeps only the necessary fields: timestamp, path, status code, the User-agent verbatim or a normalised identifier, the site’s own classification, whether a declaration file was requested, and one aggregate statistics key containing no personal information. If address-type fields are genuinely needed for troubleshooting, they should be pseudonymised or aggregated at write time, and never stored together with request bodies, cookies or authentication headers. The classification basis, roster version, rule for handling unidentified identifiers, retention period and the statement that this observation does not prove purpose belong on the observation endpoint’s description page and in the machine-readable text, so readers see the framing before the numbers. The display layer offers only aggregate views: request trends by category, how often declaration files were read, the distribution of error codes, with no drill-down to an individual visitor. As for conclusions of the kind “where was the content used”, those belong in contracts, platform relationships and after-the-fact reconciliation, not in the log. Only with the three layers separated does a report that looks thorough but actually oversteps its bounds become impossible.

How: write the boundaries as six enforceable rules

First, a field allowlist: list the fields that are necessary to verify the declaration, collect nothing else, and require a written reason for any new field. Second, split identifiers three ways: store the self-reported User-agent, the site’s own classification and the reference-directory classification separately, list unidentified ones on their own, and do not guess. Third, read only the declaration files and necessary metadata; do not capture request bodies, store authentication information or record sessions. Fourth, pseudonymise or aggregate address-type information as it is written, set a retention period proportionate to the observation purpose, purge automatically on expiry and keep a record of the purge. Fifth, write the three moments — declaration file publication, policy effective date and site change — into the same observation note so trends are not misread. Sixth, output only aggregate results plus a framing note, and attach to every report the sentence “this record describes requests, not the purpose of content”. Make all six into configuration and assertions rather than relying on the diligence of whoever is on call; among them, “list unidentified separately” matters most, because it decides whether the unknown share of a report can be quietly smoothed away.

Boundaries: four kinds of question the observatory cannot answer

First, purpose: the HTTP layer cannot see where content goes after it is taken, and both declaration and log cover only the moment of delivery. Second, authenticity of identity: a User-agent is a self-reported string, and even signature-based mechanisms prove which key signed something rather than granting authorisation; this note does not treat classification as identity verification. Third, compliance judgement: “robots.txt violations” in the panel is a statistic under the platform’s framing, and the platform documentation itself states that unsuccessful requests can stem from any rule or response error, so no violation conclusion may be drawn from it directly. Fourth, legal characterisation: on whether log fields constitute personal data and how long retention is appropriate, the third-party compliance compilations cited here offer direction only; concrete obligations must follow the applicable law and the texts of regulators, and are not decided here. In addition, static export and caching mean that pages of different vintages exist externally at the same time, so a timestamp in an observation record cannot automatically stand for which version the reader actually saw.

Conclusion: build the observation dashboard as a fact log, not a verdict on purpose

A restrained observatory is more useful: it can answer “was the declaration read after publication”, “which class of client is sending more requests” and “did error codes change after this edit”, all of them facts that can be re-checked. It should not answer “who is training on our content”, because that needs evidence the site cannot obtain. The recommendation is to implement the field allowlist, the three-way identifier split, pseudonymisation and retention, the framing note page, aggregate output and the sentence “does not prove purpose” together, and to keep the unknown category in every report. Then, when a partner or reader asks about the observation data, what the site presents is not a log that appears to know everything but a record with clear boundaries, aligned timestamps and a basis that can be re-checked — and that second thing is what remains usable for a long-term conversation.

Sources and framing (as of 13 November 2026): that AI traffic is classified by behaviour into Search / Agent / Training, that a single bot can have several behaviours, the AI Crawl Control panel column names (Crawler, Category, Requests, Robots.txt violations, Action), that unsuccessful requests can stem from any rule or response error, that the crawl-to-referral ratio is a traffic-level metric, the BotBase directory, that the three behaviours can be controlled separately from July 2026, and the default policy for newly onboarded domains of 15 September 2026 — all of this comes from the official Cloudflare Developers documentation and the official changelog and is official platform framing. That the machine-readable manifest contains an observation endpoint and that the endpoint address comes from runtime configuration comes from ZouIC’s own implementation and is the site’s own framing. Advice on minimising log fields, pseudonymising addresses and setting a retention period proportionate to the purpose comes from third-party compliance compilations and law-firm articles, is cited only as directional reference, is not written into the conclusion and carries no legal characterisation; the platform’s statistical framing and the site’s self-reported identifiers are set side by side without being merged or averaged.

Frequently asked questions

If an AI crawler is observed visiting, can we say the content was used for training?

No. The origin sees only the request path, the identifier and what was returned; it cannot see whether the content, once it left the server, went into an index, a summary or a training set. Classification by behaviour in the Cloudflare documentation is a platform-side grouping, not a judgement about the purpose of a single request. The log can support only this: an identifier requested an address at a given moment.

Why can an “unsuccessful request” in the panel not be treated directly as a violation?

The platform documentation states explicitly that unsuccessful requests can stem from any rule or response error, not only from blocking actions; Robots.txt violations is likewise a count under a statistical framing. Reaching a conclusion also requires checking the rules, response codes and declaration-file version in force at the time, so a single statistic is not enough for a characterisation.

Should the log keep full visitor IPs?

Under the purpose-driven principle of this note, usually not. To check whether the declaration was read and whether the site returned what was expected, a timestamp, path, status code and a normalised identifier suffice. Third-party compliance compilations recommend pseudonymising address-type fields and setting a retention period proportionate to the purpose, but that is directional guidance; concrete obligations still follow the applicable law and regulatory texts.
