Optical link acceptance: what the standards give you, and what the site still has to sign

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

Conclusion first: optical link acceptance does not call for a single link-is-up sheet, but for three separate records — the standards basis, the measurement record and the exception record. IEEE defines Ethernet rates and standard status, OIF defines line interfaces and electrical measurement points, and IEC defines connector end-face inspection criteria, yet no official page provides an on-site bit-error-rate target or power budget. That is something the parties must agree in advance; it cannot be derived from a rate figure. The following unfolds six things — scope, preparation, steps, records, review and exception handling — so the reader can follow it and leave evidence behind. Signing for a parcel only proves it arrived at a certain time, not that its contents are intact; a link lighting up only proves there is light, not that it can be accepted against a specification.

What: setting the scope of this checklist

This checklist applies to incoming and delivery acceptance of a high-speed optical link in a data centre or AI cluster. It answers one question: which version of a specification, which measurement point and whose signature does this pass rest on? It does not judge the merits of a transceiver vendor, does not provide bit-error-rate (BER, the proportion of bits received in error) or forward-error-correction (FEC, correcting errors at the transceiver to buy error performance) thresholds, and does not fill in power budgets that the official pages do not give. Fix one version fact first: according to the IEEE 802.3 task force page, IEEE Std 802.3df-2024 is a published 400 Gb/s and 800 Gb/s Ethernet standard whose task force work is complete. P802.3dj covers 200 Gb/s, 400 Gb/s, 800 Gb/s and 1.6 Tb/s and, as of the baseline date, is still at draft D3.3 and has not been formally published. A falsifiable claim: treating a rate target in a draft as an effective clause in the acceptance basis amounts to letting a document that is not yet final sign off delivery.

Why: separating what each layer of specification actually gives

Preparation is not about gathering material; it is about drawing the line on which layer may enter the conclusion. The first layer is IEEE: the task force page records that work on 802.3df was completed after approval by the IEEE-SA Standards Board. On the approval and publication dates, the task force page records approval on 2024-02-16, while another IEEE liaison letter records approval on 2024-02-15 and publication on 2024-03-15; both are written side by side, the difference explained, and no average is taken. The second layer is OIF (Optical Internetworking Forum): the 800ZR implementation agreement (IA, an interoperability interface specification published by the alliance) was released on 2024-10-30 and gives application targets of 80–120 km for amplified single-span DWDM links and 2–10 km for unamplified fixed-wavelength links, with an Ethernet client from a minimum of 100GE up to 800G aggregate. The OIF current-work page also lists CEI electrical measurement points TP1/TP1a and TP4/TP4a, and the scope of 224G PAM4 link training: communication method, training patterns, state sequences, diagnostics and fault handling. The third layer is IEC 61300-3-35:2022, which governs the zones, criteria and minimum microscope conditions for visual inspection of fibre-optic connector end faces. A falsifiable claim: merging the three layers into a sentence such as the specification requires the link to reach a given bit-error rate — none of the official pages checked here gives such a number, and writing one in would be inventing a clause on behalf of the specification.

Who: who measures, who judges, who reviews and who approves site thresholds

At least four roles are needed. The installation party takes the raw readings at the agreed measurement points; the test party judges against thresholds confirmed by both sides; the reviewer signs the raw record and the judgement but enters no figures of their own; the accepting party approves the specification version and the site thresholds as an attachment. A fifth kind of evidence needs its own signature: OIF held the first 800ZR multi-vendor interoperability demonstration at OFC 2024, which shows that several implementations interworked on one occasion. That is interoperability evidence, not the acceptance record of any particular delivery. A falsifiable claim: letting the installation party measure, judge and sign in one go, with the reviewer merely ticking seen at the end, leaves no record that says who set the threshold or who took the reading when a dispute arises. Roles in the table header beat roles in the meeting minutes, because the header is what gets checked row by row on export. Water, electricity and fire protection each produce their own report when a property is handed over; the same logic applies here.

When: four acceptance nodes, each signed once, neither merged nor back-dated

Spread acceptance across four nodes, with a signature at each: incoming check (model, version and quantity match the purchase list), link connectivity and measurement-point readings after power-up, a retest after continuous operation, and record filing and review before delivery. Every node carries a timestamp, a raw-record number and a responsible person; a skipped node is marked pending review rather than passed by default. The standards basis is also labelled by status at each node: cite 802.3df as a published standard; cite P802.3dj as draft D3.3, unpublished as of the baseline date, whose November 2026 plenary announcement says it expects to complete the third Standards Association recirculation and submit the draft to RevCom before the session, with a fourth recirculation handled during the plenary if required — that is progress, not effect. A falsifiable claim: merging the four nodes into one final acceptance and back-dating it discards the intermediate states, so a later deviation cannot be traced to incoming goods, installation or drifting conditions.

Where: write the measurement point into the record, not beside the optical port

This section governs location. On the electrical side, use the OIF CEI vocabulary of measurement points: TP1/TP1a and TP4/TP4a are electrical measurement points in the specification; on site, probe position, instrument model and record format must be agreed separately. The OIF current-work page gives XSR of 50 mm or more and VSR of 200 mm host plus 20 mm module plus one connector as reach targets, not guaranteed on-site measurements. On the end-face side, IEC 61300-3-35:2022 applies: the third edition inspects fibre zones A and B and the whole contact area (up to 250 µm diameter for cylindrical ferrules), removes the C and D zone requirements of the old edition, changes the outer edge of zone B from 115 µm to 110 µm, and states that a connector cannot be rejected solely for failing visual inspection — fitness for use is determined by the specified optical performance. The standard also states that visual inspection is additional and does not replace measurement of parameters such as attenuation and return loss. A falsifiable claim: recording only that the end face is clean, without the zone, microscope model and storage location of the original image, makes it impossible for a reviewer to repeat the check under the same conditions.

How: six steps to follow, with no empty fields in the record

Turn the six things into a reproducible flow. First, build three tables: standards basis, measurement records and exception rollback, each with a version number and effective date. Second, confirm the status of every cited specification line by line, separating published standards from drafts, and keep the two date accounts of 802.3df side by side without averaging. Third, agree site thresholds and measurement points in writing; where the official pages give no bit-error rate, FEC threshold or power budget, write jointly agreed, value entered here — never leave it blank and never infer it from a rate. Fourth, inspect the end face under IEC 61300-3-35:2022 and keep the image; measure performance parameters separately and record them on their own. Fifth, keep multi-vendor interoperability results in a different column from this delivery acceptance: the former cites the 800ZR IA and the demonstration, the latter only the readings and signatures for this link. Sixth, before export, check that every pass carries a specification version, measurement point, threshold source, reviewer signature and date. A falsifiable claim: the value of a checklist is not how many items it lists but whether an assertion before export can block an empty threshold source; without it, even a long checklist relies on goodwill.

Boundaries: exception handling and what this checklist does not do

Handle exceptions in three ways. An entry found only in a draft or an in-progress project is marked status pending verification and is not promoted to a published basis; in-progress OIF projects such as CEI-224G and CEI-448G are not treated as published IAs and their status is checked item by item; a threshold absent from the official pages is left blank with joint value pending entered, neither estimated nor copied from vendor claims. When visual inspection and performance findings conflict, both are written side by side with the difference explained, with no averaging and no substitution — that is the criterion stated in the third edition of IEC 61300-3-35. When a site threshold cannot be settled at once, the item goes into a pending-review queue and is not silently passed. The checklist promises no bit-error-rate or distance result, does not turn application or reach targets such as 80–120 km, 2–10 km, 50 mm and 200 mm into contractual guarantees, does not convert rate figures such as 212.5 Gb/s per wavelength or 224G into on-site conclusions, and makes no quality judgement on any vendor equipment. A falsifiable claim: filling an empty threshold with an industry-typical value to make the table look tidy is precisely what turns a verifiable gap into an unverifiable number.

Conclusion: turning up into a string of traceable fields

In one sentence: acceptance passes are not shown by a lit indicator but by the record. A deliverable link should let you trace which specification version it rests on, whether that version was published or a draft at the time, who took the reading at which measurement point, who agreed the bit-error-rate and power thresholds under which framing, in which records the end-face inspection and performance measurements live, and whether an exception went through rollback. Once that is in place, pass stops being an adjective and becomes a string of fields carrying versions and signatures; even after the specification is updated and staff change, acceptance at different times can still be compared correctly under their own version labels. With that done, the answer to whether the link can be called accepted is: here is what the specification provided and here is what the site additionally signed — both columns are present, and whichever is missing says plainly whether it is pending verification or jointly agreed.

Sources and framing (as of 18 November 2026): Level 1 (specification text): the IEEE 802.3 task force page records that IEEE Std 802.3df-2024 work is complete and was approved by the IEEE-SA Standards Board, covering 400 Gb/s and 800 Gb/s; the P802.3dj task force page and the November 2026 plenary announcement email show that, as of the baseline date, the standard is draft D3.3 and unpublished. The IEC Webstore records IEC 61300-3-35:2022 as the third edition, published 2022-09-16 with a stability date of 2031; visual inspection is additional to performance measurement and a failed inspection alone is not grounds for rejection. Level 2 (alliance official): the OIF 800G Coherent page records the 800ZR IA released 2024-10-30, the 800G Coherent and co-packaging framework IA projects started 2020-12-08, and application targets of amplified single-span DWDM 80–120 km, unamplified fixed wavelength 2–10 km and a minimum 100GE client; the OIF current-work page records the TP1/TP1a and TP4/TP4a electrical measurement points, the 224G PAM4 link-training scope and the CEI-224G XSR/VSR reach targets. All of the above are alliance or platform statements and specification targets, not on-site acceptance thresholds. Level 3 (site framing): the three-table split, four-node signing, role division, empty-field assertion and exception rules are this site method and belong to no standard text. Level 4 (third-party compilation): no third-party compilation is cited; nothing is used even as a directional lead. Level 5 (parallel framings): two official accounts of the 802.3df approval and publication dates exist (task force page 2024-02-16; IEEE liaison letter approval 2024-02-15 and publication 2024-03-15); both are written side by side, the difference explained and no average taken; visual-inspection and performance findings are likewise parallel and neither replaces the other. The baseline date is the verification date, not a standard publication date, demonstration date or any link delivery date.

Frequently asked questions

Can both 802.3df and 802.3dj be called an 800G standard?

No, they must not be conflated. 802.3df-2024 is a published 400G/800G Ethernet standard. P802.3dj covers a wider range of rates but is still draft D3.3 as of the baseline date and has not been published. The acceptance basis must state the version and status; writing a draft target as an effective clause amounts to letting an unfinished document sign off delivery.

If end-face inspection passes, can the link be signed off as accepted?

No. IEC 61300-3-35:2022 states that visual inspection is an additional check and does not replace measurement of parameters such as attenuation and return loss. Conversely, a failed visual inspection alone is not grounds to reject a connector; fitness for use is determined by the specified optical performance. Both need a record, and neither replaces the other or is averaged with it.

Can the 80–120 km in OIF be written straight into a contractual distance guarantee?

No. That is an application target of the 800ZR IA for amplified single-span DWDM links, not an on-site acceptance guarantee. The CEI-224G figures of 50 mm or more and 200 mm plus 20 mm are reach targets as well. A contract should state the conditions and thresholds measured by both parties; a specification target may be cited as a design basis but cannot replace an on-site reading.
