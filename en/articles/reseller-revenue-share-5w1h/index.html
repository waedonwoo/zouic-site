Revenue share is not a markup: where a service provider’s income actually comes from

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

In reselling a platform’s advanced features, what gets questioned most is not capability but motive: customers suspect the site has added another layer on top of the official price. Repeating “no markup” is not actually the best answer, because what really explains it is the revenue structure — where the money comes from, by what rule it is split, and who receives it when. What follows unpacks the difference between revenue share and markup along the 5W1H axes. Conclusion first: a service provider’s income comes from the order share under platform rules, not from a self-set markup on the list price; what can be compressed is the cost of selection, provisioning and coordination, while what cannot be compressed is responsibility for compliance, delivery and after-sales.

What: the share that happens only after the order is completed

The WeCom service provider integration guide states it plainly. When an order is created, the pricing strategy the service provider configures on the platform first produces a version price; on expansion or a version change, the value of the currently active version is prorated and deducted as a discount; the service provider may waive a further discount amount, then multiply by the payable coefficient to obtain the payable price, which the user pays. The funds generated once the order is completed are shared according to platform rules, and that share involves three parties — the service provider, the platform, and the agent present only when that enterprise application has been registered by an application agent — and the interface also supplies the relevant information to developers for reconciliation. The same document says the payable coefficient depends on who places the order: 1.0 when the enterprise orders itself, the platform revenue-share ratio when an application service provider places it, and 1.0 minus the agreed agent revenue-share ratio when an agent service provider places it. In other words, the share happens after the transaction, follows platform rules and comes with reconcilable interface data; it is not a number the site appends to the list price, nor the result of a one-off negotiation.

Why: only a clear revenue structure makes the position clear

What customers really worry about is information asymmetry: if a site can both change the price and interpret it, then its product recommendations deserve suspicion. Writing “income comes from the official share, the site adds no markup of its own and promises no discount” puts the conflict of interest on the table voluntarily, and only then does selection advice become credible. Conversely, writing only “no markup” and not the mechanism leaves you unable to answer concrete questions about expansion proration, proxy-order coefficients and registration relationships, which looks evasive instead. There is a further judgement that belongs to the service provider’s own operations: for a one-person company or a small team, what can be compressed is coordination cost — chaining selection, proxy provisioning, deployment and guided rollout into one process so the customer does not shuttle between platform, documentation and implementer. What cannot be compressed is responsibility cost — the boundary of compliance retention, data ownership, account permissions, invoicing and after-sales response, each of which needs a person to sign off. Describing revenue share as “an almost cost-free price difference” makes people assume those responsibilities do not exist either.

Who: four roles and one optional path

The platform sets the rules, provides the order page and the share framing; the service provider configures a pricing strategy, may place orders on a customer’s behalf and carries service delivery; the application agent enters the share only when the condition “this enterprise application has been registered” holds; and the enterprise administrator places the order and pays. Of the four, the site controls its own service scope and promises, but not platform rules, share ratios or registration relationships. External description should therefore be two sentences: one on “how income arises” — a share under platform rules, reconcilable through interface data; and one on “what we do” — selection advice, proxy provisioning, deployment and subsequent guided rollout. The two must not be merged into “we sell these features”, because “sell” implies both pricing authority and endorsement of the product itself. Making the roles explicit has a practical benefit too: when a price dispute arises, you can immediately tell whether to check the order page with the platform or the service scope with the site, instead of each side pointing at the other.

When: three moments decide what the money means

The first is the ordering moment: an enterprise ordering itself and a service provider ordering on its behalf correspond to different payable coefficients, and whether a registration relationship exists is settled beforehand, so “the same product, the same seat count” is not the same account under different paths. The second is the order-completion moment: the share happens after the transaction under platform rules, interface data supports reconciliation, and promising a specific income figure in advance has no basis. The third is the rule-change moment: platform rules and share ratios are decided by the platform, and the explanation on a service provider’s page is only the current relay that must be re-checked against the documentation as it updates. The three moments imply a maintenance discipline: any statement about price, share or coefficient should carry the baseline date and point to the current official documentation; writing one reconciliation result up as a long-term business-model description will go stale quickly. External communication should also layer by time — capability boundaries and estimates during consultation, the order-page framing at ordering, reconciliation and after-sales after the transaction.

Where: capability statements and responsibility boundaries written apart

The official mall and the order page are where product and price facts live; the service provider integration guide is where share and order rules live. The site’s services page should state only the capabilities it can actually deliver — selection, proxy provisioning, deployment, guided rollout — and the things it does not deliver: no self-set pricing, no discount promises, no endorsement of platform feature details on the platform’s behalf. Contracts and email then state who handles compliance configuration, who retains the data, who responds to after-sales, and by which framing disputes are handled. The most common overreach here is writing “can be activated” as “we provide”, and “official feature” as “site capability”: the feature is provided by the platform, and what the site provides is the process of putting that feature to work. The responsibility boundary especially needs to be put in writing, because the share can be computed from platform rules while responsibility cost can only be borne by the service party; if a page talks only about the share and not responsibility, customers will naturally assume the latter is included in the price too.

How: write the revenue structure as five verifiable sentences

Sentence one: products and list prices are provided by the platform, and the amounts on the page are the official public list prices with a verification date. Sentence two: the service provider’s income comes from the platform share after the order is completed, interface data supports reconciliation, and the site adds no markup of its own and promises no discount. Sentence three: proxy ordering changes the order’s payable coefficient, and the order page and the current official integration guide govern. Sentence four: the site’s scope covers selection, proxy provisioning, deployment and guided rollout, while compliance configuration, data ownership, invoicing and after-sales response are agreed separately in the contract. Sentence five: the consultation stage gives only a cost estimate, never a commitment on the transaction price. The five sentences map to price setter, income source, path difference, capability boundary and communication stage. A customer who asks through all five can tell whether the site has said everything, and the site answers with the same text everywhere, avoiding different versions across channels.

Boundaries: what the revenue-share mechanism cannot explain

Share ratios, payable-coefficient values and registration decisions are determined by platform rules and agreements between the parties; the official integration guide supplies the structure and the framing, not concrete numbers that can be applied directly, and this article infers no ratio. Descriptions of “value-added tool fees” and “hidden fees” in third-party channel articles are channel self-description discussing a different kind of charge; they cannot be used to explain the official share and should not sit alongside the official list price. The revenue-share mechanism also does not cover delivery quality: whether the selection fits, whether provisioning is timely, whether account permissions are kept under control, whether compliance retention is configured to the customer’s industry requirements — all of that depends on what the service party did and has nothing to do with how the money is split. There is a more fundamental boundary: platform rules can change, but a service party’s promises to its customers cannot change unilaterally with them; writing “rules are set by the platform” on the page is meant to state where the framing comes from, not to leave a door open for shifting responsibility later.

Conclusion: explain the money fully and the responsibility fully

Use the structure of the official integration guide to answer “where income comes from”, a capability list to answer “what we do”, and the contract to answer “who is liable when something goes wrong” — each written in its own place, word-for-word consistent. There is no need to keep stressing “no markup”: write clearly that the share happens after the transaction, that proxy ordering changes the payable coefficient and that the order page governs, and customers reach the conclusion themselves. What genuinely remains for the site to invest in are the responsibility costs that cannot be compressed: get the selection right, keep permissions tight, and take after-sales seriously. Revenue share is given by the platform; trust is built by the service party.

Product and list-price information on the official mall comes from the WeCom official mall page. The statements “the service provider’s income comes from the official share, the site adds no markup of its own, no discount is promised” are the site’s own service framing. Where framings conflict they are set side by side rather than averaged. After the baseline date platform rules may change; the order page and the current documentation govern.

Frequently asked questions

Is a service provider’s income a layer added on top of the official price?

According to the official integration guide, income comes from the share applied under platform rules after an order is completed, and the interface also supplies reconciliation data. The site adds no markup of its own to the list price and promises no discount. A markup and a revenue share are two different structures; do not explain them as one.

Does proxy ordering change what the customer actually pays?

It changes the calculation path. The guide sets the payable coefficient at 1.0 when the enterprise orders itself, at the platform revenue-share ratio when an application service provider places the order, and at 1.0 minus the agreed agent revenue-share ratio when an agent service provider places it. The order page and the current official documentation govern.

Does a revenue-share model mean the service provider has no responsibility cost?

No. Rules can compute the share but not delivery quality: compliance configuration, data ownership, account permissions, invoicing and after-sales response all need somebody responsible. Revenue share explains where the money comes from; the responsibility boundary should be written separately on the page and in the contract.
