When the grid asks you to cut load: which contract does flexibility belong in

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

Calling a data centre a flexible load sounds like a statement of attitude, but it is actually a set of clauses that must be written down: what triggers curtailment, who issues the instruction, how many megawatts, how quickly service must recover, what happens if it does not, and who receives the avoided capacity charge or the compensation. Miss one and flexibility stays in a press release. Conclusion first: measure the curtailable amount for real, put trigger and recovery into the interconnection and supply contracts, then use the power curve from each event as the basis for settlement and review. In the other order, what you signed is a promise nobody can perform.

What: flexibility is an obligation with numbers and deadlines

Several distinct forms are already public. In March 2026 Google announced 1 GW of demand-response capability written into long-term contracts with five US utilities, shifting machine-learning workloads to times and places with spare grid capacity or temporarily reducing non-critical operations (company announcement). EPRI DCFlex has more than 45 members and nine demonstration sites; in an Arizona field test an AI compute cluster cut power by 25 percent across a three-hour peak event without compromising essential services (industry organisation and media accounts). On the regulatory side, Texas SB 6 was, as of May 2026, the only statewide law mandating curtailment for large loads; Delaware HB 233, enacted in August 2026 and codified at 26 Del. C. section 317, requires utilities to plan for curtailing large energy-use facilities of 30 MW or more, applies to loads connected after 31 December 2026 and exempts facilities building qualifying new generation from most requirements. These are a contract, a demonstration and a statutory duty — three different things.

Why: uncoordinated disconnection is itself a grid risk

A frequently cited event is a voltage fluctuation in Northern Virginia in July 2024: roughly 60 data centres disconnected at once, suddenly leaving about 1,500 MW of surplus on the grid and forcing emergency action to prevent cascading outages (think-tank and legal-blog accounts). It argues not that data centres should not reduce load but that reduction must be predictable, dispatchable and have a recovery curve. A second driver is cost and fairness: capacity charges follow peak contribution, so actively lowering load in a few critical hours affects the next year bill. One think-tank account also puts more than 4.3 billion US dollars of 2024 transmission upgrade costs for serving AI data centres onto consumers across seven PJM states, which makes who pays for the grid costs a large load imposes a contested regulatory question (third-party commentary, not a regulatory finding).

Who: who may instruct, and who should be paid, is still disputed

The parties include grid operators, utilities, data-centre operators, regulators and other customers. Utilities in at least five states — Ohio, Arkansas, Idaho, Montana and Wyoming — have regulatory approval for separate rate classes for AI data centres, and the new rates can make peak-hour demand charges expensive enough to create a financial reason to curtail (industry compilation). The disagreement persists: the PJM Independent Market Monitor argues that data centres without their own generation should be curtailable but should not be paid as if they were providing demand response; others argue compensation is needed to build the software and systems that shed load safely and quickly (both positions relayed by a legal blog). Whether flexibility should be paid for therefore has no uniform answer yet, and the contract has to say it explicitly rather than wait for the market to decide.

When: write clauses before interconnection, execute in minutes during an event

The most important moment is before interconnection: queue position, whether you must bring your own generation, the rate class and curtailment obligations are all settled there and are almost impossible to add later. Next is the effective date of the threshold — Delaware applies to loads connected after 31 December 2026, so projects on either side of that date have different duties. Third is minute-level response when the grid is stressed: UPS batteries offer short-duration capacity, with an industry report putting the common order of magnitude at 50–100 MW for one to four hours (verify on site); Meta demonstrated 10–15 MW of dispatchable capacity with the PJM DCFlex project and Microsoft piloted sub-second fast frequency response at its Dublin site (company and project announcements). Fourth is contract renewal, when capacity charges and peak contribution are recalibrated, because the previous year curve sets the next year price.

Where: clauses are spread across four kinds of documents

The interconnection agreement holds triggers, notification lead time, curtailable amount and recovery time. The supply contract or a special rate class holds capacity charges, how peak contribution is calculated and any flexibility discount. Operating procedures hold duty authority, the reduction order and the list of loads that must never be touched. Settlement and audit material holds the power curve, instruction time and measured response for each event. Demonstrations and company announcements are often mistaken for general rules, when they only show what one site achieved under one set of conditions. They cannot be copied across regions either: the state laws, rate classes and PJM positions cited here are US-specific regulatory arrangements with a different set of tariff rules, demand-response mechanisms and interconnection procedures from those in China. Use them as a reference for structure, never as a compliance basis.

How: measure it, write it down, keep the record

Step one, split curtailable load into three measurable classes — shiftable batch jobs, pausable non-critical loads and UPS battery discharge — each with a different response speed and recovery cost, so they must not be merged into one number. Step two, put trigger conditions, notification lead time, target reduction, allowed deviation, recovery time, non-performance consequences, compensation ownership and an explicit never-touch list into the interconnection and supply contracts. Step three, calibrate the committed value with on-site measurement instead of adopting an industry report 50–100 MW range as your own capability. Step four, retain the power curve, instruction time, actual response and recovery process for every event as the basis for settlement and review; without a curve, the parties can only argue from memory about whether load was actually reduced. Four steps turn flexibility from an attitude into an auditable obligation.

Boundaries: conclusions these cases do not support

A company announcement of 1 GW of demand-response capability is a portfolio-level contractual figure, not 1 GW that one hall can shed at any moment. A 25 percent demonstration result is a single field test, not a generally achievable ratio. The 50–100 MW over one to four hours UPS range is an industry-report order of magnitude that depends on battery configuration and load. State laws, rate classes and the PJM monitor position belong to a specific US region, and whether curtailability should be compensated is explicitly contested; this article takes no side. The July 2024 Northern Virginia event and the 4.3 billion US dollar cost-shift figure come from think-tank and legal-blog accounts whose definitions and scope need checking. All figures here illustrate structure and imply no compliance or revenue judgement for any project.

Three documents that make flexibility handover-ready

One, a capability list with measured values, response speed and recovery cost for each of the three curtailable classes. Two, a clause list covering trigger, notification, reduction amount, recovery and compensation in the interconnection and supply contracts. Three, an event archive with power curves and instruction times. With those three, flexibility stops being a slogan and becomes something operations can execute, finance can settle and regulators can audit. For the compute aftermarket this is another case of coordination cost being compressible while liability cost is not: a dispatchable megawatt has value, but only if somebody can prove it was actually reduced as agreed.

Sources and qualifiers (baseline 11 November 2026): the March 2026 Google 1 GW demand-response contracts with five utilities, the Meta 10–15 MW PJM DCFlex pilot and Microsoft Dublin sub-second UPS frequency response come from company and project announcements compiled by third parties. EPRI DCFlex membership, demonstration-site counts and the Arizona three-hour 25 percent test come from the industry organisation and media reports. Texas SB 6, Delaware HB 233 / 26 Del. C. section 317, the five state rate classes and the PJM Independent Market Monitor position come from a legal blog and regulatory compilations (third parties, not regulatory originals). The July 2024 Northern Virginia disconnection of 60 data centres with roughly 1,500 MW surplus and the 4.3 billion US dollar transmission cost shift come from think-tank and commentary accounts. The 50–100 MW over one to four hours UPS range is an industry report order of magnitude. None of these regional definitions can be applied directly to domestic projects.

Frequently asked questions

Does saying we are willing to reduce load count as flexibility?

No. Flexibility needs a trigger, the instructing party, the reduction amount, recovery time, consequences for non-performance and who receives compensation. Without numbers and deadlines it can neither be executed nor settled.

Can UPS batteries be counted directly as dispatchable capacity?

Only after site measurement. The 50–100 MW over one to four hours quoted in industry reports is a common order of magnitude; actual capability depends on battery configuration, load and discharge strategy, and recharging during recovery also matters.

Can these US state laws and rate classes be applied to a domestic project?

No. They are regional regulatory arrangements, and even there whether curtailment should be compensated is disputed. The clause structure and evidence trail are worth studying; compliance must be based on local rules.
