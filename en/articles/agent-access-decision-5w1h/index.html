Opening the door to signed agents means making five separate decisions

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

Treating agent access as a boolean switch is the most expensive mistake in the new access regime. Five things actually need a decision: who to recognise, which purpose to recognise, which paths to allow, under what conditions to charge, and what record can be produced afterwards. They live in a standards draft, platform policy, site configuration and your own logs respectively, and no layer can decide for another. The short version: signatures made identity reliable, they did not make the allow decision easier. Writing verified into allowed is mistaking the means for the conclusion.

What: one switch is actually five independent decisions

On 15 September 2026 Cloudflare replaced its single Block AI Bots control with three independent controls for search, AI training and AI agents, and began showing newly onboarded domains a recommended setting based on their business model: ad-supported sites allow search, disallow training and block agents on ad-bearing pages; other sites allow all three (official press release). The same release reports that mixed-use crawlers are 36.6 percent of verified crawler traffic on its network, the largest single category, while fewer than 1 percent of sites block search crawlers and 17 percent restrict AI training. The AI crawler everyone argues about is therefore not one thing that can be switched off: the same crawler often builds an index and collects training material, so refusing the latter while keeping the former requires purpose to be expressed separately from client identity.

Why: the cost of a blunt decision changed from lost traffic to lost money

For nearly three decades the bargain was simple: crawlers take content and send traffic back. Mixed-use crawlers broke it. Without purpose separation, refusing training also cost a site the search inclusion delivered by the same crawler, so many sites allowed everything. The four Accountable criteria published by Cloudflare — a clear opt-out from training through robots.txt or an equivalent standard, an opt-out from AI-generated search summaries, URL-level visibility into how content is used, and public confirmation that opting out of training does not affect traditional search ranking — are essentially a demand that operators separate purposes; Apple, Google and Microsoft were listed as meeting them or committed to a timeline (official wording). The asymmetry changed too: a false block costs one impression, while a false allow can hand paid content, pricing data and inventory endpoints to a client with no settlement relationship.

Who: four parties own one segment each, and the end user is absent from the protocol

Site owners set policy, crawler operators declare purpose and provide opt-outs, verifiers and edge networks execute, and standards bodies define how identity is proven. The IETF webbotauth working-group draft builds on RFC 9421 HTTP Message Signatures: operators publish public keys in a well-known directory, requests carry Signature-Agent plus signature labels, created and expires, and the verifier checks which key signed the covered parts. The draft itself states that a valid signature says nothing about who operates the agent, whether it is benign, or whether the request was authorised, and that it does not define authorisation or delegation. Whether an end user consented, and to what scope, has no role in the protocol at all. Treating verification as authorisation means deciding on behalf of a party who is not present.

When: three dates that have not converged

On 1 September 2026 an individual draft was adopted by the working group as draft-ietf-webbotauth-httpsig-protocol-00; it is an active working-group document but still an Internet-Draft, not an RFC. On 15 September the three platform controls and recommended defaults went live. In the same press release, giving site owners one place to control how much of their content appears in AI summaries was described as a goal for early the following year — a plan, not a delivered capability. These three dates have different consequences: standards status decides whether you need a compatibility layer, platform defaults decide what a new domain sees on day one, and a roadmap statement must never appear in an acceptance test. Until the draft becomes an RFC, field forms, coverage items and directory resolution rules may all change.

Where: four layers with hard boundaries between them

The declaration layer is robots.txt, Content Signals and terms files: it states what uses you allow, which is a norm and a claim whose truth needs separate evidence. The identity layer is the signature and key directory, answering only whether a request was signed by that key. The policy layer sits at the edge or gateway and decides allow, charge, deny and what content is returned. The fact layer is your own request log and ledger, recording what actually happened. Public third-party analysis points out an easily missed detail: if a terms file such as terms.txt fails to parse, the whole file is treated as absent and the visible governance state jumps from conditional to no terms at all. Publish terms with versions, validate before release, replace atomically and verify externally. The fact layer is the one most often skipped, and it is the only one that can answer what was actually returned when a dispute arrives.

How: turn the decision into a table someone can execute

Step one, group by path rather than by whole site — at minimum ad-bearing pages, editorial pages and documentation or API endpoints — and write purpose-by-action for each. Step two, record the protocol version and verifier behaviour: public document comparisons show the working-group -00 expects a dictionary-form Signature-Agent indexed by signature label, while some platform implementation docs at the time still expected the older bare-string form and explicitly said dictionary-form signatures would fail verification. Test interoperability with real traffic and retain IP, reverse DNS and User-Agent fallbacks. Step three, log key ID, directory URL, created/expires, draft version, request target and timestamp, treating verification as one risk input rather than the authorisation verdict. Step four, measure false blocks and false allows over two weeks of real traffic before discussing pricing. A price without a baseline is just a number with a unit attached.

Boundaries: where this framing stops working

The three controls, the Accountable list and the recommended defaults only exist inside that platform network. Self-hosted origins or domains on other CDNs do not get the verified-crawler label and must implement verification, directory caching and policy themselves. The declaration layer cannot prove that a stated purpose is true: a valid signature does not make the claim true, nor does it establish which company controls the key in the real world, so sites needing higher assurance still need additional trust relationships. The standards status is still moving, and freezing one draft version into a long-lived interface will be expensive to upgrade. Finally, these are access and visibility tools: HTTP cannot show whether delivered content later entered an index, a summary or a training set.

Three things you can ship now

First, write policy as a versioned purpose-by-path-by-action table so every change has a retrievable rationale. Second, complete the fact layer: at minimum it should answer, for any request, the signing identity, declared purpose, response code and returned content version. Third, keep signature verification degradable, falling back to a predefined policy when the directory or verifier fails instead of returning 403 across the site. Only after these three does a site qualify to discuss charging — otherwise, once a price is published, nobody can reconcile who fetched what and what is owed. Signatures provide better evidence for admission; opening the door remains a commercial decision the site owns.

Sources and qualifiers (baseline 11 November 2026): the Cloudflare press release of 15 September 2026 and the Pay Per Crawl engineering blog are official platform statements. The 36.6 percent, fewer than 1 percent and 17 percent figures, and the claim that over half of AI crawl traffic re-fetches unchanged pages, are the platform own network measurements, not internet-wide ratios. IETF draft-ietf-webbotauth-httpsig-protocol-00 and RFC 9421 are standards documents. The dictionary-versus-bare-string version mismatch and the consequences of a terms.txt parse failure come from public document comparisons and third-party technical analysis; official material does not confirm full production interoperability between the two sides. The draft may be revised after this baseline date — check the current version before implementing.

Frequently asked questions

Can a verified signature be treated as permission to serve?

No. A signature only proves that the holder of a published key signed the covered parts of a request. The draft explicitly says it does not establish operator honesty, user authorisation or delegation. Allowing access still needs separate checks on quota, purpose and approval.

If a site does not sit behind such an edge platform, is this split still relevant?

Yes, but the starting point differs. Without the platform label of a verified crawler, you implement signature verification, directory caching and policy yourself; purpose grouping, logging and fallback paths become even more necessary because no platform absorbs mistakes for you.

Should a site put a price on agent access now?

Build the ledger first. At minimum you need request volume and returned content reconciled by purpose, path and client, followed by two weeks of false-block and false-allow data. Pricing without that baseline is an uncalibrated number with a currency sign attached.
