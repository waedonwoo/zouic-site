Longer than the GPU lead time is the grid queue: the logic of AI data centre siting has changed

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

A few years ago, choosing a data centre site meant asking about fibre, land price and distance to users. In 2026 there is only one first question: can this plot get power before 2028? GPU lead times are measured in quarters, grid connections in years — and the longer pipe sets the compute ceiling.

Powering Artificial Intelligence was commissioned by the French G7 presidency and published in October 2026, covering 34 jurisdictions and about 90% of global data centre capacity. It counts 134 relevant policies, nearly 40% of them issued in the preceding 12 months. That sudden jump in policy density is itself a sign that the bottleneck has reached regulators.

Queue inflation is the variable everyone misreads

The IEA found that in several regions the cumulative capacity of connection applications already exceeds existing local peak load. That is not because demand is really that large: developers queue in several places at once to secure resources, and many projects have neither a customer nor locked-in compute demand. Grid planning is therefore squeezed between two risks — overestimate and build stranded assets, underestimate and stall projects. In 2025 about 60% of data centre capacity sat in existing clusters; if every planned project is built that rises to 70% — load topology is being rewritten by a handful of sites.

Another counter-intuitive point: AI data centre load swings rapidly and sharply. The IEA notes that these swings strain the technical capability of on-site gas turbines, which is why storage is moving from backup power to a connection condition. It also explains why more projects discuss demand response: let non-urgent training jobs scale down automatically at residential peak times in exchange for a faster, cheaper connection permit.

Nuclear PPAs are options, not spot power

The loudest headlines are about nuclear and SMR offtake agreements, and the IEA does confirm that the tech sector accounted for about 40% of corporate renewable PPAs in 2025 and became the main driver behind advanced nuclear and enhanced geothermal. But treating 45 GW of conditional offtake agreements as 45 GW of built capacity is the same category error as treating a 6 GW GPU supply agreement as 6 GW of delivered racks. Between an SMR agreement and grid connection sit site selection, licensing, construction and commissioning.

What can actually deliver in 2026-2028 are three less glamorous things: queue reform (higher entry thresholds, milestone-based capacity release), non-firm connections (the grid may curtail load under agreed conditions at congested times) and grid-enhancing technologies (dynamic line ratings, power flow control). The IEA's numbers sit side by side: 2,500 GW queued, 1,600 GW releasable in the near term, 750-900 GW available through non-firm connections. Half the bottleneck is institutional, not a question of generation.

Bottom line

The ceiling on compute is being redefined by the power system, and the question has shifted from how much electricity exists to how quickly it can be connected. For AIDC investment and site selection, move three checks to the very start of due diligence: queue position and expected energisation year, whether non-firm connection terms are acceptable, and whether local rules allow demand response in exchange for capacity. As for nuclear PPAs, book them as options for after 2030 — that is not power that will light up racks in 2027.

Frequently asked questions

Why is grid connection slower than GPU delivery?

The IEA counts more than 2,500 GW of projects stuck in connection queues, and high-voltage connection waits exceed 7 years in some G7 jurisdictions. GPU supply is scheduled in quarters, power access in years, so the energisation date usually decides when a project really starts.

What is a non-firm connection?

The grid operator may curtail a data centre's load under pre-agreed trigger conditions to handle congestion or system disturbances. The IEA estimates the mechanism could unlock an additional 750-900 GW of grid capacity worldwide; the price is that compute workloads must be interruptible and migratable.

Does an SMR offtake agreement mean nuclear power has been bought?

No. The 45 GW figure is a pipeline of conditional offtake agreements — a long-dated option that still needs siting, licensing, construction and grid connection. The IEA also notes that PPAs actually signed in H1 2026 amount to 45 TWh, mostly conventional renewables.
