Xeon 6+ Agent Density: What Procurement Should Verify

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

Xeon 6+ puts agent density in a prominent position: a single liquid-cooled rack can support 36,864 cores at about 100 kilowatts of rack power, which Intel calls its “leading agent-density rack”; one socket carries up to 288 E-cores (efficiency cores); and official performance data include up to a 79% gain in performance per watt. The three figures come from a rack configuration, a socket specification and a comparative test, each with its own framing. Reading them as a single thing makes it easy to mismatch processor models, racks and your own workload. The following separates the applicable boundary of each figure against the official product page and the relayed exhibition reporting, with 21 November 2026 as the as-of date.

What: Three things said together

According to the Intel official Chinese product page (checked as of the baseline date), the Xeon 6+ is the first Intel 18A processor for the data centre, with up to 288 E-cores (efficiency cores) per socket plus more last-level cache and higher memory bandwidth. On the same page Intel says a single liquid-cooled rack can support 36,864 cores at about 100 kilowatts of rack power, calling it its “leading agent-density rack”. The official SKU table lists four models: the 6990E+ at 288 cores, 576MB and 450W; the 6980E+ at 264 cores, 528MB and 400W; the 6970E+ at 192 cores, 480MB and 400W; and the 6960E+ at 144 cores, 432MB and 330W; all marked for release in the second quarter of 2026 on socket FCLGA7529. According to a China International Import Expo display reported by Phoenix and Tencent on 5 November 2026, a single processor can stably support more than 1,000 agents; this is vendor framing relayed by media. The same report notes that Intel 18A combines RibbonFET and PowerVia. These facts belong to four categories, product specification, rack example, performance comparison and relayed display, which are easier to understand together but only usable for decisions when separated.

Why: Why the three framings must be separated

The 288 cores per socket describe the upper limit of core configuration for one processor and correspond to the 6990E+ in the SKU table; the other three models have 264, 192 and 144 cores, so 288 does not cover every model. The 36,864 cores and about 100 kilowatts describe one specific liquid-cooled rack configuration example, and the official page does not write it as a guaranteed value for every rack; how many processors a rack holds and which tier they are determines the final core count. The 79% performance-per-watt gain is an official comparison with a stated comparator, the Xeon 6 with E-cores, and carries footnote 9G29. Merging the three into one figure loses the model differences, the configuration premise and the comparison conditions at once. Procurement needs to establish which layer it belongs to before taking the matching figure.

Who: Who should read by model and who by rack

Anyone selecting a single processor should start with the specific SKU: the 6990E+ offers 288 cores, 576MB of last-level cache and 450W, while the 6960E+ has 144 cores, 432MB and 330W, and all four tiers differ in core count, cache and power. Anyone planning racks should check whether a rack can reach 36,864 cores with the about 100 kilowatt power and cooling conditions of the official example, restoring the example to a configuration before judging it. Anyone concerned with efficiency should look at the comparator behind each figure: 79% performance per watt (Xeon 6 with E-cores, footnote 9G29), 2.5 times performance (Xeon 6 E-core, footnote 9D23) and a 9:1 server consolidation ratio (second-generation Xeon, footnote 9W20). The page explicitly states that “results may vary”. Different responsibilities map to different figures, and mixing them overstates the benefit.

When: Launch, display and verification dates

The official SKU table marks all four models for release in the second quarter of 2026. Phoenix and Tencent reported the agent-support claim from the China International Import Expo display on 5 November 2026. The as-of date of this piece, 21 November 2026, is when the site checked the official page and that reporting; it is neither the product launch date nor the date of the display. Launch date, reporting date and verification date each constrain a different fact, and substituting one for another strips the figure of its time premise. Procurement records should list all three side by side so a later review can judge whether a claim still holds.

Where: Specification page, rack or exhibition floor

The 288 cores and the cache and power figures of the four SKUs sit in the official product specification table and can be checked item by item; the 36,864 cores and about 100 kilowatts sit in the official description of the liquid-cooled rack as a configuration example; the 79%, 2.5 times and 9:1 figures sit in the footnote system of the official performance data as conditional comparisons; and the statement that a single processor can stably support more than 1,000 agents sits in media reporting of an exhibition display as vendor framing. The four layers differ in certainty and must be cited with their source. Turning exhibition framing into specification-page framing is the most common overreach in procurement material.

How: Steps for checking the figures

First, fix the model: choose a tier among the 6990E+, 6980E+, 6970E+ and 6960E+ and record the core count, last-level cache and power. Second, restore the rack configuration: confirm the processor count and the power and cooling conditions of the target rack, then judge whether the 36,864-core and about 100-kilowatt example applies; if it does not, recalculate against the actual configuration. Third, read the footnotes one by one: 79%, 2.5 times and 9:1 correspond to 9G29, 9D23 and 9W20, with the comparator and baseline conditions recorded together and the “results may vary” note retained. Fourth, handle AET: Intel offers Intel Application Energy Telemetry for real-time insight into CPU energy consumption and activity with application-level visibility, which can be included in verification as an observation tool. Fifth, flag the agent figure separately: more than 1,000 agents is vendor framing relayed by media with no published measurement method, so keep it as an item to verify rather than a settled conclusion.

Boundaries: The three figures cannot replace one another

Three boundaries need to be stated. First, 36,864 cores is an official liquid-cooled rack configuration example, and the page does not write it as a guaranteed value for every rack; the actual core count depends on the processor models installed and their quantity. Second, 79%, 2.5 times and 9:1 all carry footnotes and comparators, and the page also states that “results may vary”, so these figures describe results under specific comparison conditions and cannot be taken as general gains once the conditions are dropped. Third, Intel Application Energy Telemetry provides real-time insight into energy consumption and activity with application-level visibility; it aids observation and is not itself an energy-saving commitment. The claim that a single processor can stably support more than 1,000 agents has no published measurement method, is vendor framing relayed by media, and can only be kept as a display claim awaiting verification.

Conclusion: Align the framings before buying

In the official material for the Xeon 6+, 288 cores is a socket specification, 36,864 cores and about 100 kilowatts are a rack configuration example, and the 79% performance-per-watt gain is a footnoted comparison, each with its own scope. Procurement needs to align them to one question: which tier is the target model, how is the target rack configured and in what range does your own workload sit. AET provides a way to observe and the agent figure provides a display clue; both need further verification before entering a conclusion. Checking item by item against the model, the configuration and the footnotes is more useful than memorising any single prominent number.

Frequently asked questions

Do all Xeon 6+ models have 288 cores?

No. In the official SKU table the 6990E+ has 288 cores, the 6980E+ 264, the 6970E+ 192 and the 6960E+ 144. 288 is the per-socket maximum for the top tier; selection must check the core count, last-level cache and power of the specific model.

How should 36,864 cores be read?

It is an official configuration example for a single liquid-cooled rack, paired with about 100 kilowatts of rack power, which Intel calls its “leading agent-density rack”. The page does not present it as a guaranteed value for every rack; the actual core count depends on which processor models and how many are installed, so procurement should recalculate against its own rack configuration.

Can AET be used directly as proof of power savings?

No. Intel Application Energy Telemetry provides real-time insight into CPU energy consumption and activity with application-level visibility; it is an observation tool. It makes energy data visible but is not itself an energy-saving commitment and cannot replace a footnoted comparison such as the 79% performance-per-watt gain.
