45 Robots Enter the Post Office, Supply Efficiency Jumps to 1500 Items per Hour

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

In early September, a batch of new colleagues appeared at the small parcel feeding station of the Hangzhou postal district centre. They do not drink and do not change shifts; 45 Xingdong M7 robots lined up on the production line to take on the job of putting small parcels onto the sorting line. According to official news from Xingdong Epoch on 25 November, the scheme’s supply efficiency was 1200 items per hour during testing, and is now above 1500. The number on the counter jumped a notch, and it is worth more than any demonstration video.

What Kind of Scale Is 45 Robots?

China Post Hangzhou branch drove this deployment together with Xingdong Epoch and Woneng Embodied (Shenzhen), with 45 Xingdong M7 robots moving into the Hangzhou postal district centre to handle small parcel feeding. Officials call it the first large scale cluster application of embodied intelligence in logistics. Before this, Xingdong Epoch robots had already gone into scaled service across eleven sites in eight cities in the single province of Guangdong, forming a cross regional footprint. Putting 45 machines into one postal district centre is a completely different matter from one robot waving on a stage: the first means work station allocation, backup for failures and charging schedules; the second only needs a camera angle.

The Counter Jumps From 1200 to 1500

The numbers come from two points in time: the initial test and the present. The scheme finished deployment and testing in early September 2026, with supply efficiency at 1200 items per hour; by the time the official message came out the figure had passed 1500 per hour. Efficiency moved up over two months, which means the cluster still had room to settle in after it started working. But both numbers refer to supply efficiency, the speed of putting small parcels onto the line. They are not whole site throughput, not sorting accuracy, and not the labour cost saved. Reading supply efficiency as total site capacity is the most common misreading of this kind of news.

How Much Does One Robot Actually Do?

Spread 1500 items across 45 robots and each does about 33 items an hour. That is an estimate: the official message does not disclose work station allocation, nor does it mean every unit is running at full load. Change the time scale and an 8 hour shift at 1500 items per hour comes to about 12000 items. That is also an estimate, not a published shift output. Estimates are useful because they break a cluster figure that sounds large down to the scale of one machine and one shift. Estimates have limits too, because on a real line a few machines are always charging and a few are waiting for repair. As of 26 November 2026, the only figure that can be checked is supply efficiency.

Why the Feeding Stage

Small parcel feeding is the most repetitive, smallest motion stretch of the logistics line: grasp, turn, put down at a set spot. It does not need a robot to run or jump, nor to understand complex instructions, but it does need stable repetition over long hours. For embodied intelligence, that kind of stage is a reasonable way in. Putting robots first into work stations with room for error is more honest than promising sorting, packing and hauling all at once. The skill of doing a backflip on a stage may matter less at the feeding table than eight straight hours without a mistake.

Who Benefits in This Ledger

The structure of the three party deal is worth noting. The postal side supplies the scene and the acceptance standards, the robot side supplies the equipment and tuning, and the embodied intelligence side supplies on site integration. The same efficiency figure means different things to the three. The post office cares whether feeding holds steady through a shift; the equipment maker cares whether 45 units can turn into repeatable orders; the integrator cares whether problems on site can settle into a standard process. Count everyone’s gain as the same number and an engineering trial reads like a commercial explosion. Who benefits, who accepts, and who bears the loss when the line stops: those are the real questions of this ledger. That also explains why the same 1200 items per hour is proof of capability in the equipment maker’s release, but only a work station parameter on the post office acceptance sheet. The number did not change; the position of the person reading it did.

Cluster and Single Machine Are Different Questions

One robot reaching 1200 items per hour and 45 reaching 1500 together have three gates between them: scheduling, division of work and backup for failures. Cluster efficiency is not single machine efficiency multiplied by the count; coordination cost usually eats a piece of it. The figures released this time give no single machine baseline and no difference between full load and low load, so it is hard to judge how good the scheduling actually is. This is not fault finding; it is a reminder that the ruler for comparison has not been set up yet. Future reports of this kind should give two sets of numbers at least, one for a single machine and one for the cluster. Without a single machine baseline there is no way to tell how many of the 45 are really contributing; without low load data there is no way to tell whether the system turns into a pile of floor space when orders swing. The larger the cluster, the higher the price of those two missing sets.

Acceptance Needs One More Layer of Detail

To judge whether such a project really works, supply efficiency alone is not enough. Ask also how long it runs without stopping each day, the share of abnormal items, how often a human takes over, the mean time between failures per unit, and how long recovery takes after a stop. None of those figures is in the official message, which means disclosure is still at the stage of ‘does it run at all’. Running is step one; running for a long time is step two; running cheaper than a person is step three. The distance between those three steps is often longer than the distance from a demo to a production line. Here is a concrete way to calculate: add up purchase, depreciation, charging, maintenance and exception handling costs for the 45 units, divide by the number of accepted items fed during that period, and you get the labour replacement price per item. That figure has not been disclosed, so no one can claim it is already below the human cost. The phrase about the first large scale cluster deployment in the official message describes an event, not a conclusion.

Where Else the Counter Will Appear

The biggest value of this project is that it pushed the measure of embodied intelligence one notch, from whether it can move at all to ‘how many items per hour it feeds’. That does not conflict with leaderboard scores or funding valuations, but it is closer to the line that appears on a real purchase order. What comes next is whether the counter in this Hangzhou postal district centre can be reproduced at other sites: same equipment, same process, a different warehouse, a different shift, and still 1500 items. If it can be reproduced, that is scale; if not, 45 robots are just 45 robots. The rhythm of a postal scene is regular, and parcel shapes and weights are fairly fixed, which makes it easy for robots to start and narrows the range where the conclusion applies. Move to a warehouse with more mixed goods and messier work stations and it is an open question whether the same counting logic still holds. The counter will keep jumping; the question is how far we dare to read it. Getting a machine onto the production line is harder than making it move on a stage; keeping it feeding steadily for months is harder than the day it went in. Neither difficulty has a number to answer it yet.

Frequently asked questions

Is 1500 items per hour the sorting capacity of the whole site?

No. The official message explicitly says supply efficiency: the speed at which small parcels are fed onto the sorting line at the small parcel feeding stage. It is not whole site throughput, and no sorting accuracy or labour saving figures were disclosed. Treating supply efficiency as sorting capacity overstates how much of the process the system actually covers.

Are all 45 robots running at full load?

The official message does not disclose work station allocation or how many units are on duty. Dividing 1500 items per hour by 45 gives about 33 items per robot per hour; that is a calculation from public figures, not an official per unit capacity, and it cannot show how well the cluster is actually scheduled.
