Three Rounds at the Hundred Million Yuan Level in 48 Hours, and the Money Bypassed the Whole Robot

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

Three checks were written in two days, and not one of the recipients builds a complete robot. In the 48 hours from 23 to 24 November, Octopus Dynamics, Aoyi Technology and Yinghansi Dynamics successively closed rounds at the hundred million yuan level. The three companies have been around for anywhere from ten months to ten years and entered from different angles, yet they share one striking trait: none builds a general purpose bipedal humanoid robot. Put these three checks on the same table and they look less like three funding stories than a signpost pointing to where capital is genuinely willing to pay. Where hot money goes often says more than where it has left.

Read What the Three Checks Bought

Read first what the three checks were for. Octopus Dynamics raised several hundred million yuan again this year, with new investors including Xindongneng Investment and Midea Capital, while early backers such as Linear Capital and Xiaomi continued to add; the money goes mainly to building a high end manufacturing line for dexterous hands and to research on embodied models. The company states plainly that it does not make complete robots, but bundles dexterous hand hardware, embodied large models and manipulation data into one piece of software hardware co design. Aoyi Technology closed a several hundred million yuan Series D, jointly invested by Zhongbing Shunjing, Yangtze Capital and Optics Valley Industrial Investment, its second round this year, with funds going to expand end effector capacity and data collection. Yinghansi Dynamics closed a Series B of over one hundred million yuan led by CAS Investment, putting robot power and physical AI technology into tennis ball machines and sports training systems. Three companies: one builds a hand brain integrated manipulation platform, one makes dexterous hands already shipping in volume, one makes hit hardware for a vertical scene. Connect the uses and the money is heading for the same place: not a complete robot doing backflips on a display stand, but links where the accounts can be worked out and cash flow collected.

Why No One Is Betting on the Whole Robot

To understand where this money went, look first at the current state of the complete robot business. For the past two years, investors talking about embodied intelligence defaulted to general purpose humanoid robots: whose robot can do a backflip, who pushed the price under one hundred thousand yuan, who folded laundry at a launch event again. But the track is already crowded, with shipment numbers and price wars starting at the same time, while the material cost, yield and scenario fit of a single machine are all still climbing a learning curve. Capital pulling back into components and vertical scenes is not a loss of faith in robots; it is a search for a foothold that is easier to verify. A polished complete robot demo struggles to answer whether customers will buy again; a dexterous hand that has already sold tens of thousands of units answers directly with a shipping list. On a factory line, what decides whether the customer pays is whether the arm puts a part into the fixture at the right angle, whether the torque on a screw is enough, and whether the whole thing still holds after hundreds of repeats. That work happens at the last workstation of the line, and steady legs cannot substitute for it. Betting on the hand is betting on the link that truly decides payment. The choice looks conservative, but it is more pragmatic than betting on the whole robot.

What Each of the Three Routes Bets On

Spread the three out and you find they are not betting on the same thing, but on three different sides of the same thing. Aoyi bets on the water seller route: no chase for complete robots, the dexterous hand made into a standard part, five series and more than twenty SKUs sold to nearly 200 companies, and anyone building a complete robot may become a customer. Octopus Dynamics bets on the middle layer: bundling hardware, embodied large models and manipulation data into software hardware co design, working against a year end mass production and delivery schedule, aiming to serve several robot body makers at once. Yinghansi bets on the vertical hit product route: avoiding the narrow bridge of bipedal humanoids, putting its own power, sensing and control technology into tennis robots and training systems, and taking real user data and overseas channels first. The cards differ, but the thing each must prove is the same: can they sell the goods and collect the money. This also explains why capital makes no choice among the three: they sit at different positions on the same chain, and the bet is that the chain as a whole will rise, not that one complete robot maker will win.

Open the Shipping Lists

Among the three, the numbers already shipped speak most clearly. Aoyi Technology was founded in 2015; its founder Ni Hualiang was an engineer at Qualcomm in his early years, and the company spent long years grinding away at brain computer interfaces and bionic hands. It now holds five series and more than twenty standard SKUs, with a self built plant in Zhuhai at a capacity of ten thousand units a year. According to TMTPost, in the first half of 2026 its dexterous hand orders crossed the ten thousand unit mark, serving nearly 200 embodied intelligence companies worldwide, with full year shipments aimed at tens of thousands. Yinghansi Dynamics Tenniix tennis robot raised over one million dollars in overseas crowdfunding, set a record for the sports category on the Japanese platform Makuake, and sold tens of thousands of units through its own site and Amazon. The difference between shipping numbers and a demo video is that a video can be filmed on the best attempt, while a shipping list has to send units out one by one and bring the money back. Capital entering at the high point of a Series D is looking not at the imagination in a slide deck but at that shipment table. Turn whether something is worth money into how many units were sold and how many customers are served, and it is more convincing than any technical narrative. This is the hardest logic in this funding round.

Industrial Capital Brings More Than Money

Few of the investors writing checks this round are financial investors chasing valuation gaps alone. Midea, Zhongbing Shunjing, CAS Investment, Yangtze Capital and Optics Valley Industrial Investment carry industry and state capital in their very names. What they bring is not just funds but scenarios, manufacturing capability and supply chains. Take Octopus Dynamics: as of 25 November 2026, Midea has planned tens of billions of yuan of investment in fields such as embodied intelligence, and its Welling robot components unit is expanding precision reducer capacity. For a hand brain integrated company, the home appliance and industrial manufacturing system means workstations and real operating data that can be plugged into at once. Among Aoyi investors, the Zhongbing system corresponds to special operations scenarios, while Yangtze Capital and Optics Valley Industrial Investment correspond to local manufacturing clusters in Hubei, and orders and scenarios often land together with the capital. One thing must be clear: industrial capital entering is both an opportunity and a constraint. The orders and data are real, and so are the demands on delivery pace and cost control. Take industrial capital money and you work to the rhythm of industry, not the rhythm of a demo. Where the money comes from decides how it must be paid back.

What It Means When Parameters Go Into the Announcement

One detail is worth noting: funding announcements are starting to put hard metrics on display. The dexterous hand of Octopus Dynamics is disclosed as bionic tendon driven, with 23 degrees of freedom across the whole hand, 28 motors, more than 1,900 sensing units in the tactile system, millimetre level array density, 1,000 failure free hours, and repeat positioning accuracy no worse than 0.1 millimetres. In the past, talking about a dexterous hand meant saying it can hold an egg or turn a screw; now the language is counts of degrees of freedom and sensing units, positioning accuracy and failure free hours. This change matters: the terms of competition have shifted from whether it moves at all to whether precision, lifespan and batch consistency can be accepted by the customer. For buyers, these parameters for the first time let dexterous hands from different vendors sit in one table for comparison. For vendors, writing parameters into an announcement sets up a promise that can be questioned. The next step, whether joint position feedback is stable and whether temperature drift and durability survive a long shift, will be compared item by item alongside motors, reducers and sensors. Making parameters public is a sign that a component is becoming a mature supply item.

The Losses on the Other Side of the Ledger

Still, the other side of shipments and funding must be laid open too. According to the Hard Tech Funding Daily, Aoyi Technology booked revenue of 31.0775 million yuan in 2024 with a net loss attributable to the parent of 7.5349 million yuan, and revenue of 24.9534 million yuan in the first half of 2025 with a net loss attributable to the parent of 2.6572 million yuan. Octopus Dynamics is less than a year old, its products are not yet in mass production, and whether its year end delivery target is met depends on three tracks: building the production line, managing the supply chain and customer validation; a delay in any one pushes revenue recognition into 2027. These figures are a reminder that a round at the hundred million yuan level buys future capacity and research spent in advance, not present profit. To turn the funding into a return, ask three things: can shipments rise along with complete robots, can the unit price hold, and can costs come down. A flood of new players entered the dexterous hand track this year, price competition has begun, incumbents with ten thousand unit capacity and hundreds of customers must defend share, and newcomers must offer comparable metrics on precision, lifespan and cost at the same time. Behind the noisy funding lies a hard fight over who sells first and collects the money.

The Signal Worth Watching

Bring these three checks down to one signal: the funding announcements put 23 degrees of freedom, 0.1 millimetre accuracy and 1,000 failure free hours on display as hard metrics. What matters most to component suppliers is that parameters can now be taken by customers and compared item by item. In the past a customer asked whether a robot moves and whether the demo looks good; now they can ask how many degrees of freedom, what positioning accuracy, how long it runs without a fault, and then put several vendors on the same sheet. A complete robot demo cannot produce a parameter list that can be checked line by line. The turn in capital follows the plainest business logic: money goes where the final payment can be collected. The hand in the workshop is already turning screw after screw, while the robot on the display stand is still doing backflips. Once parameters can be compared item by item, a component supplier quotation finally has something comparable to sit next to.

Frequently asked questions

What direction do the three funding rounds point to?

According to TMTPost, within 48 hours Octopus Dynamics, Aoyi Technology and Yinghansi Dynamics closed rounds at the hundred million yuan level, and none of the three builds a general purpose bipedal humanoid robot. They are betting respectively on a hand brain integrated manipulation platform, dexterous hands already shipping in volume, and hardware for a vertical scene. The shared direction is that capital is turning away from the complete robot story toward core components and vertical hit products where the accounts can be worked out, delivery can be accepted and cash flow can be collected.

Why is it easier for components than for complete robots to get money?

Because components have shipments and parameters that can be checked. Aoyi Technology passed ten thousand dexterous hand orders in the first half and serves nearly 200 embodied intelligence companies, Yinghansi Dynamics has sold tens of thousands of tennis robots, and Octopus Dynamics wrote 23 degrees of freedom, 0.1 millimetre positioning accuracy and 1,000 failure free hours into its announcement. On a factory line, what decides whether the customer pays is whether the arm puts a part into the fixture at the right angle and whether the torque on a screw is enough; these parameters map directly to whether the customer pays the final installment, while a complete robot demo struggles to answer whether anyone buys again.
