What does usable mean for a record: quality criteria and a testable acceptance line

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

Data quality in component libraries is often translated into field fill rate, but what actually hurts downstream engineers is never an empty field — it is a complete-looking, already expired conclusion: status says Active, package says available, compliance says conformant, and the page never says on which day or against which document. An empty field makes people stop and check; a full one makes them place an order. Conclusion first: usable means the value can be traced to original evidence, its time and version can be stated, and the judgement made then can be reproduced. All three are required.

What: quality is checkability, not fill rate

Split usable into four checkable properties: the value, its source, the collection and verification time, and the document version it relied on. The official Part-DB documentation lists an event log, per-user change tracking and rolling parts back to older versions as built-in capabilities, alongside parametric search, multiple currencies, attachments and an API. Together they say something basic: in this class of system, who changed what to what and when is more fundamental than how many fields exist. For public component master data add one more property — whether a third party can reproduce the conclusion. A cross-reference that only says replaceable, with no conditions, validation method or original basis, creates more risk in a BOM than no record at all, because it stops people looking for a second source.

Why: data decays over time and an enum cannot hold that

Component state is not a static label. Lifecycle moves through Active, NRND and EOL, and official notices may be revised or reactivate a part number; material declarations lapse when regulatory lists are updated; distributor prices and stock are snapshots. Storing a time-dependent conclusion as a single enum preserves only what it looks like now, not why it was written or when it stops holding. This is why an event table matters more than a status field: each change appends an event with time, source and basis, and the old conclusion is retained rather than overwritten. Compliance data is the sharpest case — a PDF saying conformant without a bound list version and file hash stays on the page after the list changes, yet reads like a promise that is still valid today.

Who: entering, reviewing and using are three different responsibilities

The person entering data owns where the value came from, the reviewer owns whether it still holds, and the downstream BOM user owns whether it applies to their context. These cannot be collapsed into one person or one field: source tier, verification state and applicability are different dimensions. Manufacturers and distributors publish facts; an open library only relays them and must state that role explicitly, otherwise a line saying conforms to REACH reads like the library own compliance guarantee. Permission design should reflect this too: the fine-grained permissions, user groups and two-factor authentication in the official Part-DB feature list suit separating who may edit values, who may change state and who may publish to a public interface. Even a one-person team should leave those three actions as separate log entries.

When: four moments that must leave a trace

At write time, record source and collection time. When an upstream list or regulation changes, trigger a review instead of waiting for a complaint. When a manufacturer publishes a PCN or PDN, generate a new event and keep the old conclusion. When a downstream BOM is imported, re-fetch affected part numbers, because the import moment is when the judgement actually takes effect. The four moments correspond to four failure modes: wrong entry, upstream change, notice revision and downstream use of a stale version. Logging only the first gives you a system that looks audited but was accurate only at the beginning. In practice the last-verified time also needs a visible staleness threshold: records not reviewed within a set period should be labelled on the page rather than silently continuing to look trustworthy. Uncertainty belongs on the interface, not hidden in the database.

Where: four layers, each storing its own kind of thing

The field layer holds current values and units; the evidence layer holds original files — PDFs, notice numbers, URLs — and file hashes; the event layer holds time, source type, operator, before and after values, and the basis version; the display layer decides whether to show last verified, source tier and possibly stale. Compressing all four into one wide table is convenient for short-term queries and guaranteed to cause trouble later: you cannot detect a swapped evidence file, nor mark a batch as dirty when a list version changes. Layering has a practical benefit too — licensing can act only on the evidence and display layers. For a file you may not redistribute, keep only the link and hash in the database and do not serve the original, while the value remains usable. That keeps quality and licensing from holding each other hostage.

How: a quality line you can write on an acceptance form

Step one, require the four-part set for every record — value, source tier, collection time, verifier — and keep incomplete records out of public interfaces. Step two, replace status enums with an event table; the current state shown on a page is a projection of the latest event, and history remains reversible. Step three, for high-risk fields such as compliance and lifecycle, additionally store list version, notice number and file hash, and mark batches dirty for review when a list updates. Step four, define a testable acceptance line: sample thirty publicly visible records at random, and each must lead back to original evidence within two steps and state the date on which the conclusion held; if they do not, that batch does not ship. This line does not demand perfection at scale, but it turns usable from an adjective into a check that either passes or fails.

Boundaries: three things a quality definition cannot replace

Checkability is not compliance: a fully evidenced record may still be one you are not allowed to distribute. Verified is not a legal guarantee either; the library is a relay and applicability always belongs downstream. Full verification is unrealistic for a small team, so effort must be graded by material risk, concentrating on fields that affect discontinuation, safety and export controls rather than applying one process to every field. One more easily missed point: the acceptance line tests whether you can get back to the evidence, not whether the evidence itself is correct. Manufacturer notices get revised too; all a system can do is record the revision as an event, not guarantee the manufacturer will not change its mind.

Put usable into the product, not into the README

Three things to ship: make the four-part set a required constraint so incomplete records never go live; show last-verified time and source tier on the page so uncertainty is visible; make the thirty-record sample a fixed pre-release step and keep the results. After that, users no longer see a pile of tidy-looking fields but a verifiable statement: where this value came from, when it held, and whether it can still be trusted. For a component library, that checkability is what engineers can depend on over years — and it is considerably harder to copy than any coverage number.

Sources and qualifiers (baseline 11 November 2026): Part-DB event logging, version rollback, parametric search, fine-grained permissions, 2FA and distributor enrichment come from its official documentation site. The observations on lifecycle revisions, reactivated part numbers and material declarations lapsing with list updates draw on publicly available manufacturer PCN/PDN pages and industry practice (manufacturer notices are official documents; the practice synthesis is third-party). REACH, IPC-1752 and IEC 62474 are mentioned only as common frameworks for compliance declarations; no interpretation of their provisions is offered. The thirty-record sample, the four-part set and staleness thresholds are engineering proposals in this article, not requirements of any standard.

Frequently asked questions

If every field is filled, why is the record still not usable?

Without source, time and version, an expired conclusion looks exactly like a valid one. Usable means returning to the original evidence, stating when it held and reproducing the judgement made at that time.

Why insist on an event table instead of a status field?

A status field only stores what is true now, not why or from when it stopped being true. Lifecycle notices get revised and part numbers are reactivated; only appended events preserve that history.

What if a small team cannot verify everything?

Grade by material risk: build evidence chains first for fields affecting discontinuation, safety and export controls; for the rest, show source tier and verification time. Making uncertainty visible is safer than pretending to full coverage.
