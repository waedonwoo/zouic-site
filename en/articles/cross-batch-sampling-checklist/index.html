After used-GPU grading: keeping sampling consistent across batches

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

This is a hands-on checklist you can follow and that leaves evidence, and it solves one very concrete problem: once a batch of used GPUs has passed inspection, how do you make sure the next batch, and the one after that, are judged with the same ruler. A single batch passing is not the same as consistency across batches; as soon as the sampling ratio, the criterion version or the reviewer has quietly changed, different batches may each be using their own standard while all of them are marked “pass” in the ledger. An everyday analogy helps: measure lengths with a ruler whose graduations differ from batch to batch, write “qualified” on every slip, and only when you try to compare the batches side by side do you find they cannot be compared at all. This article works through six things — applicability, preparation, steps, records, review and exception handling — so a reader can follow along and leave the evidence behind. Conclusion first: consistency only holds if “what to sample, which criterion version, who reviews it and how exceptions roll back” is written down as a reproducible record.

What: applicability — the conditions under which this checklist should be used

The first thing is to judge applicability, so the checklist is not used in a setting it does not fit. This cross-batch sampling applies where the same class of used GPUs arrives split across several batches, is inspected by several people or at several stations, and the criteria may be adjusted over time; it answers “are the batches still being judged by the same standard”, not “is this particular card any good”. A few terms need defining first: PDN is a product discontinuation notice, PCN is a product change notice, and in component specifications the two are different kinds of notice; they are borrowed here only for the idea that “a notice needs an identity, a date and a version”. A claim worth arguing against: the cross-batch consistency problem is not caused by careless inspection but by the fact that “criteria can be swapped out silently”, so the checklist’s first goal is to lock the criteria down, not to add one more test.

Why: preparation — why a single passing batch is not enough to prove consistency

Before starting to fill in records, get the things to prepare in order and state the reasoning plainly: a single-batch report only proves that this batch passed under the criterion version then in force; it cannot prove that the “pass” in that document means the same as the “pass” in another batch. Four things are needed: a criterion document with version numbers, a rule for batch tracking numbers, a fixed sampling ratio and station arrangement, and a ledger template that keeps raw records and review signatures. The requirements JEDEC JEP30-S100A places on the product discontinuation notice PDN can be borrowed as a method: the notice needs its own independent tracking number, the last order date and the last shipment date are mandatory fields, the reason for discontinuation and the version level must be given, and suggested replacement parts may be included. It must be stated explicitly here that all of the above is the framing of component notification standards, not a used-GPU trading standard; it is a methodological borrowing, not an industry rule. A claim worth arguing against: when preparation is done properly, consistency no longer depends on a veteran inspector’s memory but on a template anyone can read.

Who: who samples, who judges, who reviews, who approves a change

Roles have to be fixed first; otherwise, however complete the records are, they cannot be traced back to a person. There are at least four roles: a sampling operator who draws samples at the fixed ratio and stations, a reader who gives the conclusion under the current criterion version, a reviewer who signs off on the raw records and the conclusion, and a change approver who is responsible for activating and retiring criterion versions. The reviewer should not be the same person as the reader, and change approval should not simply be co-signed by the sampling operator. A judgment worth arguing with: recording review as “someone looked at it” is meaningless; review has to come down to a checkable signature and date before a cross-batch dispute can be answered — “who judged this batch and under which version” and “who approved that version for use”. Writing these roles into the header of the ledger template is far more reliable than leaving them as a verbal agreement.

When: the sequence of steps — at which point each one is done

What follows are steps you can actually carry out, in the order they happen. Step one: define the batch tracking number and the criterion version, giving each batch a number that can be traced uniquely and stating which criterion version that batch is judged under. Step two: fix the sampling ratio and the stations, writing down how much is sampled and at which stations. Step three: keep raw records and review signatures for every batch, with raw readings neither recopied nor overwritten, and a separate column for the review date. Step four: when the criteria need to change, run the old and new versions in parallel for one cycle; during that period record the conclusion for the same batch under both versions rather than switching over directly. Step five: set exception rollback and re-inspection thresholds, making clear which situations send the work back one step and which trigger a re-inspection of the whole batch. Step six: before export, check that every “pass” record carries a version and a date. A claim worth arguing against: of these six steps the one most often skipped, and the one that least should be, is step four — without a parallel period a criterion change becomes a break that cannot be attributed.

Where: where records land and where the evidence is kept

Records need a fixed home; otherwise “leaving evidence” turns into material scattered in different places that nobody can gather in full. Three locations, each with its own job, are recommended: the batch ledger holds each batch’s tracking number, arrival information, sampling ratio, raw readings, conclusion and review signature; the criterion version repository holds the full text of every criterion version, its effective date, the reason for change, the approver and the replacement relationships; and the exception and re-inspection record gets a separate column for the rollback reason, the re-inspection threshold and the outcome. Here the component-notification idea is borrowed once more: JEDEC J-STD-048 (the product discontinuation notice standard, which supersedes JESD48) requires timely notice of a planned discontinuation, while the product change notice PCN is governed by J-STD-046, and PDN and PCN are two kinds of notice that must not be mixed. Mapped onto this case, that means the “criterion version change record” and the “batch suspension or recall record” should be stored separately, not served by one form doing two jobs. A judgment worth arguing with: where the evidence sits decides whether it can be found in a dispute; mixing the three kinds of record into one file leaves no entry point for review.

How: turning the checklist into reproducible actions and handling exceptions

The key to turning all of the above into actions is the word “reproducible”: hold to three hard rules when executing. First, every single “pass” record must carry at the same time the batch tracking number, the criterion version number, the sampling ratio, the reader, the review signature and the date; if any one is missing it does not count as complete and must not be exported. Second, a criterion change goes through “old and new in parallel for one cycle”; within the parallel period the two conclusions are set side by side and no average is taken, and only at the end is the old version retired with the effective date of the new one noted — which matches exactly the idea that PDN and change notices must not be mixed. Third, exception handling needs clear thresholds: if a batch is missing its review signature, if raw readings have been overwritten, or if a “pass” record carries no version and date, the work goes back to the corresponding step and is redone, and re-judging happens only once the re-inspection threshold is reached; patching a hole afterwards with a “pass” is not allowed. A claim worth arguing against: the enemy of consistency is not the occasional bad card but those harmless-looking back-filled entries and proxy signatures; keeping those actions outside the threshold does more for cross-batch comparability than testing a few more cards.

Boundaries: what this checklist does not promise

The boundaries have to be spelled out so the checklist is not mistaken for a quality guarantee. This article promises no grading pass rate, gives no numeric value for the sampling ratio, and draws no conclusions about warranty obligations or trading rights and duties; the sampling ratio, how strict the criteria are and the re-inspection threshold are all set by the user for their own scenario, and the checklist only governs “whether those settings were recorded and whether they were applied consistently”. The more important point: JEDEC J-STD-048, JEP30-S100A and J-STD-046 belong to the framing of component notification standards, not a used-GPU trading standard. They are cited here purely as a methodological borrowing — what is borrowed is the record-keeping idea of “a tracking number, an effective date, a reason for change, version and replacement relationships, and never mixing the two kinds of notice” — not an application of those standards to the used-GPU trade. A claim worth arguing against: methodological borrowing is not a compliance basis; anyone who uses component standards to assert acceptance duties for used cards is using the analogy in precisely the wrong place.

Conclusion: make consistency a reproducible record

Pulled together into one sentence: cross-batch consistency does not rest on how carefully any one batch was inspected, but on “what to sample, which criterion version, who reviews it and how exceptions roll back” all being written as reproducible, versioned records. Done properly, a “pass” slip is no longer just a conclusion but a chain of traceable fields: which batch it belongs to, which criterion version it rests on, who sampled, who judged, who reviewed on which date, and which step an exception sends it back to. That way, even when criteria change and staff rotate, different batches can still be compared correctly under their own version labels. For the reader, what is delivered is not just a few batches of qualified cards but a way of working that can be followed again next time and checked clearly when a dispute arises.

Frequently asked questions

If every batch is marked “pass”, why does that still not prove consistency?

Because “pass” may come from a different criterion version, a different sampling ratio or a different reviewer. A single-batch report only proves that this batch passed under the criterion version in force at the time; it cannot prove that its “pass” means the same as another batch’s. For consistency, every pass record must carry a batch tracking number, a criterion version number and a date — if any one is missing, it should not be exported.

When the criterion version changes, do records for old batches have to be redone?

No, there is no need to start over, but the old and new versions should run in parallel for one cycle. During that period, record the conclusion for the same batch under both versions, set the two side by side and take no average; only when the cycle ends retire the old version and note the effective date of the new one. That way a criterion change does not become a break that cannot be attributed, and different batches can still be compared correctly under their own version labels instead of having the ruler quietly swapped.

Can the JEDEC PDN and PCN standards be used directly as an acceptance specification for used GPUs?

No. J-STD-048, JEP30-S100A and J-STD-046 are the framing of component notification standards, not a used-GPU trading standard. This article only borrows the record-keeping idea — a tracking number, an effective date, the reason for change, version and replacement relationships, and never mixing the two kinds of notice. That is a methodological borrowing, not an industry rule, and it is not a compliance basis.
