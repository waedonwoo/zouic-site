An open component database at cold start: where the first few dozen records come from

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

The most tempting opening sentence for an open component database is to fill it with data first. What actually stalls these projects is never storage or search but licensing: datasheets, cross-references, lifecycle states and material compliance declarations each have different rights holders, and the answers differ on redistribution, commercial use and inclusion in a downloadable set. Conclusion first: start with the BOM and inventory you already own, label every record with a source tier, and keep licensing status as a separate field. The public part-number catalogue is step two, not step one.

What: cold start delivers a republishable boundary, not a record count

Three things have to be fixed at the outset: the data boundary (which classes of fields you may publish), source tiers (the evidence strength behind each record) and a minimal usable set (when you dare let others depend on it). Commercial component platforms describe scale with phrases like hundreds of millions of datasheets and tens of millions of cross-references — platform-stated coverage, not open data. The reason a yearly subscription feels expensive is precisely that the value comes from curation, licensing and continuous updates, not from the PDFs. If an open project makes coverage its first milestone, it takes on compliance debt and cleaning debt before it can answer whether it has the right to publish a single record. The deliverable of a cold start should be a boundary statement plus a small batch of traceable records, not an empty library with an impressive number.

Why: manage what you have before managing what exists

This order is not conservatism, it follows what the tools are actually good at. Part-DB is an open-source inventory system for electronic components; its official feature list covers categories, footprints, manufacturers, storage locations, prices, tags, attachments, barcodes, projects and BOMs, plus an event log and version rollback. It is built to answer what you own, where it sits and how many boards you can build. It can also enrich part data through distributor interfaces such as Octopart, DigiKey, Farnell, Mouser and TME, and serve as a KiCad data source (official feature list). External data is an enrichment layer, not the layer of truth. Making your inventory and BOM solid first means errors are reversible; building the public catalogue first means one bad record propagates along every downstream BOM.

Who: maintainers, contributors and manufacturers each own a segment

Maintainers define the field model and admission rules, contributors submit records with evidence, manufacturers and distributors are the source of facts but rarely promise open redistribution, and EDA users plus engineers downstream have the lowest tolerance — a wrong footprint or lifecycle state becomes a scrapped board. Existing projects also expose the human risk: Part-DB is released under AGPL-3.0, a third-party assessment describes a modest contributor base with reliance on a single primary maintainer, contributor counts differ between the documentation site and an evaluation site (46 in one, 66 in the other), and verifiable large-scale production deployments are scarce (third-party assessment, not official figures). This is not a verdict that the project is unusable. It is a reminder that maintenance structure is part of the selection decision, as important as field design.

When: order milestones by how checkable they are

Three phases. Phase one is your own data only: import existing BOMs, stock and purchase records with a minimal field set — MPN, manufacturer, package, quantity, location, source link — and call it done when one hundred lines all trace back to an original document. Phase two adds external references: enrich and cache through distributor APIs, recording fetch time and source tier for each record and stating explicitly that a cache is not a licence. Phase three is a public catalogue and a service, which requires answering licence obligations first. For reference, Part-DB 2.20.4 was released on 26 October 2026 and the project is actively maintained (relayed from release records). That activity favours self-hosting, but version upgrades should not become a dependency of phase one; stabilise your own data model first.

Where: four locations with four different levels of authority

Data in your self-hosted instance is your responsibility and can be traced line by line. Symbol and footprint libraries on the EDA side are engineering conventions validated by design. Distributor APIs give a snapshot at one moment: prices and stock change, and interface terms differ. Manufacturer notice pages — PCN/PDN and material declarations — are the highest authority for lifecycle and compliance facts, but are often subscription-based and arrive as PDF or Excel attachments needing manual parsing. Mixing all four into one table with a single source string is the root of later disputes. A safer model gives each field its own source type, collection time, original file link and verification state, and lets the display layer decide what to collapse. Price from a distributor and lifecycle from a manufacturer are two separate records even for the same MPN.

How: four steps to a minimal working loop

Step one, draw the licensing boundary as a table: your own records, openly redistributable material, cite-only material and material you must not use, each marked for whether it may enter a downloadable set. Step two, tag every record with a source tier — manufacturer PDF, distributor API, user entry, inferred value — and never let inferred values drive downstream decisions. Step three, use distributor interfaces only for enrichment and caching, with expiry and refetch policies, never as the single source of truth. Step four, make licensing status a field rather than a note, and filter exports and public APIs by it, so if a class of data later has to be withdrawn you can process it in bulk instead of re-auditing the whole library. After four steps you have a first release you can explain publicly, and scale can grow slowly.

Boundaries: three things off-the-shelf tools do not solve

Part-DB is an inventory system, not public component master data; treating it as the foundation for an industry-wide catalogue hits a model boundary quickly. AGPL-3.0 permits free and commercial use, but offering a modified version as a network service carries source-disclosure obligations whose application depends on your own deployment — do not conclude anything from the word open source alone. The concentrated contributor base and the shortage of documented large-scale deployments noted by third-party assessments are maintenance risks rather than functional defects, but they belong in the plan of a one-person company building for the long term. Finally, coverage figures, subscription prices and cross-reference counts from commercial platforms are platform statements or user reports with inconsistent definitions; they cannot be used to benchmark progress or compute savings.

The first milestone can be very small

Define version one as one hundred of your own BOM lines, each with a source link, a collection time and reversible history. That is more useful than one hundred thousand part numbers: it can be checked by others, maintained by you and trusted by downstream decisions. Then add distributor enrichment, then a public catalogue. Three things to ship: publish the licensing boundary table; make source tier a field instead of a note; put event logging and rollback at the top of the feature list, because data will be entered wrongly and being able to undo matters more than being complete on day one. The moat of an open component database was never volume — it is that every conclusion leads back to evidence.

Sources and qualifiers (baseline 11 November 2026): the Part-DB feature list, AGPL-3.0 licence and multi-language/multi-database support come from its official documentation site. The 2.20.4 release dated 26 October 2026, contributor counts and maintenance-structure assessment come from third-party catalogue and evaluation sites (46 and 66 respectively, listed side by side because the definitions differ). InvenTree star counts come from a third-party ranking and only indicate ecosystem size. The commercial platform figures of hundreds of millions of datasheets and tens of millions of cross-references are platform statements, and subscription fees are user reports; neither was independently verified and neither is used in any calculation here. For licence obligations, consult the licence text and your own deployment; this article is not legal advice.

Frequently asked questions

Can we scrape a commercial database first and sort out licensing later?

Not advisable. Datasheets, cross-references, lifecycle states and compliance declarations have different rights holders and different redistribution terms. Scraping first buries takedown cost and compliance debt inside the data model.

Can an AGPL-3.0 project be used for a commercial service?

The licence permits use and commercial use, but offering a modified version as a network service carries source-disclosure obligations whose application depends on your deployment. Check the licence text and get professional advice where needed.

How big should the first usable release be?

Define it by checkability: one hundred of your own BOM lines, each with a source link, collection time and reversible history. Scale can grow later; the evidence chain has to exist from day one.
