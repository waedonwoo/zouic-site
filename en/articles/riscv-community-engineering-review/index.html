RISC-V Progress: Commits Beat Launch Events

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

Progress in RISC-V over the past two years is easy to misremember the more roadmap slides one sees at launch events. What can actually be checked are the commits in repositories, version numbers and reproducible build results. openRuyi, CORE-V, XiangShan, RevyOS and spacemit-robotics each have a batch of checkable commits in early November, and GigaDevice updated the page for its GD32V series in November as well.

A batch of verifiable commits and version numbers

The openRuyi main repository raised the kernel to 7.3.3, LTS to 6.18.62 and glibc to 2.44 on 8 November and added x86_64 to testing. CORE-V CVW merged 20 commits on 7 November, removed two sets of local branch predictors and BTB parameters, and published RV32GC CoreMark/MHz 4.33. XiangShan difftest had five commits on 7 and 8 November centered on closing out the matrix extension, with the matrix completion signature changed to CRC32C. RevyOS merged 20 commits at once on 9 November, unified six platforms under debian_defconfig and added cross-architecture and native build CI. spacemit-robotics opened the ros2_spacemit_vslam repository.

Commits are quarterly progress, not product launches

Why read commits as quarterly progress? A commit records an engineering action: raising a version, deleting a parameter, changing a signature, adding CI. It shows that code is moving and tests are being added, but code moving does not mean there is a product to buy. The RV32GC CoreMark/MHz 4.33 published by CORE-V is a score for a specific core under a specific configuration, used to compare microarchitectures, and is not the performance of a particular chip under real workloads. Treating it as a product performance metric is like taking a single reading on a health form as the conclusion about the whole machine. A launch event gives targets, a repository gives what has already happened, and they differ in certainty.

Who commits, who aggregates, who ships

The sources need separating. openRuyi, CORE-V, XiangShan, RevyOS and spacemit-robotics are project parties whose commits come from their own repositories; the RISC-V daily aggregates these commits, a tier 2 aggregation channel that is convenient for viewing together but is still a relay. GigaDevice is a chip vendor, and information about the GD32V series RISC-V MCU comes from an official page updated on 13 November, marked industrial grade with at least ten years of supply and paired with the free Nuclei Studio IDE and the GD32VF103V-EVAL evaluation board, also checked as tier 2. Project parties give commits, aggregators give an index, vendors give products that can be purchased, and the roles should not be swapped.

Commit dates cluster in early November

The timing is concentrated in early November. CORE-V CVW was on 7 November, openRuyi on 8 November, XiangShan difftest on 7 and 8 November, RevyOS on 9 November and the GigaDevice page update on 13 November. These dates show engineering activity clustering within one week, a slice of a period rather than a snapshot of a single day. The as-of date of 22 November 2026 is the checking point, later than all these commits; it only means the site checked the public records on that day and does not mean there has been no new activity since. Treating one week of commits as the verdict for a whole quarter stretches the time span.

Progress sits in repositories, not at launch events

Where progress sits determines whether it can be rechecked. The openRuyi versions and x86_64 testing sit in the main repository; the CORE-V 20 commits, removals and 4.33 in the CVW repository; the XiangShan matrix commits and CRC32C in the difftest repository; the RevyOS debian_defconfig and CI in its own repository; ros2_spacemit_vslam is a newly opened repository. The GD32V industrial grade, ten-year supply and evaluation board information sits on the GigaDevice official page. Repositories and official pages can be opened and checked directly, while roadmaps often appear only in presentation material; different resting places carry different credibility.

Use builds, tests and reproducibility as hard metrics

To read community progress, three indicators can be checked on the spot: it builds, it tests, it reproduces. It builds means the code can be pulled and compiled per the instructions, it tests means the test set produces a record, and it reproduces means someone else following the same steps gets the same result. These three, more useful than counting launch slides, are the hard metrics of community maturity. Read the commit record as a health report, not as publicity copy: temperature, blood pressure and lab results can be measured on the spot, while a roadmap is a wish written for oneself. openRuyi adding x86_64 to testing and RevyOS adding cross-architecture and native build CI are efforts toward these three, while CORE-V removing two sets of local branch predictors and BTB parameters is converging the implementation rather than piling on features. Only when all three pass does community progress land on a layer that can be relied on.

Neither scores nor roadmaps enter definite conclusions

CoreMark/MHz 4.33 is a published score for a specific RV32GC core and is not product performance; it cannot be applied across cores, configurations or workloads. Commit counts are not maturity in themselves: RevyOS merging 20 commits at once and CORE-V merging 20 commits show only that changes are dense, not that functions are stable. XiangShan changing the matrix completion signature to CRC32C is an adjustment to test methods, not proof that the matrix extension is productized. Roadmaps, expected mass production dates and future targets are lower-grade framings; under the source grading, project repositories and official pages are tier 2 and aggregation and relay do not upgrade to definite conclusions. Writing checkable commits as product launches is the most common overreach in this kind of review.

Read commits, not launch events

In early November openRuyi raised the kernel, LTS and glibc and added x86_64 to testing, CORE-V CVW merged 20 commits and published RV32GC CoreMark/MHz 4.33, XiangShan difftest had five commits closing out the matrix extension, RevyOS merged 20 commits at once to unify six platforms and add CI, spacemit-robotics opened a new repository, and the GigaDevice GD32V page was updated on 13 November. What can be verified are the commits and version numbers, not the roadmap. CoreMark/MHz 4.33 is a score for a specific core, not product performance. To judge community maturity, the hard metrics of builds, tests and reproducibility are more reliable than watching launch events.

Frequently asked questions

Can CoreMark/MHz 4.33 be taken as the performance of a RISC-V chip?

No. The 4.33 is the RV32GC CoreMark/MHz score published by CORE-V CVW on 7 November, a microarchitecture indicator for a specific core under a specific configuration used to compare designs, and not the performance of a product under real workloads. It cannot be applied across cores, configurations or workloads. This score is checked against public repository information and comes from a tier 2 source, but it is a score framing rather than a product framing and does not enter definite conclusions.

How can you tell whether a RISC-V community is really making progress?

Look at repository commits and version numbers, not launch roadmaps. Items that can be checked include openRuyi raising the kernel to 7.3.3, LTS to 6.18.62 and glibc to 2.44 and adding x86_64 to testing, RevyOS adding cross-architecture and native build CI, and XiangShan changing the matrix completion signature to CRC32C. Harder metrics are that it builds, it tests and it reproduces: the code compiles when pulled, the test set produces a record, and someone else following the same steps gets the same result. Dense commits show only that changes are happening, not that functions are stable, and roadmaps and expected mass production dates do not count as verifiable progress.
