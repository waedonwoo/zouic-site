Before charging for content, decide what is actually being sold

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

Debates about whether AI should pay for content usually skip the important question: what event is being charged for. A successful crawl, an appearance inside an answer, and a content licence are three different products with different triggers, different owners of the unit definition and different reports coming back. Calling all of them content payment makes sites think they are making one decision. Conclusion first: the smallest unit a small publisher can verify itself is the crawl, so build that ledger; models where the platform defines the unit remain experiments until the calculation detail is published, not revenue lines.

What: three models sell three different events

Pay Per Crawl charges per successful retrieval: the site sets a domain-wide per-request price, a crawler without payment intent receives a 402 with pricing, and a signed retry with accepted terms returns 200 and completes the charge, with allow, charge or block set per crawler (official Cloudflare blog). Pay Per Use charges per appearance: the Ceramic.ai partnership pays when content appears in its AI search results and returns queries, citations and ranking, while the Google AI contribution pilot pays when content contributes significantly to a Gemini, AI Overviews or AI Mode answer, with significance defined by Google and confirmation or linking after generation excluded (media accounts of pilot terms). The Microsoft Publisher Content Marketplace sells a licence: publishers define licence and usage terms and receive usage-based reporting, with Copilot as the first demand partner (February 2026 announcement). One sells retrieval, one exposure, one rights.

Why: the old exchange is broken, but the break is often misread

Two observations drive this. First is re-fetching: Cloudflare says over 50 percent of AI crawler traffic re-fetches pages that have not changed, so publishers pay bandwidth and AI companies pay compute with no better answers (platform statement). Second is the crawl-to-referral gap: frequently quoted platform figures put Anthropic at roughly 11,122 crawls per referral, OpenAI at 1,700 to one and Google at about five to one (platform figures relayed by media). These show that per-crawl pricing would also bill worthless re-fetches; they do not prove that per-citation pricing is fairer, because that hands the unit definition to the platform. What a site actually needs to repair is the absence of first-party records: without knowing who fetched which paths and what was returned, no model can be reconciled at all.

Who: pricing, definition and settlement belong to three different parties

The site sets the price and the allow/charge/block policy. In Pay Per Crawl the platform acts as Merchant of Record, aggregating charges and paying monthly. Crawler operators must accept the price and implement signatures and payment-intent headers. In appearance-based models, whether a significant contribution or a valuable appearance occurred is defined by the platform, and the site sees monthly earnings without the calculation detail. In licensing marketplaces the publisher defines terms, but the number of demand partners is small, which favours large publishers with premium content. Third-party comparisons add a practical constraint: the major AI labs have not publicly announced support for Pay Per Crawl, so a site that selects charge may simply be excluding crawlers with no billing relationship. The official blog explicitly permits that configuration, and in effect it behaves as a denial.

When: pilots, private betas and shipped features are not the same thing

Pay Per Crawl has been in private beta since 2025 and a third-party roundup from July 2026 still listed it as private beta. In September 2026 Cloudflare announced its evolution into Pay Per Use, naming Ceramic.ai and You.com as partners — a direction, not general availability. Google AI contribution pilot is invitation-only, and a September 2026 comparison still described it as a learning phase with unpublished terms and calculations. The Microsoft marketplace was announced in February 2026, with Yahoo onboarding at announcement. Even the price floor conflicts: one media account gives a 0.001 US dollar minimum while another third-party roundup says 0.01 US dollars. Do not average them; check the live console. Treating a beta quote as a forecast turns an experiment into a contract.

Where: quoting, settlement and evidence live on different layers

Quoting happens at the edge: the 402 response carries a price, clients can send crawler-max-price as a ceiling and crawler-exact-price as the accepted price, and a successful response returns crawler-charged (official documentation and protocol examples). Settlement happens in the platform ledger, usually as a monthly aggregate without per-request detail, so the platform statement must reconcile with origin logs. Licensing happens at the contract layer, with its own terms, usage scope and reporting. Only the fourth layer belongs entirely to the site: the origin request log, recording signing identity, declared purpose, path, response code, content version and timestamp. Without it, whatever the platform reports is the only number; with it, a specific disagreement such as a bill that does not match what was served becomes possible.

How: start from the smallest verifiable unit

Step one, build a first-party ledger that any platform can be checked against: timestamp, path, signing identity, declared purpose, response code, content version and whether it was billed. Step two, classify content by path — editorial pages that need discovery, premium data pages, and archives that should not be reused — and set allow, observe or deny per class instead of one site-wide price. Step three, if running a charging experiment, use the successful crawl as the smallest verifiable unit because it is countable, reproducible and reconcilable line by line with origin logs; defer contribution-based pricing until the calculation detail is available. Step four, document conflicting figures internally, including the price floor, pilot scope and invitation-only status, so third-party summaries are never mistaken for platform commitments. After these four steps you are measuring how your content was used, not how many dollars arrived this month.

Boundaries: three conclusions the current evidence does not support

You cannot conclude that enabling charging produces revenue: the major labs have not announced support for the billing protocol, so for most crawlers charge acts as a denial. You cannot conclude that citations are worth more than crawls: significance is platform-defined and reporting omits the calculation, so the site cannot recompute the amount however large it looks. You cannot conclude that a licensing marketplace suits the long tail: it serves content with bargaining power, while a small site is better served by getting visibility and attribution right first. The crawl-to-referral ratios also come from one platform network and were relayed by media; the sample is not the whole web and your own ratio may be entirely different. Put it in the denominator of a business plan and you have budgeted using somebody else traffic mix.

Ledger first, price second

Reversing that order costs you any basis for review: without first-party logs, every quote and every statement must be accepted whole. Three things can be shipped: make request logs reconcilable line by line with the monthly platform report and retain content versions; write allow/observe/deny per path with a recorded reason for each so policy changes are traceable; treat charging as an experiment with a stop condition, for example returning a path to observe if it produces zero paid requests in two weeks rather than leaving it blocked. The value of content being used will ultimately be argued from checkable facts; if you cannot win back the definition of the unit, at least do not hand over your own records as well.

Sources and qualifiers (baseline 11 November 2026): the Cloudflare Pay Per Crawl blog and the September 2026 Agents Week press release are official platform statements. The over-50-percent re-fetch figure and the crawl-to-referral ratios are platform network measurements relayed by media. The Google AI contribution pilot, the Microsoft Publisher Content Marketplace and the side-by-side comparison of the three models come from a third-party comparison published on 18 September 2026 (media account). The conflicting 0.001 and 0.01 US dollar price floors come from two different secondary sources, are not reconciled in official documentation and are listed here side by side rather than used. Private-beta status and the absence of announced support from major labs come from a July 2026 third-party roundup. All of these are pilots and imply no revenue expectation.

Frequently asked questions

Is a 402 response an invoice?

No. It is a quote and a payment-intent handshake: the site states a price and the client may retry with an acceptable ceiling. Actual charging and settlement happen in the platform ledger, and the site usually sees a monthly aggregate.

What happens if a crawler with no billing relationship is set to charge?

The effect is close to a denial. The official blog explicitly allows a publisher to choose charge for a crawler it cannot bill; nobody pays and no content is returned. Reading it as a revenue switch is a common misreading.

Which model should a small publisher try first?

Per-crawl metering and denial rules, because the unit is countable and can be reconciled line by line against origin logs. Contribution-based pricing is worth observing only once the calculation detail is available; licensing marketplaces suit publishers with premium content.
