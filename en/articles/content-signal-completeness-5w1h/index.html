Write all three signals or leave one blank: absence is not refusal

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

Adding a Content-signal line to robots.txt looks like filling in one more configuration item, but it is actually an outward statement about what may be done with content after it is taken. This policy text defines three purpose terms, and defines something equally important alongside them: when a use is not written into that line, the meaning is not a prohibition but the fact that the site has not expressed a preference about that use through this file. The 5W1H breakdown of how many of the three fields to fill, who interprets them and who verifies them follows. Conclusion first: filling them all is a clear posture and leaving one blank is a clear posture too; only reading a blank as a prohibition, or reading a declaration as a gate, are two misreadings pointing in opposite directions but costing the same.

What: three text segments and three purpose terms

The Cloudflare Content Signals Policy is written into robots.txt as a comment, in three segments. The first gives the interpretation rule: a content-signal of yes means content may be collected for the corresponding use, no means it may not, and if a site gives no signal for a use, it neither grants nor restricts permission through that signal. The second defines the purpose terms: search means building a search index and serving search results (returning links and short snippets), and explicitly excludes serving AI-generated search summaries; ai-input means feeding content into one or more AI models, for example retrieval augmentation, grounding or other real-time use of content to generate an answer; ai-train means training or fine-tuning models. The third is a reservation of rights: any restriction expressed through Content Signals is an express reservation of rights under Article 4 of EU Directive 2019/790. A site can then state its own preference on a single comma-separated line, for example search=yes, ai-train=no. The most easily skipped sentence in all three segments is precisely the third one of the first: the semantics of an omitted signal are neither prohibition nor tacit permission, but no position stated here.

Why: moving preference from whether to crawl to what content may be used for

robots.txt traditionally answers only which paths an automated client may request, not how content may be used after it is accessed. The official blog states the motive bluntly: fetchers take content without sending back convertible referral traffic or attribution, leaving sites with only two extremes — lock the content away or accept a free ride — so a machine-readable place to express use preferences is needed. That motive also sets its capability boundary: the same developer documentation says robots.txt compliance is voluntary, the file expresses a preference but does not prevent crawling, and enforcing a block requires edge crawler-control capabilities. The real function of the three purpose terms is therefore not access control but compressing positions previously scattered across terms, commercial negotiations and legal letters into one line that a machine can parse and a counterparty can cite. Compression brings consistency and risk alike: when downstream reads that one line as already authorised or already blocked, a site often notices only much later, because the two sides are simply not looking at the same layer of the system.

Who: who writes the line, who writes it for them, and who explains it

The site operator ought to be the one writing this line, but a managed service may write it for them. The official blog says explicitly that for customers using managed robots.txt the platform supplies search=yes, ai-train=no and deliberately omits an ai-input signal, on the grounds that it does not know the customer preference for that use and will not guess on their behalf; the wording stresses that leaving ai-input blank does not mean the customer has no preference, only that they have not used this part of the file to express it. The developer documentation shows that managed content currently defaults to search=yes, ai-train=no, use=reference, where use is a fourth field under test whose values immediate, reference and full correspond to no retained reuse, may index, quote and link back, and may summarise and reproduce. The three roles are therefore: the platform sets a default, the site makes the decision, and legal and commercial teams explain what this line means in a contract; whether crawler operators recognise it is entirely outside site control. If a site fills in all three fields itself, for example search=yes, ai-input=yes, ai-train=no, it is expressing allow real-time use, do not train explicitly, which is not the same thing as the platform default with its deliberate blank.

When: declarations take effect slowly and the fields are still changing

The policy text was published in 2025, the managed robots.txt developer documentation page is marked updated 2 November 2026 and says managed content appears within seconds of saving a policy with no cache purge needed; but crawlers may cache robots.txt for up to 24 hours, so an irreducible gap sits between changed on our side and read on their side. The fields themselves are still evolving: the three purpose terms are relatively stable, while the fourth field, use, is marked by official documentation as an extension under test and must not be written into acceptance criteria or public commitments. One more kind of time cost comes from false alarms: Search Console occasionally reports a new directive such as Content Signals as unrecognised syntax, and the platform documentation states no observed effect on crawl rate or SEO. Taken together, a sensible review rhythm is to check the full text once when a policy changes, re-fetch it externally after 24 hours, and re-confirm the defaults whenever the platform documentation is updated, rather than treating a screenshot from one moment as lasting evidence.

Where: where the declaration, enforcement and interpretation layers sit

The declaration layer is the comment policy text in robots.txt plus that one value line; it does two jobs at once, defining the purpose terms and expressing the preference, and under RFC 9309 group-matching semantics it is visible only to clients that read the group containing it — give each AI crawler its own dedicated group and leave the declaration only in the wildcard group, and those named clients will not see this segment. The enforcement layer sits in the edge WAF, crawler control, gateway and authentication, and turns not allowed into an actual refusal or a charge. The interpretation layer sits in terms files, contracts and legal positions; the reservation of rights citing Article 4 of the EU Directive is written as a comment in the third segment precisely because it addresses people, not only parsers. The fact layer is the origin log: which client identifier a request carried, what content was returned, and whether it later reached a summary or training set — HTTP cannot see that last part. Of the four layers, declaration and enforcement are the ones most often merged, and the fact layer is the one most often left out.

How: turn three decisions into one review table

The first decision is whether each of the three purpose terms gets a value or is deliberately left blank: a blank must be written into the internal notes so it is not later misread as forgotten to configure. The second is which group the declaration goes into: with a single wildcard group, write it directly there; with several groups prepended by a platform, check group by group in the final full text whether the declaration covers the target clients, and confirm that the AI crawler roster has not slipped out of the declaration group through dedicated groups. The third is who enforces it: for a stance such as ai-train=no, map it explicitly to an edge policy or a contract clause rather than leaving only a line of text. At release, make four things assertions: the full text actually served contains the complete policy comment and the value line; the status is 200; the three field values match the internal decision table; and the change time and version number are written to the log. On review, fetch the full text once more from an external viewpoint and keep a 24-hour observation window, so old behaviour inside the cache period is not mistaken for a regression.

Boundaries: what this one line cannot guarantee

Content Signals is not a specification published by a standards body, and parsers handle non-standard directives inconsistently; it is an expression of preference and a reservation of rights, provides no technical enforcement, and cannot prove whether a client has read it or used the content as declared. The discussion in third-party technical write-ups that a declaration is not a gate and the official platform documentation saying compliance is voluntary are two ways of saying the same thing, both pointing to enforcement having to happen elsewhere. The use field under test in particular must not be written into public commitments. One more framing difference is worth keeping side by side: the platform managed default deliberately leaves ai-input blank, while a site that fills in all three fields itself expresses a more explicit stance; neither is right or wrong, but mixing them makes internal and external audiences see different postures. Finally, the declaration does not cover where content flows after delivery — what happens in an index, a summary or a training set needs contracts, platform relationships and after-the-fact reconciliation, not one line of configuration.

Conclusion: state the posture clearly and complete the enforcement

Start with an internal decision table: for each of search, ai-input and ai-train, write yes, no or leave it blank, with a reason noted for every blank. Then publish that line together with the complete policy comment into a group that every target client can read. Then map each no to an edge policy or a contract clause so the declaration has somewhere to land. In external communication, say honestly that this is a declaration and not a gate, and do not present a field under test as an existing capability. That way the site loses nothing even if the other side never reads the line at all; and once someone does read it and comes to discuss licensing, the site can at least produce a position it has signed itself, clearly dated and matching the live file word for word.

Frequently asked questions

If ai-input is not written, does that forbid using content in real time?

No. The policy text states explicitly that when a site gives no signal for a use, it neither grants nor restricts permission through that signal. The official platform blog adds that leaving it blank does not mean the site has no preference, only that it has not used this part of the file to express one.

If ai-train=no is written, will training crawlers be blocked?

Not automatically. Compliance with robots.txt is voluntary and the declaration expresses a preference rather than enforcement; actually stopping them requires a matching policy in edge crawler control, the WAF or the authentication layer, with origin logs used to verify the real effect.

Must all three fields be filled in?

No, but the choice has to be deliberate. Filling all three is a clear statement, and deliberately leaving one blank is a clear statement too, provided the decision is recorded internally and the public full text matches the internal decision table. The worst case is an accidental blank inherited from a template default that is later read as a different stance.
