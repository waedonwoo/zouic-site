HBF Fills a Capacity Tier, Not an HBM Replacement

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

The most common misreading when HBF enters procurement is treating it as a cheap stand-in for HBM. The first HBF open specification, jointly released by SK hynix and SanDisk at FMS 2026 and published through OCP, places HBF in the capacity tier between HBM and conventional enterprise SSDs, aiming at a large-capacity warm-data layer with bandwidth close to HBM, and expressly not an HBM replacement. Specification values, sample timings and third-party cost estimates sit in three different kinds of material with different certainty; collapsing them into one conclusion removes the preconditions from a procurement decision. For procurement, what matters is at which tier and which timing HBF can be verified as a capacity layer, rather than whether it can cheaply replace HBM.

What the HBF open specification sets out

Under the HBF open specification jointly released on 4 August 2026 at FMS 2026 and published through OCP, HBF uses 8-layer or 16-layer NAND stacking, up to 512GB per package, bandwidth from 0.4 to 3.0 TB/s split into three grades, Grade 1 to Grade 3, and a UCIe interface. The specification puts power consumption at about 60% lower than DRAM stacking. The positioning is explicit: HBF fills the capacity tier between HBM and enterprise SSDs, serves warm-data scenarios with bandwidth close to HBM, and does not take on the replacement role for HBM. The existence of grades means there are different performance positions under one specification, so an inquiry must land on a specific grade. These are target values and grades in a specification text, not measured results from a shipping part, and a citation should name its source.

Why specification values, samples and estimates cannot be mixed

A specification value describes a design target and a grading method, telling you where capacity and bandwidth are written. A company statement about silicon out, samples and commercial years describes industrial progress, telling you when something might be testable. A Bernstein figure for cost per GB and wafer consumption is an analytical model indicating a directional cost picture, without commercial validation. Certainty falls across the three. Treating a specification target as a spot parameter is like treating a design drawing as a delivery note. Reading HBF as an HBM stand-in is like moving a warehouse high shelf into a kitchen as a worktop: however large the shelf and however many its layers, it cannot replace the speed of a wok. Procurement should ask about capacity tier, bandwidth tier and interface form, and when these things can actually be verified. Skipping these questions and comparing unit prices directly only compares target values and estimates under different framing, and yields no executable selection conclusion.

Who publishes, who has silicon, who makes estimates

The publishers are SK hynix and SanDisk, which jointly released the first HBF open specification at FMS 2026 and published it through OCP; the specification text is Tier 1 material. The silicon progress comes from SanDisk, which says the first HBF silicon has come out, with AI inference samples targeted for 2027 and commercial availability in 2028 to 2029; this is a company statement at Tier 2. The cost and wafer consumption figures come from a Bernstein analytical model at Tier 4, directional only. Judgments about the medium must return to NAND read latency, write behavior, endurance and thermal limits, which are properties of the medium rather than promises from any company. The roles determine how each is cited: a specification sets the framing, a company statement sets the schedule, and an analytical model only shows direction.

Specification, sample and commercial timings

Timings must be recorded separately. 4 August 2026 at FMS 2026 is the specification release node; SanDisk saying the first HBF silicon has come out is a current progress statement; AI inference samples targeted for 2027 and commercial availability in 2028 to 2029 are company expectations, not delivery dates. Specification release, silicon out, sample targets and commercial expectations are four different things, the first two closer to checkable facts and the last two conditional plans. The as-of date of 22 November 2026 is the site checking time, not the occurrence time of these nodes. Writing an expected year as a delivery year is the most common bias in this kind of topic.

Which layer of material carries each claim

Where a claim sits determines how far it can be used. The 8 and 16-layer stacking, 512GB, 0.4 to 3.0 TB/s in Grade 1 to 3, the UCIe interface and the roughly 60% lower power sit in the OCP-published HBF open specification; silicon out and the 2027 and 2028 to 2029 cadence sit in SanDisk framing; 0.64 dollars per GB against 6.78 dollars per GB and about 6.5 times TLC NAND wafer consumption sit in the Bernstein analytical model; read latency, write behavior, endurance and thermal limits sit in NAND medium properties. A citation should keep its source in the same sentence; specification clauses and relayed estimates must not be swapped. The four kinds of material answer different questions, and mixing them removes the boundaries from a conclusion.

What procurement should ask first

Procurement can move through four questions. First, the capacity tier: is the target the 16-layer scheme with 512GB per package or an 8-layer scheme, and how do usable capacity and layer count map to the workload. Second, the bandwidth tier: 0.4 to 3.0 TB/s is split into Grade 1 to 3, which tier does the business need, and is that tier testable at the sample stage. Third, the interface form: does UCIe connect to the existing system, and is extra bridging or a controller required. Fourth, the delivery node: samples targeted for 2027 and commercial availability in 2028 to 2029, and whether the evaluation window covers that period. If the workload is warm-data inference, also confirm that the data movement path from the capacity tier to compute stays within interface capability. A parameter sheet answers only part of the first three questions; the fourth requires checking sources.

Estimates and medium limits stay out of firm conclusions

Several boundaries need to be kept. Bernstein estimates about 0.64 dollars per GB for 8-layer HBF, about 6.78 dollars per GB for HBM4, and about 6.5 times TLC NAND wafer consumption; these are analytical model results without commercial validation, Tier 4 directional information that does not enter firm conclusions. Power about 60% lower than DRAM stacking is a comparative figure from the specification with a comparison object and conditions, not a general gain. The 0.4 to 3.0 TB/s range is a grading target, not a measured value from any sample or shipping part. NAND read latency, write behavior, endurance and thermal limits are inherent constraints of the medium and do not disappear because a specification was published. Sample and commercial years are expectations, and a plan is not a delivery. Once conditions are dropped, a directional estimate reads like a price promise, the most common way a cost figure loses its meaning.

Keep three kinds of framing in separate accounts

What can be confirmed is that HBF has a specification text, a company progress statement and a third-party cost estimate, answering design targets, industrial progress and directional cost respectively, and that these cannot be added or used to prove each other. HBF is positioned in the capacity tier between HBM and enterprise SSDs, aimed at warm data with bandwidth close to HBM, and is not an HBM replacement. Procurement should first ask about capacity and bandwidth tiers and the UCIe interface, then check sample and commercial nodes, and finally mark cost and wafer estimates as directional. This sequence also applies to any future capacity-tier product. Keeping three kinds of framing in separate accounts is closer to something executable than memorizing striking figures such as 512GB or 0.64 dollars per GB.

Frequently asked questions

Can HBF replace HBM?

No. The HBF open specification released by SK hynix and SanDisk at FMS 2026 places HBF in the capacity tier between HBM and conventional enterprise SSDs, targeting a large-capacity warm-data layer with bandwidth close to HBM, and expressly not an HBM replacement. HBF trades some latency and endurance characteristics for capacity, so procurement should ask about capacity and bandwidth tiers and the UCIe interface rather than expect a like-for-like swap for HBM.

Can the bandwidth and capacity in the specification be bought today?

Not as a shipping part. The 8 or 16-layer stacking, 512GB per package, 0.4 to 3.0 TB/s in Grade 1 to 3 and UCIe interface are target values in the specification text. SanDisk says the first HBF silicon has come out, with AI inference samples targeted for 2027 and commercial availability in 2028 to 2029. Specification values are design targets, and the sample and commercial years are company expectations rather than delivery dates.

Can Bernstein cost estimates go into a procurement conclusion?

No. Bernstein figures of about 0.64 dollars per GB for 8-layer HBF, about 6.78 dollars per GB for HBM4 and about 6.5 times TLC NAND wafer consumption come from an analytical model without commercial validation. They are Tier 4 directional information and should be recorded as such, not written as a firm price or a verified cost advantage.
