158 Metrics Measure Robots, and Open and Closed Source Differ by 18.4 Points

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

1113 questions tested 16 models, and Kling 4.0 scored 53.32. On 24 November, BAAI released the REAL physical AI evaluation system; according to the Humanoid Robot Brief, it covers 5 major capability domains, 14 capability dimensions and 158 metrics. On the first world model leaderboard, closed source models averaged 48.7 and open source models averaged 30.3, a gap of 18.4 points. One exam paper put every model in front of the same set of questions, a first for robotics: capabilities each vendor once described in its own words finally had a common sheet to be measured on.

Start by Spreading Out the Exam

For the past two years, robot capability was shown mostly through video. In one clip a robot does a backflip, folds a shirt, walks a few steps like a person, and a wave of amazement follows. But video has a flaw it cannot get around: it records only the successful attempt. How many times it failed, and whether it still works in another room under different light, the viewer never sees. REAL turns a one off performance into an exam that can be repeated, compared and reproduced: 5 major domains, 14 dimensions, 158 metrics, and a paper of 1113 questions. This step matters to an industry. Without a common set of questions, anyone can claim to be strong and buyers can only judge whose video looks better; with one, models can finally be compared side by side. Real progress is often not that one robot suddenly learns a new move, but that the industry at last agrees on one way to measure it.

How to Read the 158 Metrics

The first reaction to the number 158 is that it is too fine. For physical AI, though, fine is necessary. What a robot does is not a multiple choice question but a chain of perception, judgement and action: reading the scene is step one, judging the relations between objects step two, planning the action step three, carrying it out steadily step four. If any link drops, the task fails. Breaking capability into 158 metrics is like fitting a readout onto every link of that chain. Take a task such as putting a cup on the second shelf: object recognition, judging spatial distance, controlling grip force and planning an obstacle free path all sit behind it. Look only at success or failure and you get a black box number; split it by metric and you learn which step failed. That matters for research and purchasing: the team knows which link to fix, and the buyer knows this exam paper measures the scene they actually care about, not some pretty composite score. More metrics are not automatically better; what counts is that each one maps to an observable action. The real value of 158 is that the question of whether a robot can do the work finally has something you can follow up on item by item.

Add a Body of Data to the Paper

For an evaluation to stand on its own, a leaderboard alone is not enough; it needs data too. As of 25 November 2026, released alongside the leaderboard were the open sourced HumanCOIN first person observation, intention and action dataset, plus Wujie Innata-0 and Physis-V1-Preview. There is a clue here that is easy to miss: first person data is exactly the kind world models and embodied intelligence lack most. What a robot must learn is the whole chain of what a person sees, what they intend and how the hand moves, while most traditional internet video is third person and lacks precisely the link between intention and action. Open sourcing data like this lays one more foundation stone for the whole industry. The leaderboard writes the questions and scores them; the data feeds the models. Both must land before evaluation can serve research and purchasing. What will show its value later is not how many metrics were published today, but how many teams actually use its questions for purchasing and its data for training.

Where Does the 18.4 Point Gap Sit

The leaderboard gives concrete numbers: closed source averages 48.7, open source averages 30.3, a gap of 18.4 points, with Kling 4.0 leading at 53.32. That gap deserves to be taken apart rather than swallowed as a blunt verdict that open source loses. Spread over 1113 questions, it means that in a fair share of them the two kinds of model do not answer at the same level of quality. Where the gap comes from, the leaderboard does not say in full, but combined with the nature of physical AI a few directions show up: understanding and predicting the physical world, where open source models usually have less data and compute behind them; stability over long task sequences, where closed source models make fewer mistakes in complex scenes; and access to real machine data, precisely the part the open source camp finds hardest to make up. Put 18.4 points into words a buyer can use: for the same task, choosing an open source model may mean one extra round of validation and one extra fallback plan. Scores are not a vanity metric; they translate into how much extra engineering deployment will cost. But this 18.4 point gap is the gap on this paper, not the whole of the capability gap. What the paper does not test, from adaptation to durability to batch consistency, will also decide how a model behaves on a production line.

What the Leaderboard Cannot Reach on a Real Machine

Treating a leaderboard score as real machine capability is the easiest mistake right now. The 1113 questions test ability in a given scenario, while conditions in a real factory vary endlessly: light, temperature, how parts are placed, the required cycle time, each can differ from the standard questions. A leaderboard can answer how a model performs on standard questions, but not whether it will hold up running for eight straight hours on your line. Between the two lies a layer of validation on real machines. According to the Humanoid Robot Brief, the design of REAL includes an analytical comparison across benchmark, simulation and real machine, which shows the designers themselves are aware of this gap. For a buyer, the safe use is a first sieve: shortlist by score, then take your own real scenario and plug it in for a fresh test. The score decides who enters the shortlist; the scenario decides who gets bought. Merge the two steps and even a beautiful score can crash in practice.

What the 158 Metrics Cannot Measure

However fine the paper, there are things it cannot measure. The 158 metrics cover perception, planning and manipulation, but one thing is hard to quantify: how a robot improvises in an unfamiliar scene. Standard questions are fixed, while the real world keeps producing surprises beyond them: a part placed at a new angle, the light suddenly dimming, the cycle speeding up. That is why evaluation needs scenario partners at the table: the more scenarios, the closer the paper gets to reality and the harder it is to game by drilling for points. One reverse judgment a buyer can use: a model that scores high on the standard leaderboard but frequently needs a human to take over in a new scene more likely owes that score to familiarity with the test points than to real ability to work. Conversely, a middling score that steadily finishes tasks in unfamiliar scenes is often closer to what a factory actually needs. The value of a leaderboard is a starting point, not an ending point.

Who Sets the Questions, Who Sets the Price

An evaluation system does not build robots, but it changes where the money flows. In the past, model vendors raised investment and closed deals on demo videos and self reported results. From now on a buyer can point to a leaderboard and a set of metrics and ask how many points this item scored in your scenario. That shifts part of the pricing power from the vendor to the question setter and the scenario partner. For the buyer, the gap on the leaderboard means that with the same budget, open source or closed source carries a different cost of human fallback. For the vendor, scores become for the first time something publicly comparable and usable by customers to argue the price down. Whoever holds the position of setting the questions holds, to some degree, the bargaining power in this business. That is not something technical neutrality can wave away.

Who Writes the Next Exam

Bring the view back to the paper itself: according to the Humanoid Robot Brief, BAAI has teamed up with 17 scenario partners to launch REAL Olympics, pulling real operating conditions into the business of setting questions. What is worth watching from here is who writes the next exam, in which scenario, and whether anyone is willing to base real money decisions on it. If the leaderboard gets written into more purchase contracts, the gap on the paper stops being a line of numbers and becomes a price difference on a quotation; if it stays in papers and press conferences, however fine the exam is, it is only an ornament. One batch of answerers after another has come and gone. Whether the question setters can truly put the scenario into the paper is still an open question.
