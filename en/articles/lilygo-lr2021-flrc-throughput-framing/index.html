2.6 Mbps Is the Pipe Diameter. How Fast Do Your Packets Run?

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

A pipe stamped 50 millimetres does not guarantee that much water an hour from the tap. Water pressure, bends and losses in between all take their share. The LILYGO T-Beam 1W2 store page says 2.6 Mbps, and Semtech's material specifies that as the LR2021 transceiver peak in FLRC-Burst mode. The diameter is written clearly; the outflow is a distance nobody fills in for you.

What did this board change?

The T-Beam 1W2 keeps the ESP32-S3 dual-core Tensilica LX7 host with 16 MB flash and 8 MB PSRAM, plus GNSS, OLED and microSD on board, with 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi and Bluetooth 5 LE. The change is on the radio side: the SX1262 is replaced by a Semtech LR2021 supporting LoRa, FLRC, FSK, OOK, O-QPSK and LR-FHSS at chip level. FLRC is the new part, opening up experiments with larger payloads, such as sending an occasional image. The host handles the application, storage and GNSS data; the radio handles how packets go out. Each has its own job. Tell first which part changed.

Why not copy 2.6 Mbps into the budget

Semtech's 2.6 Mbps is a transceiver peak, and the cited specifications do not establish application-layer throughput or range. That wording draws a boundary: what a chip can do, and how many bytes your application sends per second with the same antenna and distance, are two things. Packet overhead takes airtime, retransmission takes airtime, and what is left is useful data. A GNSS node that occasionally sends an image should measure, with the same payload, antenna and distance, how many application bytes actually arrive per second. Writing the peak into a plan as a promise will show up at the first field test.

Three SKUs — do not buy the wrong radio

LILYGO maps the three SKUs clearly in its PlatformIO configuration: H768 to SX1262, H768-A to LR1121 and H768-B to LR2021. Copying an older board's configuration directly may select a different radio chip, sending the project off course before it starts. For an existing ESP32-S3 project, the most useful first power-on step is to run the new radio target's example, confirm the link, then reconnect the application piece by piece. Reversing that order makes diagnosis much harder. Try whether a key opens the door before moving furniture through it.

What if the frequency numbers do not match?

The store lists H768-B at 830 to 945 MHz, while the board guide gives 840 to 930 MHz for the XY16E3AXP33 RF module. The two figures come from different vendor documents and do not agree. As of 2026-11-28, what can be checked is these two public statements; no more authoritative unified statement is available. This is not about memorising a number, but about confirming the supported range with LILYGO for your exact model before choosing a frequency. Putting both numbers on the purchase order is safer than quietly picking one.

What does FLRC actually change?

FLRC makes room for larger payloads to be tried. LoRa is good at small packets over long distances, trading rate for reach; FLRC takes the other end, suited to sending more data over shorter distances. A sensor node with a camera that wants to send an occasional image previously had to compress it hard or switch to a link such as Wi-Fi. LR2021 gives a third option. But the maker's specification is a chip-level capability; how fast and how far your application runs depends on deployment conditions. The pipe got wider; water pressure and bends are still there.

Is it worth switching from a LoRa design?

If your project sends small packets such as temperature or position, LoRa is already enough and switching to LR2021 brings little. The cases worth switching are larger payloads with flexible range: sending a low-resolution image during inspection, or collecting a batch of sensor readings at a gateway before uploading. Before changing boards, run the real payload once and measure three things: time to deliver one packet, retransmission rate and stable rate at the same distance. Those three are closer to your real experience than any figure in a specification table.

What to watch when flashing and debugging

The board supports Arduino IDE and PlatformIO, with examples from GPIO and UART to Wi-Fi and Bluetooth. For a board with a radio, run the example before connecting your application, especially around the SKU mapping and frequency configuration. GNSS, OLED and microSD are all on board; how they share resources with the radio needs validation in your own application. Do not assume that because the example worked, your configuration is fine. Record each change; when something fails in the field, those records are how you trace it back.

The diameter is written; the water is yours to run

This board pushes radio capability forward and puts the framing question on the table: 2.6 Mbps is a peak, not application throughput; 830 to 945 MHz and 840 to 930 MHz are numbers in two documents, not one promise. Separate those two, and what remains is testing with real payloads, real antennas and real distances. The diameter is printed on the pipe; the outflow you must check by opening the tap. This board suits people willing to take that step, not people who want to copy a rate figure.

Frequently asked questions

Is 2.6 Mbps the board's real transfer rate?

No. The 2.6 Mbps from Semtech is the transceiver peak in FLRC-Burst mode, and the cited specifications do not establish application throughput or range. Packet overhead and retransmission consume airtime; measure the application bytes delivered per second under real payload, antenna and distance.

How do the three T-Beam 1W2 SKUs differ?

LILYGO's PlatformIO configuration maps H768 to SX1262, H768-A to LR1121 and H768-B to LR2021. When migrating from an older board, check the SKU first and run the new radio target's example to confirm the link before reconnecting the application.

The store and guide disagree on frequency. Which to trust?
