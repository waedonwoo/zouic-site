A Chip You Can Verify: How Far Baochip-1x Has Come

Compute industry notes · GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon

GeekWaedon @GeekWaedon · November 2026

Bottom line: Baochip-1x moves the trust boundary in open hardware from readable source to verifiable silicon, but the official wording is mostly-open, not fully open. What can be checked is the compute RTL, the bootloader and the operating system, plus package-level support for infrared in-situ inspection. What still rests on foundry facts is the physical implementation below the TSMC 22nm process. Keeping those two layers apart prevents an interesting engineering effort from being read as a mature procurement path. It is like buying a machine you can open: the screws and the drawings are there, but that does not mean you can retest every solder joint yourself.

What: A microcontroller with the inspection port built into the package

According to the Baochip site and the Crowd Supply project update, Baochip-1x is a microcontroller built on TSMC 22nm. A 350MHz Vexriscv CPU carries an MMU, so virtual memory becomes practical on a small embedded device. Alongside it sits the BIO, a quad-core 700MHz PicoRV I/O coprocessor cluster. On-chip memory is 4MiB of RRAM and 2MiB of RAM, with a TRNG, AES and several hash units integrated. It targets Xous, a microkernel operating system written in Rust. The key design choice is not the parameters but the package: it is prepared for IRIS, infrared in-situ inspection, a noninvasive way to look at the transistor pattern on the silicon and confirm that the part matches the published design. A claim worth rejecting is that open silicon means every layer is auditable. Baochip-1x does not support that reading, and its own documentation says mostly-open.

Why: Supply-chain trust cannot rest on a promise alone

The reason this chip exists is not raw performance. It is to turn the question of whether you received the part you expected into something you can check on the bench. On the official blog, bunnie puts the context plainly: buyers of security chips are often banks and governments, technical evaluation is outsourced, and managers focus on risk; source code in that setting is frequently never read. Baochip pushes verifiability forward: the bootloader is open source and NDA-free, most of the compute logic source is open and NDA-free, and the package supports infrared inspection. The claim to reject is that a vendor promise of openness is enough. If the inspection hook is not designed into the package, auditing stops at the documentation layer and a substitution in the supply chain cannot be detected. What is added here is the gate between the document and the object.

Who: Who this fits, and who should wait

The official positioning and ecosystem define the audience. The Baochip site describes a Rust-first ecosystem with Xous as the reference OS, and a BIO protocol emulator plus BIO-sim, an open RTL simulator, are already available. Researchers, embedded security teams and developers of high-assurance devices who are willing to write drivers for a Rust microkernel and need inspectable hardware are the natural readers. Teams expecting to run Linux directly should first check the software status: the MMU makes Linux technically possible, but official support centres on Xous, and possibility is not delivery. Treating the presence of an MMU as Linux support confuses a hardware capability with a software deliverable, leaving an entire porting project unaccounted for. For line procurement where mature tooling and long-term availability come first, watching is more sensible than substituting.

When: Record foundry timing in layers, not as one date

Two timelines must be kept apart. The Crowd Supply update describes a campaign: up to 3,500 Dabao boards, starting at USD 9.50, with an estimated June delivery subject to die-yield assumptions. The Baochip site gives the longer view: mass production begins in the second quarter of 2026, roughly 20 weeks are needed from blank wafers to reeled chips, and general availability is scheduled for the fourth quarter of 2026. The first is a campaign delivery estimate; the second is a company-level availability plan. They are not the same milestone. Averaging them, or collapsing both into a vague 2026, loses the distinction. Both should be written down with their sources and conditions, with the baseline date of 19 November 2026 stated. If yield assumptions fail, the campaign delivery date will move.

Where: Where the verifiable boundary is drawn

The official site is explicit. The chip source code, reference guide for the core complex and peripherals, hardware reference documentation, Xous and the board support package are in public repositories. Physical design layers tied to the TSMC 22nm process remain proprietary. Verifiability therefore sits at the RTL and inspectable-layout layer, not at the process-documentation layer. The delivery path is also public: Crossbar hosts the tape-out and fabricates wafers at TSMC, and Baochip buys packaged, tested chips from Crossbar. Buyers can also purchase the same chips directly from Crossbar, but those do not come with Baochip software preloaded, and application and software support go through Crossbar. The claim to reject is that identical silicon guarantees identical software support. The chip is the same; the delivery contents and the responsible support party are not, and that distinction belongs in the purchase order.

How: Four steps to evaluate this class of chip

First, translate the requirement into checkable items: whether virtual memory is needed, whether on-chip TRNG, AES and hash primitives matter, and whether infrared inspection is genuinely required. Replace wanting open source with concrete capabilities. Second, confirm the software stack can land on Xous, or that the team can port another Rust-based microkernel; the site documents a BIO simulator and loader, so simulation can precede hardware. Third, confirm the supply path: a Dabao board through Crowd Supply, or the direct Crossbar channel, with different preloaded software and support responsibility. Fourth, if audit matters, budget for inspection equipment and interpretation time, and agree in advance what happens if a check disagrees with the design. Skipping the fourth step as optional is hard to defend when inspectability is the reason for buying in the first place.

Boundaries: What this chip does not solve

It does not solve general compute problems or mature software ecosystems. With a 350MHz main core and a 700MHz I/O cluster, it sits in microcontroller territory, not in the range for large models or general-purpose work. The software focus is Xous, not mainstream Linux distributions. It does not replace formal verification either: seeing the transistor layout answers whether the part was made to the published design, not whether the logic is formally correct. The claim to reject is that infrared inspection eliminates backdoors outright. Those are assertions of very different strength; one is an evidence mechanism, the other a conclusion, with interpretation flow and coverage in between. The site also states that the company is one person, self-funded with no investors. That is not a flaw, but support and capacity should be planned on small-team terms.

Conclusion: Its value is one more layer of checkable evidence

In industry terms, the significance of Baochip-1x is not its position on a performance chart. It turns inspectability from a slogan into a packaging design decision. For teams that must demonstrate supply-chain integrity without running a fab, it is a path worth tracking. For readers who simply want a cheap board with a working system, this is not the most convenient moment. A claim worth rejecting is that publishing RTL completes the job of an open chip. Baochip shows that what blocks high-assurance adoption is often not the source code but whether the physical part in hand can be checked. That judgement does not depend on shipment volume or on whether a large vendor follows.

The Crowd Supply update states a 350MHz Vexriscv CPU with MMU, a quad-core 700MHz PicoRV BIO coprocessor, 4MiB RRAM and 2MiB RAM, TRNG, AES and hash units, IRIS-ready packaging, a limit of 3,500 boards, a starting price of USD 9.50 and an estimated June delivery subject to yield assumptions. Tier 2, company technical blog: bunnie on supply-chain trust and engineering sample screening. Frequently asked questions

What exactly does IRIS inspection prove?

It answers whether the transistor pattern on a specific die matches the published design. It is a non-destructive physical check, provided the package supports it and the reader has the equipment and skill. It does not prove logical correctness and does not replace formal verification.

Why do the official sources say mostly-open rather than fully open?

The compute RTL, bootloader and software stack are public and NDA-free, but the physical design layers tied to the TSMC 22nm process remain proprietary. Calling the chip fully open would overstate what can actually be audited, which matters in assurance-driven settings.

Is it ready for a production project now?

It depends on the goal. Teams that need inspectable hardware for research or high-assurance work can start with the Dabao board. Production lines that need a mature toolchain, long-term availability and ready-made Linux support should watch the software stack and supply cadence first rather than schedule against a general-purpose microcontroller.
